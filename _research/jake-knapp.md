## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

### The premise

Sprint compresses months of debate, design, prototyping, and testing into five days. The output is not a product — it's validated learning about whether a direction is right.

> "The sprint gives our startups a superpower: they can fast-forward into the future to see their finished product and customer reactions, before making any expensive commitments."
> — *Sprint*, Introduction.

### The 5-day structure

**Monday — Map and Target.** Set a long-term goal. Write Sprint Questions (the risks and unknowns). Draw a Map of the customer journey. Ask the Experts (2-4 domain experts give 15-30 min talks). Write How Might We notes during expert talks. Organize and dot-vote on HMW notes. The Decider picks one target customer and one target moment on the Map. Output: Map with circled target, Sprint Questions on the wall.

**Tuesday — Sketch.** Lightning Demos (3-min tours of inspiring solutions from any industry). Then the 4-step individual sketch process: Notes (20 min), Ideas (20 min), Crazy 8s (8 sketches in 8 min), Solution Sketch (30-90 min, detailed, anonymous, self-explanatory). No group brainstorming. Work alone. Output: one Solution Sketch per person, anonymous, taped face-down.

**Wednesday — Decide.** Art Museum (silent review of all sketches on the wall). Heat Map (dot stickers on interesting parts). Speed Critique (3-min structured discussion per sketch — narrator describes, team calls out ideas, author stays silent). Straw Poll (one vote per person, non-binding). Supervote (the Decider gets 3 votes, binding). Rumble or All-in-One decision. Then: Storyboard (10-15 frames on whiteboard, step-by-step prototype plan). Output: a Storyboard that guides Thursday's build.

**Thursday — Prototype.** Build a realistic facade — "Goldilocks quality" — looks real, isn't real. Preferred tools: Keynote/Google Slides, Figma. Team roles: Maker(s), Stitcher, Writer, Asset Collector, Interviewer (prepares Friday's script, does not build). Trial run at 3-4 PM. Output: clickable prototype and tested interview script.

**Friday — Test.** 5 customers, staggered 60-min slots. 5-Act Interview: Act 1 (friendly welcome, 5 min), Act 2 (context questions, 10 min), Act 3 (introduction to prototype, 3 min), Act 4 (tasks and observation, 30 min), Act 5 (debrief, 5 min). Rest of team watches via live video feed, takes notes on color-coded sticky notes (green/red/yellow). After all 5: find patterns (3+ customers = strong pattern), answer Sprint Questions. Output: patterns documented, Sprint Questions answered, next-step decision.

### Key roles

**The Decider** — person with real authority (CEO, VP, founder). Picks the target (Monday), casts the Supervote (Wednesday), breaks ties. The single most important role. Without a Decider, the sprint stalls on Wednesday.

**The Facilitator** — runs exercises, manages time, keeps the group on track. Not necessarily the most senior person. Must be separate from the Decider to prevent bias.

**Ideal team:** 7 or fewer people. Cross-functional: finance, marketing, customer, tech/logistics, design experts. Plus the Decider and Facilitator.

### Key mechanisms

**Note-and-Vote** — silent decision-making at every stage. Write individually, vote with dots, discuss only after. Prevents the loudest voice from anchoring.

**Sprint Questions** — "What questions do we want to answer this week?" Turns anxiety into testable hypotheses. Specific, testable, important. Frames the entire sprint.

**How Might We** — reframing tool. Problem heard → HMW note → dot-voted → attached to the Map.

**Crazy 8s** — 8 variations in 8 minutes. Forces divergent thinking past the first (obvious) idea.

**Goldilocks quality** — prototype fidelity target. Real enough for honest reactions, rough enough to build in a day and throw away.

**"5 is enough"** — based on Jakob Nielsen's research. 5 users surface ~85% of usability patterns.

### Logistics

- Dedicated room, full week, 10 AM - 5 PM
- No devices (phones and laptops outside the room)
- Two whiteboards minimum, sticky notes, dot stickers, Sharpies, Time Timer
- Snacks and lunch in the room

## 3. VOICE & TONE

**Register:** Practical, friendly, story-driven, deliberately un-academic. Teaches through concrete stories from real sprints. Anti-jargon — avoids "ideation," "diverge/converge," "empathize" in favor of plain language ("sketch," "come up with ideas," "talk to customers"). Writes like a smart friend explaining a recipe, not a professor defending a framework.

**Rhetorical moves:**
- Story first, principle second. Nearly every teaching point opens with a sprint case (Blue Bottle, Savioke, Slack).
- "We tried X and it didn't work, so we tried Y." Positions the method as iteratively discovered, not theoretically derived.
- Counterintuitive insights stated plainly. "Working alone produces better ideas than brainstorming." No dramatic buildup.
- Humor through self-deprecation and absurdity, never snarky.
- "Try it once exactly as written, then adapt." Trust-the-recipe for first-timers.
- The "magic" framing — describes the moment of unexpected insight during customer testing as "magic."
- Numbered lists and step-by-step instructions throughout. The book is a recipe, not a manifesto.

**How he disagrees:** Gentle but firm. When disagreeing with brainstorming, cites research (Charlan Nemeth), describes what he observed, offers the alternative. No enemies, no straw men. Direct but warm.

**Words he avoids:** "Ideation," "diverge/converge," "empathize," "stakeholder alignment," "design thinking" (as vocabulary), "methodology," "framework."

**How he differs from adjacent voices:**
- vs. IDEO / Tim Brown: more prescriptive, more time-boxed, shares philosophical roots but rejects openness
- vs. Eric Ries: complementary (Ries = business model, Knapp = solution design), mutual fans
- vs. Ryan Singer: completely different problems (5-day test vs. 6-week ship), different registers (warm/story vs. dry/systemic)
- vs. Teresa Torres: different cadences (one-time intervention vs. ongoing weekly discovery)

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO'S / DON'TS / GOTCHAS

**Do's:**
- Get a real Decider in the room (CEO, VP, founder — real authority)
- Ban devices during sprint hours (10-5)
- Keep team at 7 or fewer
- Write real words in the prototype (not lorem ipsum)
- Start the Storyboard with an opening scene (how customer discovers the product)
- Recruit Friday's customers the week before
- Do a trial run Thursday afternoon
- Take notes on individual color-coded sticky notes during Friday tests

**Don'ts:**
- Don't brainstorm in groups — use individual sketching + structured evaluation
- Don't build a real product on Thursday — build a facade
- Don't skip Friday — testing is the point
- Don't test with fewer than 5 customers
- Don't let the Interviewer guide/correct the customer during tests
- Don't run a sprint without Sprint Questions
- Don't compromise on Wednesday — the Supervote exists to prevent Frankenprototypes

**Anti-patterns:**
- "The sprint without a question" — no Sprint Questions written, Friday produces impressions but no answers
- "The polite sprint" — everyone agrees, Supervote is ceremonial, prototype tests the safe idea
- "The Facilitator-Decider collapse" — Decider runs the exercises, biasing the process
- "The tourist sprint" — team members attend selectively, weakening every day they miss
- "The feature-list prototype" — prototype shows 15 features instead of one end-to-end flow
- "The post-sprint shrug" — sprint ends, team goes back to business as usual, nothing changes

## 5. REAL CASES

**From the book (GV sprints):**
- **Blue Bottle Coffee** — online store experience. Tested story-led vs. product-grid approach. Story-led won.
- **Savioke** — hotel delivery robot personality. Tested different robot behaviors with real hotel guests. Discovered specific behaviors that felt friendly vs. creepy.
- **Slack** — explaining Slack to non-users. Tested different explanations and onboarding approaches. "Show, don't tell" won.
- **Flatiron Health** — clinical trial matching for cancer patients. Tested whether oncologists would use an in-workflow matching tool. Only viable if integrated into existing medical record system.
- **Foundation Medicine** — genomic report redesign. Lead with actionable treatment options, not raw data.

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**Design Thinking (IDEO / Stanford d.school):**
Sprint is an opinionated distillation of design thinking. Shared philosophical roots (human-centered design, prototyping, testing); completely different execution (open-ended vs. 5-day recipe). Sprint was born from Knapp's frustration with design thinking's lack of structure. They compose: use design thinking for broad exploration, Sprint to test the leading candidate.

**Lean Startup (Eric Ries):**
Different altitudes. Lean Startup = business model validation (pivot or persevere). Sprint = solution validation (does this work for customers?). They compose well: use Lean Startup's hypothesis framing, Sprint to test the riskiest assumption. Ries endorsed the Sprint book. Mutual respect.

**Shape Up (Ryan Singer):**
Different problems entirely. Sprint = answer a question in 5 days. Shape Up = ship features in 6-week cycles. Sprint is a diagnostic tool; Shape Up is an operating system. They compose: Sprint to validate a major bet before committing a Shape Up cycle. No competition — different altitudes and timescales.

**Scrum:**
Naming collision is unfortunate. A Knapp Sprint is a 5-day design-and-test intervention. A Scrum sprint is a 2-week development cycle. Completely different tools. Do not confuse or blend.

**Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres):**
Different cadences. Torres = ongoing weekly customer contact. Sprint = intensive one-week intervention. They compose: Sprint to test a big bet, Torres for ongoing smaller iterations.

**Jobs to be Done (JTBD):**
Complementary, not competing. JTBD is a theory of why customers hire products; Sprint is a process for testing whether a specific solution works. JTBD can inform Sprint Questions and customer recruitment criteria.

**Foundation Sprint (Knapp 2025, from *Click*):**
Same author, different tools. Foundation Sprint = "Is this idea differentiated enough?" (upstream). Design Sprint = "Does this solution work?" (downstream). Most startups need the Foundation Sprint first.
