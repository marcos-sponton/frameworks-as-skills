## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL — THE FIVE DYSFUNCTIONS PYRAMID

**Book:** *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team: A Leadership Fable* — Jossey-Bass, 2002. 20th Anniversary Edition 2022. Written as a fable: Kathryn Petersen, new CEO at fictional DecisionTech, inherits a dysfunctional executive team and walks them through the model over an off-site. Theory section at the back (~40 pages).

### The pyramid (bottom to top — foundation first)

The 5 dysfunctions, in the order they compound. The Table Group's canonical current-language definitions (verbatim from tablegroup.com):

1. **Absence of Trust** *(foundation)* — "The fear of being vulnerable prevents team members from building trust with each other."
2. **Fear of Conflict** — "The desire to preserve artificial harmony stifles productive ideological conflict."
3. **Lack of Commitment** — "The lack of clarity and/or buy-in prevents team members from making decisions they stick to."
4. **Avoidance of Accountability** — "The need to avoid interpersonal discomfort prevents team members from holding each other accountable."
5. **Inattention to Results** *(top)* — "The pursuit of individual goals and personal status erodes the team's focus on collective success."

### The pyramid inverted — the healthy team

Each dysfunction, when overcome, enables the next:
1. **Trust one another** (vulnerability-based).
2. **Engage in unfiltered conflict around ideas.**
3. **Commit to decisions and plans of action.**
4. **Hold one another accountable** for delivering against those plans.
5. **Focus on the achievement of collective results.**

### Load-bearing distinctions inside the model

**Trust: vulnerability-based, NOT predictive.**
- *Predictive trust* = confidence a colleague will behave as expected (won't break rules, will meet deadlines). Common baseline.
- *Vulnerability-based trust* = willingness to be completely exposed with one another — admit mistakes, ask for help, share weaknesses, say "I don't know," without fear of retaliation, humiliation, or resentment.
- **Only vulnerability-based trust unlocks the next layer.** Predictive trust doesn't. Teams can look civil (predictive trust) and still be stuck at the Absence of Trust dysfunction because vulnerability isn't happening.

**Conflict: productive ideological, NOT interpersonal.**
- The productive conflict is *around ideas* — disagreement, debate, challenging assumptions. Emotional, sometimes heated, but about the work.
- The unproductive conflict is *personal* — attacks on character, factions, grudges.
- **Fake harmony (artificial harmony) is the tell for Fear of Conflict.** Meetings where nobody disagrees look great and are quietly killing the team.

**Commitment: buy-in without consensus.**
- The bar is NOT consensus. Consensus-seeking is one of the ways teams stall.
- The bar is *"I disagree but I'll commit."* Everyone gets heard (which requires the Conflict layer), a decision gets made, and the team leaves the room aligned — even the people who lost the argument.
- **Ambiguity kills commitment.** If people leave the room unclear on what was decided, commitment is gone.

**Accountability: peer-to-peer, NOT top-down.**
- The most common misapplication: managers assume accountability is their job (top-down enforcement).
- Lencioni's insistence: **peer-to-peer accountability is stronger than top-down.** The peer whose deliverable depends on yours can hold you accountable in a way the manager can't.
- Requires the Commitment layer — you can't hold peers accountable to a plan they never committed to.

**Results: collective, NOT individual.**
- The dysfunction is prioritizing *individual status* (ego, career advancement, department metrics) over collective team outcomes.
- Only teams that made it through the first four dysfunctions can hold this — every prior layer feeds it. A team at Absence of Trust cannot focus on collective results even if the CEO says so.

### The team assessment

Lencioni's team assessment (available at [tablegroup.com/product/online-team-assessment](https://www.tablegroup.com/product/online-team-assessment/)) — Likert-scale instrument. Each team member scores the team on statements mapped to each dysfunction. Results plot the team on the pyramid — you see which layer is broken. Also a paper version in the back of the book. The assessment is diagnostic, not prescriptive — you still have to do the work to overcome the dysfunction.

## 3. POST-BOOK MATERIAL — THE 24-YEAR ARC (2002 → 2026)

Lencioni has published a book roughly every 2–3 years since 1998. The Five Dysfunctions is his most famous, but treating it as canon misses ~22 years of refinement, extension, and adjacent frameworks. The system is bigger than the pyramid.

### The Advantage (2012) — the consolidation

*The Advantage* is the closest thing to a Lencioni "grand unified theory." The Four Disciplines:

1. **Build a cohesive leadership team** — the Five Dysfunctions applied to the executive team. **This is where the pyramid lives inside the larger system.**
2. **Create clarity** — leadership team answers the **Six Critical Questions** together and out of a shared understanding:
   1. Why do we exist?
   2. How do we behave?
   3. What do we do?
   4. How will we succeed?
   5. What is most important, right now?
   6. Who must do what?
3. **Overcommunicate clarity** — repeat the answers relentlessly. Lencioni's rule of thumb: any leader who thinks they're overcommunicating is probably just starting to communicate.
4. **Reinforce clarity** — hard-wire the answers into hiring, performance management, compensation, recognition, promotion. If HR processes don't reflect the answers, the answers aren't real.

**Key argument:** "The single, greatest advantage any company can achieve is organizational health." — *The Advantage* (repeated verbatim across HBR piece and podcast).

**Smart vs. healthy:** organizations obsess over "smart" (strategy, tech, marketing, finance) because it's what MBA programs teach. But "healthy" (integrity, cohesion, clarity, minimal politics) is where the durable edge is — and it's underinvested in because it feels touchy-feely.

### The Ideal Team Player (2016) — the hiring layer

Three virtues, all three required:

- **Humble** — quick to point out others' contributions, slow to seek attention. Emphasizes team over self, defines success collectively.
- **Hungry** — self-motivated, diligent. Rarely has to be pushed. Always thinking about next step, next opportunity.
- **Smart** — interpersonally smart. Common sense about people. Says the right thing at the right time; doesn't step on toes. **Not IQ; not technical smart.**

**The diagnostic power** is in the missing virtue:
- Humble + Hungry, not Smart → **the Accidental Mess-Maker.** Works hard, wants to help, but keeps blowing up relationships. Coachable.
- Humble + Smart, not Hungry → **the Loveable Slacker.** Easy to work with, does just enough. Rarely a fireable offense; slowly eats team velocity.
- Hungry + Smart, not Humble → **the Skillful Politician.** The most dangerous type. High performer, popular with leadership, poisonous to peers. Lencioni: hardest to spot and hardest to remove.

**Rule:** a team member has to have all three at meaningful level to be a net-positive contributor. Missing one — especially Humble — is disqualifying at hiring.

Assessment tool: [tablegroup.com/idealteamplayer/](https://www.tablegroup.com/idealteamplayer/).

### The Motive (2020) — the leader's why

Lencioni's response to years of watching leaders who technically understood the frameworks still fail. The failure wasn't skill — it was *motive*.

- **Reward-centered leadership** — leadership is the payoff for hard work. Should be enjoyable, high-status, chosen work, freedom from mundane duties. Avoids what's uncomfortable.
- **Responsibility-centered leadership** — leadership is a duty. Should be difficult, uncomfortable, in service of others. Personal gratification is a byproduct, not the point.

**Five things reward-centered leaders systematically neglect:**
1. Developing the leadership team.
2. Managing subordinates (real 1:1s, not status updates).
3. Having difficult conversations.
4. Running great meetings.
5. Communicating constantly and repetitively.

**Diagnostic power:** if a leader is complaining about how hard these five things are, they're likely reward-centered. Responsibility-centered leaders expect the difficulty.

### The 6 Types of Working Genius (2022) — the newest product

WIDGET framework, launched via workinggenius.com. Six geniuses grouped into three stages of any work project:

**Ideation:**
- **Wonder (W)** — ponder, ask "what if?" and "why?" questions; sense that something could be different / better.
- **Invention (I)** — create original, novel ideas and solutions from scratch or from Wonder's questions.

**Activation:**
- **Discernment (D)** — assess merit and workability; intuitive judgment on what works and what doesn't.
- **Galvanizing (G)** — inspire, encourage, mobilize; get people moving on an idea.

**Implementation:**
- **Enablement (E)** — provide support; help others get an initiative off the ground.
- **Tenacity (T)** — push to meet ambitious goals; blow through obstacles to finish.

**The rule of 2-2-2:** every person has **2 geniuses** (energizing), **2 competencies** (neutral — can do them, don't love them), and **2 frustrations** (draining). Nobody is genius at all six.

**Why it connects to team health:** when someone is in a role dominated by their frustrations, they burn out AND the team suffers. When a team lacks a genius in a critical stage, projects die there. Common failure pattern: heavy on Invention + Galvanizing, light on Discernment + Tenacity → lots of exciting starts, nothing finishes.

**Not a replacement for Five Dysfunctions.** Adjacent instrument. Five Dysfunctions is about *how the team works together*; Working Genius is about *what work each person is suited for* and where the team has genius gaps.

Assessment: $25 individual, [workinggenius.com](https://www.workinggenius.com). Facilitator certification for practitioners.

### What's stable across the arc

- **Vulnerability-based trust** as the foundation of team performance — from 1998 through 2026, Lencioni's most consistent claim. Never softened.
- **Fable as pedagogy** — most books use the fable format (theory in an appendix). This is a deliberate choice, not a marketing gimmick. Lencioni argues stories install frameworks in a way lectures don't.
- **Team as a group of people who share responsibility for collective outcomes** — Lencioni's definition of team, unchanged. This is why he pushes back on "senior leadership team" that's actually a group of independent department heads.
- **Organizational health > smart** — the through-line from *Four Obsessions* (2000) through *The Advantage* (2012) and beyond.

### What's evolved

- **Ideal Team Player (2016)** added the hiring lens Five Dysfunctions didn't have. The pyramid tells you what a functional team does; Ideal Team Player tells you who to put on it.
- **The Motive (2020)** shifted focus from team behaviors to leader psychology — Lencioni's admission that even the best framework fails without the right leader posture.
- **Working Genius (2022)** added an individual-productivity + team-genius-gap lens that Five Dysfunctions never had.
- **Language refinements:** "vulnerability-based trust" (2002) still holds; "productive ideological conflict" clarified over time; "peer-to-peer accountability" gets more emphasis in later work than it did in the 2002 book.

## 4. VOICE & TONE

**Register:** Warm, folksy, deliberately non-academic. Lencioni writes fables because he believes leaders read novels but not white papers. Podcast is conversational — Pat and Cody riffing on a leadership question with client stories, in normal spoken English, not consulting jargon. Not Rumelt's provocateur register. Not Helmer's academic-precise register. Not Kim Scott's confession-first register (though there is some overlap — Lencioni does share his own consulting failures). Closer to a **experienced pastor or veteran coach** — moral clarity, patient, direct without being harsh, uses examples over analysis.

**Rhetorical moves:**

- **The fable-first pedagogy.** Every framework arrives wrapped in a story. Kathryn at DecisionTech. Bruce Wells at Peninsula. Rich O'Connor. Charlie at Yip Software. Lencioni argues you learn how the framework *feels* from the fable, then the theory section formalizes what you already saw.
- **Simple language for hard truths.** "Fear of Conflict" not "psychological safety failure modes." "Absence of Trust" not "epistemic vulnerability deficit." The naming is deliberately blunt.
- **Repetition as reinforcement.** Lencioni will circle back to the same phrase (vulnerability-based trust, artificial harmony, disagree and commit) many times. His rule from *The Motive*: leaders think they're overcommunicating when they're just starting.
- **Client anecdotes as the didactic engine.** In podcast especially — "we were working with a CEO who…" then the story, then the framework name applied to it. Applied, not abstract.
- **Moral clarity in short lines.** "Nobody wins if the team loses." "You can't reward the wrong behavior." "If you're not uncomfortable in a leadership team meeting, you're doing something wrong."
- **Warm push-back.** Lencioni disagrees firmly but rarely angrily — he'll say "I don't buy that" and then explain why. In podcast with Cody, disagreement is modeled explicitly.

## 5. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**The pyramid is sequential.** You cannot fix Accountability if Trust is broken. Teams that jump to accountability techniques (peer review software, scorecards, PIPs) while sitting at Absence of Trust are doing performative accountability. Diagnose the *lowest broken layer* and fix that first.

**Vulnerability-based trust is easy to fake.** The classic fake: the ropes-course off-site where everyone shares a childhood story, cries a little, and calls it trust. If the next Monday's meeting still has zero disagreement, the trust wasn't built — the vulnerability was theatrical. **The test of trust is what happens in the next real work situation, not the off-site.**

**Common misapplications (Lencioni's explicit "gotchas"):**

- **Checklist trust.** Off-site vulnerability exercise + no ongoing behavior change = performative. The trust has to compound in day-to-day work.
- **Fake harmony as "team health."** Teams that never disagree in meetings look great and are quietly stuck at Fear of Conflict. If your leadership team meetings are pleasant and drama-free, you probably have a problem.
- **Consensus-seeking as the wrong bar for commitment.** Teams that wait until everyone agrees stall. The right bar is "disagree and commit."
- **Manager-only accountability.** When the manager is the only person who holds people accountable, the team is stuck at Avoidance of Accountability. Peer-to-peer is the fix — and it can only exist above real Commitment.
- **Team pride confused with team results.** "We're a great team, everyone likes each other, we ship on time" is not the same as "the team achieves the collective results it committed to." Pride without results is the top-of-pyramid failure.
- **"Trust building" as an activity you do once.** Lencioni: trust is a practice, not an event. If it's not happening in every meeting, it's not there.

**The First Team rule.**
Leaders default to treating their direct reports as their primary team ("first team"). Lencioni's insistence: your **first team** is your peers — the other department heads you report alongside. If a VP of Sales prioritizes sales-team wins over cross-department cohesion, they're breaking the executive team's ability to hold the pyramid. The first team is where collective results are set.

**Do: mine for conflict.**
If your team never disagrees, don't celebrate — dig. The leader's job is to actively surface disagreement that people won't volunteer. Direct question in the meeting: "Sarah, you don't look convinced. What's your objection?"

**Do: end every meeting with a summary.**
Not "great meeting everyone" — a specific enumeration of *what we decided, who owns what, and by when*. Prevents the ambiguity that kills commitment.

**Do: hire for humble + hungry + smart, in that order of criticality.**
Missing Humble is disqualifying. Missing Hungry or Smart is coachable but hard. All three at a meaningful level = ideal team player.

**Do: peer accountability rituals.**
Structured mechanisms where peers evaluate peers on team commitments (not individual metrics). The scorecard is the artifact; the conversation is where accountability actually happens.

**Do: use the team assessment.**
The Likert-scale instrument (in the book's appendix and at [tablegroup.com](https://www.tablegroup.com/product/online-team-assessment/)) — individually scored, then aggregated and discussed. The discussion of the results is often where the work starts. Don't just distribute the assessment.

**Don't: promote skillful politicians.**
Hungry + Smart without Humble = the most dangerous type. High individual performance masks toxicity. Every promotion of a skillful politician tells the org that Humble is optional.

**Don't: treat "team" as any group of people who report to the same manager.**
Lencioni's definition: a team is a group of people who share responsibility for collective outcomes. A "senior leadership team" where each member owns their department's numbers and doesn't share collective responsibility isn't a team — it's a group. The dysfunctions don't apply the same way to groups.

**Don't: use the fable as the only artifact.**
Reading the fable and skipping the theory / assessment / field guide is common and produces surface-level understanding. The pyramid installs when you do the work — assessment, discussion, disciplined practice — not when you finish the book.

**Don't: skip the field guide.**
*Overcoming the Five Dysfunctions* (2005) exists because too many people read the fable and asked "now what?" The field guide has the exercises and scripts. Use it.

**Don't: apply the framework to teams that don't share collective responsibility.**
See above. The pyramid presumes interdependence. If members don't need each other to succeed, forcing the model creates artificial dysfunction diagnoses.

**Reward-centered vs. responsibility-centered — the leader diagnostic.**
Before applying the model to a team, check the leader. A reward-centered CEO won't fix Absence of Trust because they won't be vulnerable themselves. Diagnose the leader first, then the team.

**The 5 dysfunctions vs. Working Genius — which to reach for.**
- If the team is dysfunctional in *how it works together* (trust, conflict, commitment, accountability, results) → **Five Dysfunctions**.
- If the team is dysfunctional in *what kinds of work it can execute* (ideas die, projects don't finish, no one activates) → **Working Genius** (the team is missing genius in a stage).
- Both instruments can apply to the same team; they diagnose different failure modes.

## 7. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**vs. Amy Edmondson — The Fearless Organization / psychological safety:**
- **Edmondson:** psychological safety = belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes. Research-based, empirical, hospital-team origins.
- **Lencioni:** vulnerability-based trust = willingness to be exposed with one another; admit weakness, ask for help. Framework-based, practitioner-derived.
- **Same substrate, different vocabulary.** Edmondson's psychological safety and Lencioni's vulnerability-based trust are near-identical concepts named for different audiences (Edmondson: academic + research; Lencioni: practitioner). Both are the load-bearing foundation for team performance.
- **Where they differ:** Edmondson stays closer to the research (Team Learning Behaviors, the four-stage model of psychological safety); Lencioni stacks it into the pyramid and links it to specific downstream behaviors (conflict, commitment, accountability, results). Edmondson is a deeper theory of the foundation; Lencioni is a broader theory of what the foundation enables.
- **Compose:** Edmondson's research legitimizes the foundation Lencioni built the pyramid on. Cite Edmondson when the user needs the empirical proof; use Lencioni's pyramid to give the operational sequence.

**vs. Kim Scott — Radical Candor:**
- **Kim:** Care Personally + Challenge Directly. Focus on manager-report feedback dynamics.
- **Lencioni:** Trust → Conflict → Commitment → Accountability → Results. Focus on team-level dynamics, especially leadership teams.
- **Overlap on productive conflict.** Kim's "Challenge Directly without personalizing" is Lencioni's "productive ideological conflict, not personal." Kim's Ruinous Empathy is one form of Lencioni's Artificial Harmony.
- **Where they differ:** Kim is dyadic (manager + report); Lencioni is team-level. Kim's mechanism is a specific piece of feedback; Lencioni's is a team's disposition. Both are needed — the manager who runs Radical Candor 1:1 but leads a team stuck at Fear of Conflict has a coherence problem.
- **Compose:** Radical Candor is how individual managers give feedback inside a team the Five Dysfunctions is trying to build. Same operating stack, different layers.

**vs. Roger Martin — Playing to Win:**
- **Martin:** strategy cascade (aspiration → where to play → how to win → capabilities → management systems).
- **Lencioni:** organizational health, not strategy. The Advantage's argument is that smart (which Martin's cascade is) is table stakes; healthy is the durable advantage.
- **Not competing.** Different problem entirely. Martin answers *what to do*; Lencioni answers *how the team that has to execute it works together*.
- **Compose:** a company with a Playing to Win strategy and a Five Dysfunctions leadership team is more likely to execute than one with the strategy alone.

**vs. Christina Wodtke — Radical Focus / OKRs:**
- **Wodtke:** OKRs + the Team Health Monitor (Wodtke's own instrument, not Lencioni's). The Team Health Monitor is spiritually adjacent to Lencioni's team assessment.
- **Where they differ:** Wodtke is more operational (OKR cadence, weekly meeting rhythm); Lencioni is more foundational (team dysfunctions to fix before OKRs work).
- **Compose:** the team that runs OKRs from Wodtke's rituals needs Lencioni's pyramid to have Trust → Conflict → Commitment for the OKRs to stick. OKRs on top of Absence of Trust produce theater.

**vs. Andy Grove — High Output Management:**
- **Grove:** managerial output = output of your team + output of teams you influence. Mechanisms (1:1s, task-relevant maturity, meeting types).
- **Lencioni:** what has to be true about the team for those mechanisms to work. Grove's 1:1 requires vulnerability-based trust to be a real feedback conversation.
- **Compose:** Grove is the plumbing; Lencioni is the water quality.

**vs. Claire Hughes Johnson — Scaling People:**
- **CHJ:** operational manager craft, from Stripe / Google. Cites Lencioni explicitly, especially Five Dysfunctions and Ideal Team Player.
- **Composition is native:** Scaling People sits on top of Lencioni's frameworks and adds the operational detail (hiring rubrics, feedback templates, org-design patterns).

**vs. Brené Brown — Dare to Lead:**
- **Brown:** vulnerability as leadership discipline, "rumbling with vulnerability" in hard conversations.
- **Lencioni:** vulnerability-based trust as team-level substrate.
- **Same substrate, different scope.** Brown is deeper on the individual leader's inner work; Lencioni is broader on team dynamics that vulnerability enables.
- **Compose:** Brown gives the leader the inner work; Lencioni gives the team the operating sequence.

**Lencioni's own extensions / adjacencies (his own work as a system):**
- **Five Dysfunctions (2002)** = team behaviors.
- **The Advantage (2012)** = organizational health system that includes Five Dysfunctions as one of four disciplines.
- **Ideal Team Player (2016)** = who to put on the team.
- **The Motive (2020)** = what the leader has to be for the team to work.
- **Working Genius (2022)** = what kind of work each team member is suited for + where the team has genius gaps.

These are not five separate frameworks. They are one operating system that grew over 20 years, and users are best served treating them that way.

**Operational notes for the skill builder:**

1. **Post-book material is dominant.** Treating 2002 as canon misses 90% of the practitioner's system. The skill's `post-book.md` has to actively pull from *The Advantage*, *Ideal Team Player*, *The Motive*, *Working Genius*, and the podcast.
2. **Vulnerability-based trust is faked constantly.** The skill's `heuristics.md` needs an explicit guard against theatrical trust exercises passing as trust-building.
3. **Fable-first pedagogy applies to the skill too.** When teaching the pyramid, start with a Kathryn-at-DecisionTech-shaped example, not the taxonomy. Voice-and-tone.md must capture this.
4. **The pyramid is sequential — this is the differential.** Base-model knowledge of the framework often lists 5 dysfunctions in parallel. The skill's method.md must make the compounding structure explicit.
