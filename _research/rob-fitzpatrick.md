## 2. Method in Detail

### Core Framework: The Three Rules

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you pitch, you trigger the "pathos problem" — people protect your feelings instead of telling you the truth.
2. **Ask about specifics in the past, not generics about the future.** People can't predict their own future behavior. They can describe what they actually did.
3. **Talk less, listen more.** If you're talking more than 20%, you're pitching.

### Bad Data Taxonomy

Three types of data that corrupt your learning:
- **Compliments:** "That's so cool!" / "I love it!" — social niceties that feel like validation but aren't. "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless." (*The Mom Test*, ch. 2.)
- **Fluff:** Generics ("I usually..."), future promises ("I would definitely..."), hypothetical maybes ("I might..."). All noise. Anchor in the past: "When's the last time that actually happened?"
- **Ideas:** Feature requests without underlying motivation. The analytics/export example: the customer wanted pretty charts for a presentation, not analytics. Dig beneath: "Why do you want that? What would you do with it?"

### Commitment and Advancement

A meeting produced signal only if it ends with a commitment — time, reputation, or money. Compliments cost nothing. Commitments cost something.
- **Time:** Scheduled follow-up with clear goals, prototype review, trial.
- **Reputation:** Introduction to their boss/peer, public testimonial.
- **Financial:** Letter of intent, pre-order, deposit.

"It's not a real sales lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you." (*The Mom Test*, ch. 5.)

**Advancement** = customer moved to the next step. **Spinning** = pleasant meeting, nothing moved.

### Meeting Framing (Five Elements)

1. Vision (why you're there), 2. Framing (where you are in the process), 3. Weakness (what you need help with), 4. Pedestal (why they're uniquely qualified), 5. Ask (specific, bounded request).

### Segmentation

Before PMF, slice conversations by customer segment. Mixed-segment data cancels out. Look for who has the problem most urgently, who is already spending money/time on it.

### Note-Taking Protocol

Two people (one talks, one takes notes). Exact quotes, not paraphrases. Emotional reactions, pain points, goals, feature requests (marked for deeper investigation), commitments. Debrief with the full team immediately. Meta-review: which questions worked best?

### Post-Book Refinements

- **The bowling-lane approach:** Gentle structure, not rigid scripts. "Put the bumpers on to keep them out of the gutters." (Brian Rhea podcast, 2026.)
- **The graveyard:** Ask what solutions they've tried and abandoned. The reasons they quit are more informative than what they're currently using. (Brian Rhea podcast, 2026.)
- **Riskiest assumption first:** Different businesses fail differently. Focus conversations on what's most likely to kill you. (Brian Rhea podcast, 2026.)
- **Reduce, don't eliminate, uncertainty:** "Entrepreneurship can't be done by robots. You're building a foundation of insight for your visionary leap, not removing the leap itself." (Brian Rhea podcast, 2026.)

## 3. Voice & Tone

### Register

Conversational, practical, anti-academic. Writes and speaks like a friend who's made the mistake you're about to make. Teaches from failure, not authority. The book reads like a conversation — short chapters, concrete examples, no jargon. Self-deprecating about his own mistakes. Allergic to unexamined terms of art. Humble about scope (The Mom Test is the conversational layer, not a complete methodology).

> "I didn't wanna overstate my confidence or competence and trick founders into doing the wrong thing... this is my best guess of how it works." — Indie Hackers AMA.

### Signature Rhetorical Moves

1. **Teaching through failure stories.** He doesn't say "here's the right way." He says "here's what I did wrong." The failure comes first; the principle is extracted from it.
2. **The concrete anti-example.** Bad question first, explanation of why it's bad, then the good question. Always paired: DON'T then DO.
3. **One-liners that stick.** "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning." / "It's not a real sales lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you." / "If they haven't looked for solutions, they're not going to buy yours."
4. **The "I'm not special" move.** Undermines the idea that he has unique genius. Authority comes from the method, not the person. "Make things that become more famous than you."
5. **Medium-agnostic framing.** "Is that a SaaS? A book? A blog post? A tweet?" Don't assume the solution form.
6. **Patient redirect.** Never says "you're wrong." Says "here's what happens when you ask that question" and then offers the alternative.

### Vocabulary He Uses

Bad data, fluff, anchoring, commitment and advancement, spinning, the pathos problem, the graveyard.

### Vocabulary He Avoids

"Validated" (prefers "learned"), "user research" (prefers "customer conversations"), formal methodology jargon. Credits Blank's "customer development" explicitly but makes it concrete immediately.

### How He Disagrees

Goes back to the concrete example. "Here's what typically happens when teams do it that way..." Not argumentative, not dogmatic. Just has a lot of examples of things going wrong.

## 4. Heuristics / Do's / Don'ts / Gotchas

### Do's

- Ask about their life, not your idea.
- Anchor fluff in the past ("When's the last time?").
- Dig beneath feature requests ("Why do you want that?").
- Deflect compliments back to facts.
- Push for commitment at the end of every conversation.
- Know your three most important questions (one should be scary).
- Look for the graveyard of abandoned solutions.
- Use two-person meetings (one talks, one takes notes).
- Debrief with the full team immediately after.

### Don'ts

- Pitch disguised as learning. If you mentioned your product in the first five minutes, you're pitching.
- "Everyone says they'd buy it." Replace with: "Everyone I pitched to said nice things."
- Asking about the future instead of the past. "Would you use..." is worthless.
- Treating opinions as data. Only facts about past behavior survive the Mom Test.
- Fishing-for-compliments questions ("Don't you think this is a problem?").
- Unsegmented conversations. Mixed audiences produce mixed (useless) conclusions.
- Seeking validation instead of information. If every conversation confirms your hypothesis, you're steering.

### Gotchas

- If they haven't looked for solutions, they're not going to buy yours.
- The customer is right about their problem, not about the solution.
- Warm introductions corrupt data (social pressure from the mutual connection).
- Note-taking from memory introduces optimism bias. Use exact quotes.
- Premature closure: the problem exists, but is it painful enough to pay for?
- Idea nihilism: over-applying skepticism to the point of never building anything.

## 6. Relationship to Other Frameworks

### Frameworks Fitzpatrick Acknowledges / Builds On

- **Steve Blank's Customer Development** — The Mom Test is the conversational layer within Customer Development. Fitzpatrick credits Blank as intellectual lineage: "None of the books about sales or Customer Development seemed to help me figure out how to actually do it properly."
- **Eric Ries's Lean Startup** — The Mom Test operates within the "Learn" phase of Build-Measure-Learn. Complementary, not competing. The Mom Test is a tool inside the Lean Startup process.
- **YCombinator** — Fitzpatrick is a YC alum (S07). The YC ethos of "talk to users" is the same principle; The Mom Test is HOW.

### Frameworks That Compose Well

- **Teresa Torres's Continuous Discovery Habits** — Torres provides the cadence (weekly touchpoints, Opportunity Solution Trees). The Mom Test provides the conversational rules for those touchpoints.
- **JTBD (Christensen / Moesta / Kalbach)** — JTBD frames what to learn (jobs, forces, switching). The Mom Test provides rules for the conversations in which you uncover those jobs. You can do JTBD interviews badly; Mom Test rules prevent that.
- **April Dunford's Obviously Awesome** — Mom Test gathers customer-language raw material. Dunford's positioning method synthesizes it.
- **Ryan Singer's Shape Up** — Mom Test rules apply to the "shaping" phase when talking to users/stakeholders.

### Frameworks in Tension

- **Challenger Sale (Dixon)** — Challenger is a selling methodology; Mom Test is a learning methodology. Fitzpatrick separates the two explicitly. In discovery calls, they need sequencing (learn first, sell second), not blending.

### What Fitzpatrick Does NOT Claim

The Mom Test is not a complete product methodology. It doesn't tell you what to build, when to pivot, how to prioritize, or when to ship. It's the conversational tool — one component within larger frameworks. Fitzpatrick is explicit about this boundary.
