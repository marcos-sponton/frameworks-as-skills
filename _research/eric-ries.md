## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

Ries lays out The Lean Startup as **5 principles + a set of operational devices**. The principles are the philosophy; the devices are what teams actually run.

### The 5 Principles (theleanstartup.com/principles)

1. **Entrepreneurs are everywhere.** A "startup" is any human institution creating a new product or service under conditions of extreme uncertainty. Applies inside Fortune 500s, non-profits, government, healthcare — not just garage-stage tech.
2. **Entrepreneurship is management.** A startup is an institution requiring a new kind of management specifically geared to extreme uncertainty. Not a smaller version of a big company; not chaos.
3. **Validated learning.** Startups exist to learn how to build a sustainable business. Learning is validated *scientifically* — running experiments that test each element of the vision.
4. **Innovation accounting.** How you measure progress when the classic KPIs are all zero. Setting up milestones, prioritizing work, and measuring learning in a way that's rigorous but appropriate to zero-revenue conditions.
5. **Build-Measure-Learn.** The core feedback loop. Turn ideas into products (Build); measure how customers respond (Measure); learn whether to pivot or persevere (Learn). Accelerate this loop.

### The Operational Devices

**Build-Measure-Learn (BML) loop** — the diagram everyone knows: **Ideas → Build → Product → Measure → Data → Learn → Ideas.** Ries's own emphasis (that most retellings miss): the loop should be **planned in reverse — Learn first, then Measure, then Build.** *"What do we need to learn?"* determines the metric, which determines the smallest experiment that will produce the data. Building comes last, not first.

**Minimum Viable Product (MVP).** The most-cited and most-misunderstood term Ries ever coined. His actual definition:

> "The minimum viable product is that version of a new product which allows a team to collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort." — *The Lean Startup*, 2011.

Key nuances Ries has emphasized publicly and that most usage ignores:
- **"Least effort" not "least features."** The MVP is not the small first version of the product — it's the *smallest test that produces the learning*. Sometimes there is no code at all (Dropbox = video + landing page).
- **"Validated learning" is the outcome, not "shipped software."** A landing page with 100 signups from paid ads is an MVP. A stripped-down v0.1 that shipped but taught nothing is not.
- **The MVP is disposable.** Its job is to answer a question, not to become the product. Most first MVPs get thrown away.

**Pivot or Persevere.** The regular strategic decision, held at a fixed cadence (Ries recommends every few weeks to few months), to decide whether the current strategy is producing validated learning fast enough to be on track — or whether a structured course correction (pivot) is needed. Runway measured in **number of pivots remaining**, not months of cash.

**Ten types of pivot** (from *The Lean Startup*, chapter 8):
1. **Zoom-in pivot** — a single feature becomes the whole product.
2. **Zoom-out pivot** — the whole product becomes a single feature of something larger.
3. **Customer segment pivot** — same product, different customer.
4. **Customer need pivot** — same customer, different problem.
5. **Platform pivot** — application to platform, or vice versa.
6. **Business architecture pivot** — high margin / low volume ↔ low margin / high volume.
7. **Value capture pivot** — changing the monetization model.
8. **Engine of growth pivot** — switching between viral, sticky, or paid growth engines.
9. **Channel pivot** — new distribution channel.
10. **Technology pivot** — same solution via a different technology.

**Innovation Accounting.** How to measure progress inside a startup where every classical metric (revenue, users, retention) is close to zero. Ries's three-level maturity model:
- **Level 1 — actionable metrics** at the cohort level. Baseline built from the current MVP.
- **Level 2 — tuning the engine.** Iterating features to move the actionable metric.
- **Level 3 — pivot or persevere decision.** Comparing progress to the plan.

Central to Innovation Accounting: **actionable vs. vanity metrics.**
- **Vanity metrics** — totals that go up and to the right regardless of what you do. Pageviews, cumulative signups, cumulative downloads. Feel-good; not decision-useful.
- **Actionable metrics** — per-customer, per-cohort, causal. Ries's four preferred devices in his 2009 Tim Ferriss guest post: split-tests (A/B), per-customer metrics, funnel/cohort analysis, keyword metrics for acquisition source quality.

> "Vanity metrics might make you feel good, but they don't offer clear guidance for what to do." — Ries, 2009 guest post on tim.blog.

**Small Batches.** Borrowed straight from Toyota Production System. Do the smallest possible increment of work end-to-end (design → build → ship → measure), then repeat. Small batches expose defects earlier and reduce cost of pivot.

**Continuous Deployment.** IMVU (Ries's own company) deployed to production ~50 times per day. Ries's argument: continuous deployment is *not* recklessness; it is the discipline that enables Build-Measure-Learn at the speed the method requires. Backed by layered defenses: sandboxes, unit tests, CI, automated deploy, monitoring, alerts.

**Five Whys.** Ries's most direct import from Taiichi Ohno's Toyota Production System. When something goes wrong (bug, outage, missed launch, customer complaint), hold a structured post-mortem and ask "why" five times to move from surface symptom to systemic root cause.

Ries's canonical Five Whys example (from his November 2008 startuplessonslearned.com post):
1. Site was down → CPU spiked to 100%.
2. Why did CPU spike? → Infinite loop in code.
3. Why was that code written? → Developer error.
4. Why wasn't it caught? → No unit test.
5. Why no test? → New hire never trained on TDD.

> "What started as a technical problem actually turned out to be a human and process problem." — Ries, Five Whys post, 2008.

Corrective action must be **proportional at every level of the analysis** — not just fix the loop but also add the test, update onboarding, and (at layer 5) rethink hiring/training. Skipping the layers = repeating the failure.

**Andon Cord.** Also from TPS. Any team member can "pull the cord" to halt a bad release before it causes further damage. Ries: this is culture as much as tooling — the person who stops the line is a hero, not a nuisance. Combined with Five Whys, it turns individual failures into system upgrades.

**Genchi Genbutsu ("go and see").** Ries cites this in *The Lean Startup* as the TPS principle Japanese lean practitioners cite most often as the *most important*. In startup terms: *"get out of the building"* (a phrase Ries credits to Steve Blank / Customer Development). No secondhand data replaces watching a real customer struggle with your product in their own environment.

### The engines of growth

Startups grow through exactly three engines, and generally only one at a time:
- **Sticky engine** — retention-driven; growth = acquisition rate minus churn rate.
- **Viral engine** — coefficient-driven; growth = new users per existing user (viral coefficient > 1).
- **Paid engine** — LTV/CAC-driven; growth = margin per customer × acquisition efficiency.

The engine you pick determines the metric that matters, which determines the actionable metric, which determines the experiment.

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**Meta-heuristic (Ries's most-repeated framing since 2018):** *"Everyone thinks they know Lean Startup. Almost nobody is applying it as I described it."* The heuristics below all exist because the vocabulary got out into the world faster than the discipline behind it.

### The 4 common misconceptions Ries himself flags (Lean Startup Co)

1. **"Lean" means cheap / don't think big.** Wrong. Lean is about learning speed under uncertainty, not budget minimization. Scaling still requires capital; Airbnb raised many rounds and is a canonical Lean Startup case.
2. **Venture capital is unnecessary.** Wrong. Nothing about Lean Startup prescribes bootstrapping — it prescribes learning before pouring capital into the wrong thing.
3. **Lean startups embrace failure ("fail fast").** Wrong, and Ries pushes back hard: *"I hate the idea of 'fail fast.' It's like I'm trying to run a sprint, and you're like, 'OK. Breathe fast.'"* The point is fast *learning*, not fast failure. Failure is a byproduct; learning is the goal.
4. **Established companies gain nothing from Lean.** Wrong. The whole point of *The Startup Way* (2017) is that the method works in Fortune 500s — GE, Toyota (yes, they invented the parts), Intuit — when adapted with executive sponsorship and cultural commitment.

### DO's — how to apply the method the way Ries described

- **Plan the BML loop in reverse — Learn first.** Start from the hypothesis you need to test. Design the smallest experiment (Measure) that produces the data. Then decide what to Build. Most teams start with Build and reverse-engineer a hypothesis to justify it.
- **Redefine "done" as validated learning.** A feature isn't done when it ships; it's done when the team knows whether the hypothesis was confirmed or falsified, and has decided pivot or persevere.
- **Cohort analysis + split tests, always.** Aggregate metrics lie. Split by cohort (weekly or monthly signup groups) and by treatment (A/B) to isolate causation from noise.
- **Regular pivot-or-persevere meeting.** Fixed cadence — every few weeks to few months. Skip it and you drift into serial mini-pivots or into obstinate perseverance past the point of validated learning.
- **Small batches at every scale.** Design in small batches. Ship in small batches. Review in small batches. Batch size is the hidden variable that determines learning speed.
- **Get out of the building (genchi genbutsu).** No dashboard replaces watching a real customer struggle with the product. Aggregate customer research is a supplement, not a substitute.
- **Kill features that fail their hypothesis.** The Options tier is generative. The Learn phase is selective. Being ruthless about killing failed features is what protects team capacity for the working ones.
- **Do Five Whys after every meaningful failure.** Post-mortem, five layers deep, proportional investment at every layer. Not just the technical fix — the training, the process, the hiring.

### DON'T — misapplications Ries has warned about

**MVP as excuse for shipping garbage.** The most-repeated misapplication Ries fights. An MVP is not a v0.1 you're ashamed of; it's the smallest test that produces learning. If the test doesn't require code (Dropbox video, Wizard-of-Oz behind a landing page, concierge service you provide manually), then don't write code. The industry heard "minimum" and "product" and skipped "viable" and "validated learning."

**"Fail fast" as slogan.** See the Ries quote above. The point is learning fast, not failing fast. A culture that celebrates failure without extracting learning is just wasteful.

**Applying Lean Startup where the method doesn't fit.** Ries is more careful about this than his fans. Lean Startup is for **new products or services under conditions of extreme uncertainty**. Optimizing a mature, well-understood business is a different problem (see *The Startup Way* for the enterprise-innovation nuance). Amazon's Working Backwards is explicit on this — they use PR/FAQ instead of MVP when the cost per bet is high enough to justify weeks on a document.

**Vanity metrics dressed up as OKRs.** "Grew MAUs 40%" is a vanity metric unless you can show that specific cohorts converted at improved rates. The number goes up as a function of ad spend or virality of unrelated content; it's not a signal you can act on.

**Build-Measure-Learn as a linear waterfall.** Some teams treat it as: "This quarter we Build. Next quarter we Measure. Q3 we Learn." That's not a loop; that's a Gantt chart with lean vocabulary. The loop is *continuous*, and each turn should be *short* (days to weeks, not quarters).

**Pivot fatigue / serial pivoting.** Every 3 weeks a new pivot because the metric didn't move. Two failure modes: (a) hypothesis wasn't sharp enough to falsify, so any drop in the metric feels like proof; (b) the team is running experiments without a coherent underlying vision, so pivots are random walks. The fix is the vision + the regular pivot-or-persevere cadence, not more pivots.

**Perseverance past the falsification.** The mirror of pivot fatigue. The team keeps building because they love the product, past the point where cohort data has clearly falsified the core hypothesis. Ries calls this "achieved failure" — perfect execution of a plan nobody wanted.

**Applying MVP thinking at Amazon-scale commitments.** Amazon rejects MVP for launches at scale — see Working Backwards. Their argument: at their scale, a broken v0.1 shipped to millions is catastrophic to trust, and the cost of a bad launch dwarfs the cost of weeks on a PR/FAQ. This is a real disagreement; both positions are defensible; the resolution is *cost per bet* — Lean Startup thrives when experiments are cheap, Working Backwards thrives when commitments are expensive.

**Cargo-cult Toyota vocabulary without the discipline.** Andon Cord as a Slack channel that nobody uses. Five Whys as a template that produces surface answers. Genchi genbutsu as a slogan not backed by actual customer visits. The vocabulary without the practice is worse than not adopting the vocabulary — it inoculates the team against the real thing.

### Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

**The "actionable" metric that's actually a vanity metric in disguise.** "Weekly active users" sounds actionable. It's a lagging aggregate that moves for many reasons. To be actionable it must be cohorted, causally-linked, and per-customer or per-cohort. Substitution test: if the metric moves and you can't say which experiment caused the change, it's still vanity.

**The pivot that's actually 5 pivots stacked into one.** "Same product, different customer, different channel, different pricing model, different tech stack." That's not a customer-segment pivot; it's a new company. Real pivots are structural changes on **one axis at a time**. Otherwise you can't tell which change caused the result.

**The MVP that's actually a full product.** If the team spent months on the MVP, it's not an MVP. Ries's bar: what's the smallest thing that answers the question? If the answer is "a landing page" or "a Zapier prototype" or "a concierge service we run manually," and the team built a full-featured app instead, they built too much.

**Innovation accounting reduced to a dashboard.** The three levels of Innovation Accounting are not "add three more charts to Looker." They're a maturity model of what you're measuring and why. Adding metrics without the underlying discipline reproduces the vanity-metrics problem at higher volume.

**Applying Lean Startup inside a mature business unit as if it were a startup.** *The Startup Way* is explicit: enterprise applications need executive sponsorship, protected budget, protected team, tolerance for zero-revenue years, and an explicit accountability structure separate from BAU. Without that scaffolding, Lean Startup inside an enterprise is quickly killed by quarterly-earnings pressure. See GE FastWorks case.

## 5. REAL CASES RIES USES PUBLICLY

Primary cases from the 2011 book and Ries's own retrospectives:

- **IMVU** — his own company. The origin proof of continuous deployment (~50 deploys/day), customer development inside a tech startup, and the "wrong first product" pivot (they threw away 40k lines of code implementing an IM add-on when customers wanted standalone 3D chat). Grew to $10M revenue by 2007.
- **Grockit** — GRE test prep. Used as an early case for split-testing and cohort analysis under uncertainty.
- **Wealthfront (formerly kaChing)** — pivoted from a virtual stock-trading game to real wealth management. Canonical customer-need pivot.
- **Aardvark** — social Q&A startup (acquired by Google 2010). Wizard-of-Oz MVP: the founders manually routed early questions to answerers via IM before building automation.
- **Food on the Table** — meal planning startup. Concierge MVP: founders literally showed up at customers' houses with meal plans.
- **Zappos** — Nick Swinmurn photographed shoes at local stores, listed them online, bought and shipped them himself. Zero inventory. The canonical Lean Startup story of a landing-page-first MVP that validated demand before building infrastructure.
- **Dropbox** — the famous video MVP. Drew Houston made a 3-minute explainer video showing how Dropbox would work; posted it; email signups spiked from ~5k to ~75k overnight. **Important nuance:** Ries has publicly pushed back on the popular retelling. Houston built real product too — the video was the demand-validation piece, not "the entire MVP for a year." The story became more monolithic in the retelling than in reality.

Post-book cases (from *The Startup Way*, 2017, applying method to enterprises):

- **GE FastWorks** — Ries's canonical enterprise case. CEO Jeff Immelt sponsored the transformation; Viv Goldstein and Janice Semper co-led with Ries and David Kidder. Trained the top 5,000 leaders. One gas turbine developed 2 years faster and 40% cheaper than GE's traditional process. By 2015 the program influenced 300+ initiatives. Also produced the ethnographic-research-led neonatal incubator for Indian clinics. Later scaled to "FastWorks Everyday" across HR, legal, and finance functions. **What Ries has been honest about:** the transformation partially unwound after Immelt's departure and the broader GE strategic crisis — validating his 2026 thesis that mission-controlled governance is what protects the method from being killed by short-term pressures.
- **Toyota (in-dash electronics system)** — Toyota, ironically, asked Ries to help apply lean startup principles to a new in-car system. Full loop: the framework Toyota gave Ries returned to Toyota.
- **Intuit** — chairman Brad Smith publicly endorsed *The Startup Way*; Intuit uses variants of the method for internal innovation. Ries cites Intuit as one of the enterprise proofs.
- **Pitney Bowes** — one of the four year-long transformation clients Ries names in book publicity.
- **Amazon, Facebook, Airbnb, Twilio** — cited more lightly in *The Startup Way* as tech-native examples of the pattern.

Cases from *Incorruptible* (2026) — the current book, still fresh in the discourse:
- The book's cases are largely mission-controlled organizations (Patagonia, some public benefit corporations, some LTSE-listed companies) contrasted against companies "surgically deboned" post-IPO or post-acquisition. Named cases are dispersed across the book; the podcast interviews from May–July 2026 name several by category if not always by company.

## 6. POST-BOOK MATERIAL (2011 → 2026)

The single richest differential of this skill. Ries has published continuously since 2011; the movement has evolved substantially; the vocabulary has been widely misapplied; and his 2017 and 2026 books explicitly refine the original.

### The Leader's Guide (2016, Kickstarter-only)
A companion to *The Lean Startup* funded via Kickstarter, distributed only to backers. Not commercially reissued. Focuses on how to lead a Lean Startup transformation from an executive perch. Referenced in *The Startup Way*. Rarely cited today but historically the bridge between the 2011 book and the 2017 enterprise book.

### The Startup Way (Currency, 2017)
Full title: *The Startup Way: How Modern Companies Use Entrepreneurial Management to Transform Culture and Drive Long-Term Growth.* Amazon: https://www.amazon.com/Startup-Way-Companies-Entrepreneurial-Management/dp/1101903201.

**What it adds to Lean Startup:**
- **Applies the method to established enterprises** — GE, Toyota, Amazon, Facebook, Airbnb, Twilio, Intuit are all treated as case studies of "modern companies" doing entrepreneurial management.
- **Introduces "Meta Book" / "Startup Way" structure** — a phased playbook (Awareness → Foundation → Scaling → Deep Systems) for spreading the method inside a large company.
- **Names the enterprise-specific obstacles:** quarterly-earnings pressure, misaligned incentive structures, defensive middle management, procurement/finance/legal treating startup teams as anomalies to be normalized. Each obstacle gets a chapter with countermeasures.
- **The "Growth Board"** — Ries's proposed governance body inside the enterprise, modeled on a VC investment committee, to fund internal startups tranche-by-tranche and hold pivot-or-persevere meetings at the corporate level.
- **The "Entrepreneur" as a role in the org chart** — a first-class career path inside enterprises, not a euphemism for "innovation team leader who gets fired in the next reorg."

**What The Startup Way is honest about:** enterprise transformations often unwind. Ries names the conditions (sustained executive sponsorship, protected budget, tolerance for the J-curve, cultural commitment beyond one CEO) required for the method to stick. His 2026 book *Incorruptible* extends this thread structurally.

### Incorruptible (Authors Equity, May 26, 2026)
Full title: *Incorruptible: Why Good Companies Go Bad… and How Great Companies Stay Great.* Amazon: https://www.amazon.com/Incorruptible-Good-Companies-Great-Stay/dp/B0FWZZBPZB. Instant NYT bestseller.

**Central argument:** the failure mode of "companies founded with strong ideals drifting toward short-term thinking, extractive behavior, and mission abandonment" is **structural, not moral**. Well-intentioned people inside those companies can't stop it because the incentive structure and governance design will produce the drift regardless of who's inside.

**Key devices from the book (per publisher description + interviews):**
- **"Financial gravity"** — the accumulated pressure of quarterly earnings, dispersed shareholders, and short-tenured executives that pulls every company toward extraction. Named as a force that must be actively counteracted, not an atmosphere that companies just happen to breathe.
- **"Spiritual holding company"** — Ries's proposed governance blueprint. A parent structure that owns the mission and holds the operating entity accountable to it, insulated from short-term shareholder pressure. Modeled loosely on Patagonia's 2022 structure and on LTSE-listed companies' commitments.
- **"Governance as product design."** Ries's most-quoted 2026 line: *"Governance is not bureaucracy — it is the most important product a founder will ever design."* Governance is not the legal boilerplate you sign at Series A; it is the mechanism that protects the mission when the founder is gone.
- **Data claim:** mission-controlled companies are 6× more likely to survive to their 50th year (Ries's number, per Incorruptible).
- **"Corruption is structural, not moral"** — the load-bearing thesis. Reframes the corporate-scandal narrative away from villain-CEOs toward incentive-and-governance failure.

**Case categories (from press):** startups that "surgically deboned" their founding mission post-IPO; established corporations that resisted the same pull; public institutions retooled for long-term commitment. LTSE listings themselves are one class of case (Twilio, Asana were early listings; LTSE listing is a governance commitment to long-term operating principles).

### LTSE — Long-Term Stock Exchange (2019 SEC approval; 2020 first listings)
Founded by Ries in 2015; SEC-approved 2019; first listings August 2021 (Twilio and Asana dual-listed). https://ltse.com.

**The idea:** a new US stock exchange that requires listed companies to adopt long-term governance commitments — long-term voting power for long-term shareholders, restrictions on short-term-oriented compensation, quarterly reporting reforms, mandatory disclosure of long-term operating strategy.

**Current state (2026):** operating with ~67 employees, $23.2M revenue in 2024, $69.6M valuation in a 2025 round. Ries transitioned CEO role to Bill Harts in 2024; remains Founder & Executive Chairman. LTSE has struggled to attract volume of listings — the operating hypothesis (long-term governance will draw premium companies) is still being validated. Ries treats LTSE as the operator project that *proves* the *Incorruptible* thesis; it is his own long-arc bet on the argument.

### The Eric Ries Show (podcast, 2024–present)
The current single richest live source for Ries's own voice. https://ltse.com/the-eric-ries-show. Long-form conversations with technologists / operators / thinkers on **growth with purpose, long-term company building, mission-controlled organizations.** Bi-weekly-ish cadence. Selected 2024–2026 guests: Reid Hoffman, Dustin Moskovitz (Asana), Sahil Lavingia (Gumroad), James Reinhart (ThredUp), Luis von Ahn (Duolingo), Todd Park (Devoted Health), Craig Newmark (Craigslist), Rodney Brooks (iRobot).

**Recurring themes 2024–2026:**
- Mission drift and how to protect against it.
- The "human flourishing" reframe of what "profit" is for.
- Long-term-oriented governance as a competitive advantage.
- How founders think about succession without destroying the company.
- AI-era company-building — Ries has explored via guests (Reid Hoffman, Answer.AI co-founder Jeremy Howard) but has not published a major standalone AI-and-Lean-Startup thesis. His voice on AI is: applies to AI-native startups the same way as anything else (extreme uncertainty → validated learning → Build-Measure-Learn → Innovation Accounting). AI doesn't change the method; it accelerates the loop.

### Recurring 2024–2026 themes on LinkedIn / Newsletter
- **Corruption as structural.** The load-bearing frame that ties *Lean Startup* → *Startup Way* → *Incorruptible*: the method matters, but the governance around the method determines whether the method survives.
- **"Great companies stay great" as an active discipline.** Ries's argument against the "companies inevitably rot" fatalism. Rot is the default; staying great is a designed outcome.
- **Founder ousting and mission drift** — Sam Altman / OpenAI, Steve Jobs / Apple (as historical), and unnamed contemporary cases used as examples of governance-design failure.

## 7. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**Steve Blank / Customer Development — the direct intellectual parent.** Ries was Blank's student at UC Berkeley (Blank was an IMVU investor and required Ries to audit his class). Customer Development supplied the search-vs-execution distinction, the "get out of the building" mandate, and the four-phase model (Customer Discovery → Validation → Creation → Company Building). Lean Startup is Ries's synthesis of Customer Development + Lean Manufacturing (Toyota Production System) + agile software development, applied specifically to the search phase. **Ries always credits Blank explicitly.**

**Toyota Production System / Taiichi Ohno / Deming — the manufacturing lineage.** Small batches, Andon Cord, Five Whys, Genchi Genbutsu, continuous improvement (kaizen). Ries's translation move: manufacturing figured out how to build a known thing efficiently; startups need the same discipline for figuring out *what* to build under uncertainty. He is meticulous about attribution to Ohno.

**Continuous Discovery Habits — Teresa Torres.** Torres builds *on* Lean Startup rather than against it. Her Opportunity Solution Tree, weekly interview cadence, and assumption-testing loops fit inside Ries's BML loop. Torres updates the *specific tactical practices* for 2020s product teams — Ries stayed at the principle level; Torres wrote the daily-cadence manual. **They compose cleanly.** See `[[continuous-discovery-habits]]` (queued for this repo).

**Inspired — Marty Cagan.** Cagan's *Inspired* is complementary but more product-manager-focused. Cagan's "product discovery" adopts Lean Startup principles (validated learning, MVP as smallest test) with a distinct emphasis on the empowered product team. Where Ries writes for founders / entrepreneurs / enterprise change agents, Cagan writes for PMs. See `[[inspired]]` (queued for this repo).

**Escaping the Build Trap — Melissa Perri.** Perri explicitly credits Ries as a foundational influence. She **redefines MVP as "the minimum amount of effort to learn"** and prefers *"solution experimentation"* as vocabulary to break the industry association of MVP with "small first version to ship." Perri's Product Kata is a Ries-lineage device (via Mike Rother's *Toyota Kata*, itself another TPS translation). See [[escaping-the-build-trap]].

**JTBD (Christensen / Moesta / Kalbach) — complementary demand-side lens.** Ries's method tells you how to test a hypothesis; JTBD tells you what hypothesis is worth testing. A "customer need pivot" (pivot type #4) becomes far more tractable if you have JTBD interviews telling you what jobs the current customers hire the product for. Ries himself rarely uses JTBD vocabulary, but the frames are mutually reinforcing.

**Working Backwards (Amazon / Colin Bryar / Bill Carr) — the honest disagreement.** Amazon *explicitly rejects MVP* for launches at their scale. Their PR/FAQ + narrative memo mechanism is designed to force weeks of thought *before* any code, precisely because Amazon-scale broken launches destroy customer trust. Colin Bryar has publicly critiqued the MVP-industrial-complex ("the MVP process can be useful, but the process itself sometimes becomes the goal"). See [[working-backwards]].

**Reconciling the disagreement (honest read):** both positions are defensible; both authors are correct in their own scope. Lean Startup thrives when **cost per bet is cheap** and **learning speed dominates** (early startup, new feature, new segment, new business model bet). Working Backwards thrives when **cost per bet is expensive** (Amazon Prime, AWS launch, Kindle) and **launch quality dominates**. A team can (and often should) use Lean Startup for the discovery/search phase and Working Backwards for the commitment/launch phase of the same product. The disagreement is real about MVP-in-general; it is smaller once you decompose by phase.

**Design Sprint — Jake Knapp / Google Ventures.** Design Sprint is a compressed 5-day version of the Learn phase — heavy on prototyping and user testing, light on Build. Composable with Lean Startup for pre-code hypothesis testing. Ries and Knapp are complementary, not competitive.

**Not comparable / different scope:**
- **Playing to Win (Martin), Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Rumelt), 7 Powers (Helmer)** — corporate strategy. Above the altitude Lean Startup addresses. Lean Startup presupposes you know what business you're trying to build; strategy frameworks help you decide what to build.
- **Shape Up (Basecamp / Ryan Singer)** — a delivery cadence for shipped products. Not designed for the search / discovery phase Lean Startup targets. Composable for teams that have graduated from Lean Startup into scaled operations.
- **DORA (Accelerate)** — engineering / delivery performance metrics. Ries's continuous deployment lineage overlaps with DORA at the delivery-throughput layer; the frames are complementary but address different questions.
