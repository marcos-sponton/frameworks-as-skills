## 2. Method in detail

### The DHM Model (core)

**Definition:** "Product strategy is a set of hypotheses about how you hope to delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways."

Three dimensions, all required simultaneously:

**D — Delight customers.** Does this product initiative make customers love the product? Measured through engagement and retention metrics. At Netflix: personalization made it easy to find movies you love; the simple interface reduced friction; social features connected you with friends' tastes.

**H — Hard to copy.** Does this initiative build a durable competitive advantage? Biddle identifies four types of hard-to-copy advantages:
1. **Brand** — trust built over years of delivering value. Netflix: 150M+ members trusting with credit cards.
2. **Network effects** — value increases with more users/nodes. Netflix: device ecosystem (Xbox 2008, then every TV/game system/mobile device pre-wired for Netflix).
3. **Economies of scale** — unit economics improve with size. Netflix: amortize content cost across 150M+ members, invest more than smaller rivals in original content.
4. **Unique technology** — proprietary systems. Netflix: personalization technology that knows the tastes of 165M+ members. Took more than a decade to demonstrate it improved retention.

**M — Margin-enhancing.** Does this initiative improve the company's financial position? Not just revenue — margin. At Netflix: personalization merchandises content that costs less (right-sizing content investment); better retention improves lifetime value; economies of scale reduce per-member costs.

**The trifecta:** Netflix personalization is the canonical DHM example. It delights (better recommendations = easier to find movies you love), is hard to copy (unique taste data for 165M members + proprietary algorithms), and enhances margin (merchandises cheaper content, improves retention).

### Product Strategy = Hypotheses

Biddle's core framing: product strategy is NOT a plan. It's a set of hypotheses you test and evolve.

Process: **Hypothesize → Test → Learn → Repeat**

Each year at Netflix, the team took on about 4-6 product strategies (2005 example: Personalization, Easy/Simple, Social, Margin-enhancement, Unique movie-finding tools, Next-day DVD delivery). Each strategy was a hypothesis to be tested through A/B experiments, not a commitment to be executed.

Reed Hastings on this philosophy: when Biddle asked Hastings what he hoped his legacy would be, Hastings answered "Consumer science" — discovering what delights customers through the scientific process, not intuition.

### The Strategy/Metric/Tactic Lock-up

For each product strategy, define:
- **Strategy** — the high-level hypothesis (e.g., "Personalization")
- **Metric** — the proxy metric that measures whether the strategy is working
- **Tactic** — the specific projects/experiments that test the strategy

At Netflix, each strategy had a dedicated "pod" of engineers, designers, product managers, and data leaders. The lock-up connects the why (strategy) to the what (tactic) through the how-we-know (metric).

### Proxy Metrics

**Problem:** High-level engagement metrics (e.g., monthly retention at Netflix) are hard to move — progress is "almost glacial." You need more sensitive, lower-level proxy metrics.

**Definition:** A proxy metric is a leading indicator that is easier and faster to move than the high-level engagement metric, and ideally, moving the proxy improves the high-level metric.

**Netflix examples of proxy metrics:**
- "Percentage of new members who add at least three titles to their queue during their very first session" — theory: getting users to build a queue early predicts long-term engagement.
- "Percentage of members who rated at least 50 movies during their first two months" — theory: rating movies fuels personalization, which improves recommendations, which improves retention.
- "Percentage of members who watch 15+ minutes in the first month" — early engagement predictor.

**Key insight:** The correlation between proxy and high-level metric must be demonstrated, not assumed. Netflix ran A/B tests to prove that moving the proxy actually moved retention.

### The GEM Model (prioritization)

**G — Growth:** Year-over-year member growth rate.
**E — Engagement:** A specific engagement metric as a proxy for product quality (differs for every org). Netflix used monthly retention.
**M — Monetization:** Lifetime value (LTV) and gross margin.

The GEM model forces cross-functional teams to prioritize across these three dimensions. At any given phase, a company might prioritize growth over monetization (Netflix pre-IPO) or monetization over growth (mature Netflix).

**GEM as roadmap prioritization:** Score each initiative on its expected impact across Growth, Engagement, and Monetization. Forces trade-off conversations between short-term revenue and long-term product quality.

### The GLEe Model (long-term phasing)

**G — Get big** on an initial product/market.
**L — Lead** a key transition.
**E — Expand** into new markets or categories.

Netflix's GLEe: Get big on DVDs → Lead streaming → Expand worldwide → (current) Original content → (speculative) Interactive storytelling.

The model encourages long-term thinking: you don't have to do everything simultaneously. Phase-gate expansion behind achieving scale and leadership in the current phase.

### The Swimlane Model

Each product area (swimlane) has:
- Its own strategies
- Its own proxy metrics
- Its own project backlog

Product leaders for each swimlane define the strategy, proxy metric, and projects for their area. Swimlanes organize the product team and connect day-to-day execution back to product strategy.

### The Quarterly Product Strategy Meeting

Biddle's operational cadence: a Quarterly Product Strategy Meeting that became a mechanism for Netflix culture. Described as "a Board Meeting for Product."

Over many quarters, the cadence of each product area substantially increases — they can execute more tests, generate more learning, and continuously fine-tune instincts about what builds both consumer and shareholder value.

Benefits: strategy stays front and center; clearly described strategies and metrics make it easier to say "no"; simulations provoke debates about how strategy impacts day-to-day decisions.

### The Product Roadmap

The roadmap is "an expression of your strategy." Once strategies, proxy metrics, and projects are defined for each swimlane, the roadmap becomes straightforward — it articulates the focus and organization of the product team with rough time estimates.

## 3. Voice & tone — how Biddle teaches

**Register:** Professorial but warm. Not academic-cold and not startup-bro. Think: experienced teacher who has genuinely done the thing, enjoys sharing what he learned, and wants you to apply it to your own situation.

**Signature rhetorical move:** Tell the Netflix story first, then extract the principle, then invite the audience to apply it. Nearly every concept starts with "At Netflix, we..." followed by the specific case, then generalizes. This is Biddle's primary teaching device.

**Case-study density:** Netflix dominates. Biddle uses it as his primary text because he lived it. Chegg appears as a secondary case. He does NOT extensively use third-party companies the way Balfour uses HubSpot/Slack/Pinterest — Biddle's credibility is first-person Netflix experience.

**Framing language:**
- "Product strategy is a set of hypotheses" — repeated as a refrain.
- "Delight customers in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways" — the one-sentence summary, used as a mantra.
- "Consumer science" — Reed Hastings' phrase that Biddle adopted.
- "Hard to copy" — never "defensible" or "moat" — Biddle has his own vocabulary.

**Engagement style:** Interactive in workshops. Uses exercises with pre-formatted Google Slides. Asks "What would you do?" scenarios. High NPS (69) on workshops. The format is: concept → Netflix case → exercise → apply to your company.

**Tone markers:**
- Candid about what worked and what didn't at Netflix.
- Not defensive about Netflix's weaknesses (acknowledges personalization took a decade to prove).
- Encouraging without being cheerful-empty — "this is hard" but "you can do it systematically."
- Uses simple, concrete language. No jargon for its own sake.

**Visual preferences:** Diagrams, tables, scorecards. The DHM model lends itself to a simple 3-column scorecard. The Strategy/Metric/Tactic lock-up is a table. The GEM model is a prioritization matrix. Biddle's thinking is naturally tabular.

**What Biddle is NOT:**
- Not confrontational or provocative (contrast with Ben Horowitz's "hard thing" style).
- Not consultant-speak (contrast with McKinsey/BCG framings).
- Not primarily analytical/quantitative despite loving metrics (contrast with Balfour's systems-heavy voice). Biddle leads with narrative, then adds metrics.
- Not humble-bragging — he's direct about Netflix experience without false modesty.

## 4. Heuristics / do's / don'ts / gotchas

### Do's
- **Score every product initiative on all three DHM dimensions.** Not just D. Not just D+H. All three must pass.
- **Define proxy metrics before building.** If you can't measure it, you can't test the hypothesis.
- **Treat strategy as hypotheses, not commitments.** The whole point is that you might be wrong. Test and learn.
- **Run quarterly product strategy meetings.** Strategy decays without recurring cadence.
- **Look for the trifecta.** The best product strategies score high on all three of D, H, and M simultaneously.
- **Build hard-to-copy advantages over time.** Brand, network effects, economies of scale, and unique technology take years to develop. Start early.
- **Use bottom-up pattern-matching.** Scan your existing project list — what themes emerge? These reveal implicit strategies you can make explicit.

### Don'ts / anti-patterns
- **Don't optimize for D without H.** If customers love it but competitors can copy it in 6 months, it's a feature, not a strategy.
- **Don't build hard-to-copy advantages that don't delight.** A proprietary system nobody wants is a waste.
- **Don't ignore M.** "We'll figure out monetization later" is a trap. Margin-enhancement must be considered from the start, even if the strategy is to invest in growth now.
- **Don't confuse a project list with a strategy.** A roadmap of features is not a product strategy. Strategy = the hypotheses about HOW you'll delight in hard-to-copy, margin-enhancing ways.
- **Don't use vanity metrics as proxy metrics.** The proxy must correlate with the high-level engagement metric. Pageviews, signups, or DAU without retention correlation are vanity.
- **Don't skip the "hard to copy" analysis.** Many product leaders default to "what delights?" and forget to ask "can anyone else do this?"
- **Don't treat all four types of hard-to-copy equally.** Not all apply to your company. Choose the 1-2 that are genuine advantages.

### Gotchas
- **Proxy metric correlation is not causation.** Just because a proxy metric correlates with retention doesn't mean moving the proxy causes retention improvement. A/B test to verify.
- **The DHM filter is brutal.** Most ideas score well on D but poorly on H or M. That's the point — it filters aggressively.
- **"Hard to copy" is time-dependent.** What's hard to copy today may be commoditized in 3 years. Revisit.
- **Margin-enhancing is NOT "revenue-generating."** Margin is about the spread between value created and cost incurred. Some high-revenue initiatives destroy margin.
- **Netflix is not your company.** Biddle's cases are instructive but Netflix had unique conditions (massive subscriber base, content amortization, early-mover in streaming). Extract the principle, not the tactic.

## 5. Real cases

### Netflix — the master case

**Personalization (the DHM trifecta):**
- D: Better recommendations = easier to find movies you love. Reduced choice overload.
- H: Unique taste data for 165M members + proprietary algorithms. Took a decade to prove impact on retention.
- M: Merchandises cheaper content (algorithm surfaces catalog titles, not just expensive new releases). Enables "right-sizing" content investment. Improves retention = higher LTV.
- Result: Netflix improved monthly cancel rate from ~10% to 2% over two decades.

**Original Content:**
- D: Exclusive shows (House of Cards, Orange is the New Black, Stranger Things) that you can't get elsewhere.
- H: Economies of scale — with 150M+ members, can amortize content cost across a larger base than any competitor.
- M: Owned content has better long-term margins than licensed content (no renewal cost escalation).

**Simple / Easy UX:**
- D: Reduced friction in finding and watching content. Netflix's interface became synonymous with "just works."
- H: Brand advantage (users associate Netflix with simplicity). Hard-to-copy because simplicity requires deep understanding of user behavior.
- M: Reduced support costs, higher conversion, better retention.

**Device Ecosystem (Network Effects):**
- Starting 2008 with Xbox, Netflix built partnerships so every TV, Blu-Ray player, game system, set-top box, and mobile device came pre-wired for Netflix.
- D: Watch anywhere.
- H: Network effects — each new device partner makes Netflix more ubiquitous, making it harder for competitors to replicate the reach.
- M: Distribution cost approaches zero as partners pre-install.

**Proxy Metric — Queue Depth:**
Netflix measured "percentage of new members who add at least three titles to their queue during their very first session" as a proxy for long-term engagement. The team focused on making the service simpler to increase this percentage.

**Proxy Metric — Ratings:**
"Percentage of members who rated at least 50 movies during their first two months" — ratings fueled the personalization algorithm, which improved recommendations, which improved retention.

### Chegg — the startup case

Biddle joined Chegg (2010) as CPO. The company:
- Founded 2001 as "Craigslist for college students" — no traction.
- Pivoted to textbook rental (2008) — massive D: rent a textbook for $50 instead of buying for $200.
- Got big on textbook rental ($150M revenue in first year of Biddle's tenure).
- Led by expanding into homework help: step-by-step solutions to textbook problems.
- Expanded to writing help, tutoring, flashcards → monthly subscription "Chegg Study."
- Went public 2014-2015.

DHM analysis of Chegg textbook rental:
- D: Overwhelming value (75% savings).
- H: Brand with college students + logistics infrastructure. But weak unique tech initially.
- M: Textbook rental had good margins due to textbook reuse across semesters.

GLEe for Chegg: Get big on textbook rental → Lead digital homework help → Expand into broader student services.

Note: Chegg is also a cautionary tale in the AI era — its market cap dropped from $1.2B to ~$150M in 2024 after ChatGPT ate the homework-help value proposition (referenced by Brian Balfour in a different context, not by Biddle directly, but illustrative of the DHM model's "hard to copy" dimension failing).

## 6. Relationship to other frameworks

### Cagan — Inspired / Empowered
Cagan focuses on how product teams are organized and empowered (dual-track, cross-functional squads, product discovery). Biddle's frameworks sit one level above: they define WHAT the empowered team should work on (strategy) and HOW to measure it (metrics). Complementary, not competing.

### Balfour — Four Fits
Balfour's Four Fits is a growth-system diagnostic. DHM is a product-strategy filter. They operate at different levels: DHM helps you decide what to build; Four Fits helps you decide how to distribute and monetize it. Both emphasize margin/monetization. A team could use DHM to filter initiatives and Four Fits to ensure the go-to-market chain holds.

### Playing to Win (Lafley/Martin)
Lafley/Martin's cascade (Winning Aspiration → Where to Play → How to Win → Capabilities → Management Systems) is a broader corporate strategy framework. DHM is more focused: it provides the "How to Win" filter for product-specific decisions. Biddle doesn't reference Playing to Win explicitly, but the concepts are compatible.

### Hamilton Helmer — 7 Powers
Helmer's 7 Powers (Scale Economies, Network Economies, Counter-Positioning, Switching Costs, Branding, Cornered Resource, Process Power) is the deep analysis of "Hard to Copy." Biddle's four H-types (Brand, Network Effects, Economies of Scale, Unique Technology) are a simplified version. 7 Powers goes deeper; DHM is more accessible for product teams.

### North Star Metric
Biddle's proxy metrics are related to but distinct from North Star metrics. Biddle argues for a hierarchy: high-level engagement metric (like retention) → proxy metrics (leading indicators that are easier to move). This is more nuanced than a single North Star. His approach is compatible with Amplitude/Reforge North Star frameworks.

### OKRs
DHM strategies and proxy metrics can be translated into OKRs. Strategy → Objective; Proxy metric → Key Result; Tactics → Key Result initiatives. Biddle doesn't frame it this way explicitly, but the mapping is clean.

### JTBD / Jobs-to-be-Done
JTBD discovers what customers are trying to accomplish. DHM evaluates whether a product strategy ALSO builds competitive advantage and margin. Complementary: JTBD informs the "D" (what would delight?), DHM adds the "H" and "M" filter.
