## 2. POST-BOOK MATERIAL (2018 → 2026)

**Big picture: The Fearless Organization (2018) is the "how to build it" book. Right Kind of Wrong (2023) is the "what breaks when you don't" book with a new taxonomy that reframes the whole failure question.** The skill has to treat 2023 as a substantial extension of the framework, not a companion.

### The Failure Taxonomy (Right Kind of Wrong, 2023) — the most important post-book addition

Edmondson's three archetypes of failure. This is now core method, not add-on.

- **Basic failure** — preventable failures in known territory. Caused by inattention, skipped steps, or bad execution of well-understood processes. "You were supposed to take a left, you took a right." Response: **checklists, standardization, training.** These are not learning opportunities — they are execution failures.
- **Complex failure** — failures in familiar territory that emerge from interactions across many variables (weather + fatigue + broken tool + missed handoff). No single cause. Response: **better system design, defense-in-depth, near-miss reporting.** These are learning opportunities, but at the system level.
- **Intelligent failure** — failures in **novel territory**, undertaken as small, hypothesis-driven experiments, in pursuit of a goal you couldn't achieve without trying. Kim's tests: novel territory, opportunity-driven, hypothesis-based, as small as possible to learn. **These are the failures to celebrate, and the ones that get punished most in low-PS cultures.**

Verbatim (from *Right Kind of Wrong*, 2023, and interviews):
> "Not all failures are created equal."
> "An intelligent failure is the right kind of wrong."

**Why this matters for the skill:** the biggest culture-killing move a leader makes is treating all failures as blameworthy. The Fearless Organization gives you the diagnosis (low PS); Right Kind of Wrong gives you the taxonomy (which failures are learning, which are execution, which are systemic).

### The "psychological safety ≠ nice" doubling-down (2019-present)

After the 2018 book landed, the biggest misread was that PS = comfort / niceness / lowered standards. Edmondson has spent most of her post-book airtime pushing back against this. Key defenses:

- **Blog post: "Psychological Safety ≠ Anything Goes"** — [amycedmondson.com/psychological-safety-≠-anything-goes/](https://amycedmondson.com/psychological-safety-%E2%89%A0-anything-goes/). Explicit refutation of "lowering standards, giving up on accountability, or 'wrapping teams in cotton wool'."
- **Ongoing HBR pieces** — "Make It Safe for Employees to Speak Up — Especially in Risky Times."
- **NeuroLeadership Institute conversation** — psychological safety AND accountability as two separate dimensions that must both be high.

Verbatim (recurring across TED, blog posts, interviews):
> "Psychological safety is not about being nice. It's about giving candid feedback, openly admitting mistakes, and learning from each other."
> "Holding people accountable for great results and psychological safety are not a trade-off. They are two separate dimensions."

### The 2×2 with Accountability — the anti-misread structural device

The most important refinement in Edmondson's post-book teaching: psychological safety must be presented **paired with accountability / performance standards**, never alone. The 2×2:

|  | High Standards / Accountability | Low Standards / Accountability |
|---|---|---|
| **High Psychological Safety** | **Learning Zone** | **Comfort Zone** |
| **Low Psychological Safety** | **Anxiety Zone** | **Apathy Zone** |

- **Learning Zone** — the goal. High PS + high standards. People take interpersonal risks in service of demanding work.
- **Comfort Zone** — high PS + low standards. People feel safe but nothing hard is being asked. This is the "PS as being nice" misread lived out. Nothing ships.
- **Anxiety Zone** — low PS + high standards. High-pressure, high-fear environments. Compliance without learning. Boeing 737 MAX. Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal. The "brutal high performer" org.
- **Apathy Zone** — low PS + low standards. Neither care nor push. Coast-to-retirement culture.

**Non-negotiable for the skill:** never present PS in isolation. Always present the 2×2 with accountability on the second axis. Presenting PS alone is exactly how the misread happens.

### The Leader Toolkit — Fearless Organization Part III (Framing, Situational Humility, Curiosity)

Edmondson's "how to build it" prescription. Three leader moves:

1. **Setting the Stage (Framing the Work)** — leader names the interdependent nature of the work, the uncertainty involved, and the necessity of voice. Reframes the default assumption ("mistakes = failure = punishment") to ("this is complex, novel, learning-intensive — voice is required").
2. **Inviting Participation** — via:
   - **Situational Humility** — the leader publicly admits they don't have all the answers. Kim's stock phrase: *"we've never been here before."* Signals the invitation is genuine.
   - **Proactive Inquiry** — asking specific questions, not generic ones. "What are we missing?" not "any thoughts?"
   - **Structures and processes** — forums, guidelines, roles that make voice a designed-in behavior, not a hopeful one.
3. **Responding Productively** — the leader's response to voice determines whether voice happens again. Express appreciation. Destigmatize failure. Sanction clear violations.

### Post-2023 emphasis: leader-first, not bystander-first

Similar arc to Kim Scott's Just Work → Radical Respect rewrite. Edmondson's mature position: **PS is a leader-designed condition, not an emergent property.** Bystanders and individual contributors can practice speaking up, but the system-level failure requires system-level intervention, and only leaders can design that.

### Recurring rhetorical moves

1. **"The intelligent failure that led to psychological safety"** — Edmondson tells this origin story often. As a PhD student, she designed a study expecting that better hospital teams would have fewer medication errors. Data showed the opposite: better teams reported MORE errors. She spent months confused, then realized: better teams weren't making more errors, they were more willing to REPORT them. The variable she was actually measuring was psychological safety. Cited in: Behavioral Scientist essay, *Right Kind of Wrong*, most keynotes. The story does double duty — it explains where the concept came from AND it demonstrates the intelligent-failure taxonomy in her own work.

2. **Definitional precision** — she opens almost every talk by defining psychological safety and then immediately defining what it is NOT. The definition-then-negation is her signature move. She knows the framework gets misread and she pre-empts it.

3. **The 2×2 with accountability, always paired** — post-2018 she rarely presents PS alone. The Learning Zone / Comfort Zone / Anxiety Zone / Apathy Zone framing is always the follow-up.

4. **Case-driven teaching** — Columbia, Ford under Mulally, Google Aristotle, hospital error studies, Pixar. She uses cases like a case-method professor because she is one — the case does the pedagogy.

5. **"Situational humility"** — her operational phrase for what leaders must model. She uses it verbatim; the skill should too.

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

### The single non-negotiable — "psychological safety is not being nice"

**This is the biggest and most common misread.** Edmondson has spent seven years post-book pushing back against it. Anyone applying the framework must be prepared to name the misread immediately. Concrete signs the user has fallen into it:

- Talking about PS as "creating a safe space" without mentioning accountability or standards.
- Framing hard feedback as a violation of PS.
- Using PS to justify avoiding conflict.
- Treating PS as an HR/wellness initiative rather than a learning-and-performance one.

**Correct move:** always pair PS with accountability. If user talks about PS in isolation, name the missing axis and route them to the 2×2.

### Do's

- **Frame the work as complex and interdependent before asking for voice.** Voice doesn't happen because you gave permission. It happens because the leader named the work as requiring voice. The Framing move is the first leader move for a reason.
- **Practice situational humility.** Say "we've never been here before" or "I don't have the answer" explicitly and publicly. The invitation is only credible if the leader models the vulnerability first.
- **Ask specific questions, not generic ones.** "What are we missing?" > "any thoughts?" The specificity signals that voice is expected, not merely tolerated.
- **Diagnose the failure type BEFORE responding.** Basic failure → checklists and training. Complex failure → system redesign. Intelligent failure → celebrate the learning even if the outcome hurt. Treating all failures the same is the blame culture in a lab coat.
- **Sanction clear violations.** Edmondson is explicit: PS ≠ tolerance for everything. Bullies, harassers, and clear code-of-conduct violators must face consequences. Sanction is a feature of a psychologically safe org, not a violation of one.
- **Reward messengers, not the message.** People bring you bad news because they trust you'll handle it. Fire the messenger once and the whole org learns to hide.
- **Distinguish voice from decision-making.** PS invites voice. It does not require consensus. Leaders still decide.
- **Measure PS at the team level.** Not the individual, not the organization — the team is where PS lives. Fearless Organization Scan operates at team level for a reason.

### Don'ts

- **Don't call the framework "safety" and let people hear "comfort."** Edmondson herself has expressed regret at the word "safety" — many think it means physical or emotional safety. Correct usage: "psychological safety" as a compound term, always defined, always paired with the interpersonal-risk-taking anchor.
- **Don't treat PS as an alternative to accountability.** They are two separate dimensions. Both must be high. The 2×2 with accountability is the anti-misread device — use it every time.
- **Don't punish intelligent failures.** The single fastest way to kill learning. If your team ran a small hypothesis-driven experiment in novel territory and it didn't work — that is exactly the failure you want more of. Celebrate the process; interrogate what was learned; move on.
- **Don't treat all failures the same.** Blame culture treats basic, complex, and intelligent failures identically — as things to punish. Edmondson's counter: the leader's first job is to diagnose the failure type, then respond appropriately.
- **Don't mistake silence for agreement.** Kim Scott says this about feedback; Edmondson says it about safety. Silence is data — it means voice isn't happening. In a low-PS org, the loudest teams look aligned because the quiet ones have given up.
- **Don't announce "we have psychological safety here."** As with Radical Candor, announcement is a red flag. PS is measured by whether people actually speak up, not by whether leaders claim they can.
- **Don't build PS as a one-off intervention.** No offsite fixes this. PS is a sustained condition maintained by leader behavior every week.
- **Don't scale PS by exhortation.** "Everyone should feel safe to speak up" is a poster, not a practice. PS scales by leader behavior + structures + consequences.

### Gotchas

- **The Comfort Zone trap.** High PS + low standards. People love working there and nothing ships. Diagnostic: if the retro is warm and nothing changes, you're in the Comfort Zone.
- **The Anxiety Zone masquerading as high performance.** Boeing 737 MAX. Wells Fargo. Companies that appear to hit targets while dissent is being suppressed. The numbers look great until they don't.
- **Google Aristotle as license to skip the work.** Everyone cites Aristotle as proof PS matters. Fewer people do the work of building it. Naming the study is not the same as running Framing / Situational Humility / Curiosity as leader practices.
- **The "we don't have time for psychological safety" org.** Same failure mode Kim Scott names about feedback. The compounding cost of unspoken concerns dwarfs the time cost of soliciting them. Columbia is Exhibit A.
- **Confusing PS with radical transparency.** PS is about voice without retribution. Not about mandatory disclosure of everything to everyone. See relationship to Radical Candor / Radical Transparency below.
- **Treating PS as a fixed trait rather than a team-level condition.** Same person in Team A speaks up freely, in Team B goes silent. That's not personality — that's PS in each team.
- **Applying PS across cultures without calibration.** The axes are universal; the calibration of what "voice" looks like varies. In a high-power-distance culture, "speaking up" may look different than in a low-power-distance one. Adjust calibration; don't drop the axes.

### The failure-taxonomy diagnostic — use this before responding to any failure

For any failure being discussed:

1. **Was this in known or novel territory?**
   - Known → Basic or Complex.
   - Novel → likely Intelligent.
2. **Was there a single cause or many interacting causes?**
   - Single, preventable → Basic. Response: checklists, training, standardization.
   - Multiple, interacting → Complex. Response: system redesign, defense-in-depth.
3. **Was it hypothesis-driven, opportunity-motivated, and as small as possible?**
   - Yes → Intelligent. Response: celebrate the learning, extract the insight, run the next experiment.
4. **Was there clear misconduct (violation of ethics, safety, code)?**
   - Yes → this is a sanction case, not a failure-taxonomy case. Sanction it. PS does not shield misconduct.

## 5. REAL CASES EDMONDSON USES PUBLICLY

### Primary cases from the books and talks

- **The hospital medication error study (1996 PhD work)** — the origin story. She expected better teams to have fewer errors; found the opposite; realized she was measuring reporting rates, not error rates; PS was the hidden variable. Uses this to teach both the concept and the "intelligent failure" taxonomy (her own).
- **Columbia Space Shuttle (2003)** — engineer Rodney Rocha's concerns about foam damage went unheard in a low-PS culture; official Boeing report was accepted without challenge. Canonical PS-failure case. HBR IdeaCast episode "Leadership Lessons from a NASA Tragedy" (August 2024) is her deep treatment.
- **Boeing 737 MAX** — Anxiety Zone case. Engineers had concerns; culture didn't support raising them; retribution was the norm; two crashes and 346 deaths followed.
- **Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal** — Anxiety Zone. High standards, low PS. Front-line workers created millions of fake accounts to hit quotas because raising the impossibility of the targets was not safe.
- **Volkswagen Dieselgate** — Anxiety Zone. Engineers who knew the emissions software was cheating could not raise it safely.
- **Google Aristotle project** — validated Edmondson's earlier work; PS was the #1 predictor of team performance across 180 Google teams. Widely cited; often as license to skip doing the work.
- **Ford under Alan Mulally** — turnaround case. Mulally introduced weekly "Business Plan Review" meetings where every executive had to color-code their metrics red/yellow/green. First few weeks: everyone was green. Mulally's line: "we're going to lose $17 billion this year and nothing is red?" One exec finally went red. Mulally applauded. The room cracked open. Classic Framing + Situational Humility + Responding Productively.
- **Pixar** — Braintrust as PS-in-practice. Filmmakers give and receive brutal creative feedback in a norm-protected space. NOT nice; NOT low-standards; deeply psychologically safe by Edmondson's definition.
- **IDEO** — design-thinking teams as high-PS learning environments.
- **Julie Morath's Children's Hospitals and Clinics of Minnesota patient-safety initiative** — the case Edmondson uses most often in *The Fearless Organization*. Morath explicitly re-framed error reporting from blame to learning.

### Cases in Right Kind of Wrong (2023)

- **Thomas Edison's 10,000 filament experiments** — canonical intelligent failure at scale.
- **Basic failure examples** — surgical checklists (Atul Gawande overlap), pharmacy mis-dispensing.
- **Complex failure examples** — Fukushima, the 2008 financial crisis system-level failures.
- **Intelligent failure examples** — early-stage clinical trials, R&D moonshots, startup pivots.

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

### vs. Radical Candor (Kim Scott)

- **Kim:** manager-report feedback dyad. Care Personally + Challenge Directly. Individual behavior.
- **Amy:** team-level condition that enables voice. Psychological Safety + Accountability. Systemic behavior.
- **The bridge:** Kim's Care Personally + Challenge Directly maps almost perfectly onto Amy's high PS + high accountability. Both authors spend most of their airtime warning against the misread that drops one of the two axes (Kim: dropping Care produces Obnoxious Aggression; Amy: dropping accountability produces Comfort Zone).
- **When each is better:** Kim for the specific feedback conversation; Amy for the team-level condition that makes those conversations possible. Kim operates inside the container Amy designs.
- **Compose:** run PS at the team level (Amy) + Radical Candor at the manager-report level (Kim) + Career Conversations (Russ Laraway via Kim). Nearly the full stack.

### vs. Five Dysfunctions of a Team (Patrick Lencioni)

- **Lencioni:** pyramid model — trust → conflict → commitment → accountability → results. Trust as foundation.
- **Edmondson:** PS as the belief that permits interpersonal risk-taking. Similar substrate concept but Amy is more precise about what "trust" means (safety for specific behaviors) and more rigorous about the evidence.
- **When Lencioni is better:** offsite / team-building work with a group that responds to narrative more than to research.
- **When Edmondson is better:** any context where research rigor and precise diagnostics matter (leadership team retros, org-wide culture initiatives, healthcare / high-stakes environments).
- **Compose:** Lencioni for the diagnostic conversation; Edmondson for the operational work.

### vs. Radical Candor / Radical Transparency (Ray Dalio / Bridgewater)

- **Dalio:** radical transparency — full disclosure, recorded meetings, public rating apps.
- **Edmondson:** PS is about voice without retribution, not mandatory disclosure. She has been careful not to conflate the two.
- **The failure mode of Dalio's model** (from an Edmondson lens): radical transparency without accountability calibration can produce the Anxiety Zone at scale. High disclosure norms without leader psychological safety practices can feel like the surveillance version of "speak up."

### vs. DORA / Accelerate (Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble, Gene Kim) / Westrum culture typology

- **Westrum's 1988 typology** — Pathological / Bureaucratic / Generative culture. Generative culture: high cooperation, messengers welcomed, novelty-seeking, cross-functional bridging.
- **DORA:** Westrum's generative culture is one of the strongest predictors of software delivery performance.
- **Edmondson connection:** generative culture ≈ high PS + high accountability + learning orientation. DORA's finding is the software-delivery version of Edmondson's org-wide claim.
- **Compose:** Edmondson at the leadership-behavior level, DORA at the software-delivery level. Both point to the same substrate.

### vs. High Output Management (Andy Grove)

- **Grove:** managerial output = output of your team + output of teams you influence. 1:1s, task-relevant maturity, meeting design.
- **Edmondson:** the conditions under which Grove's mechanics actually generate voice, learning, and performance. Grove is the plumbing; Edmondson tells you whether water actually flows through it.
- **Compose:** Grove's 1:1s are one of the strongest structures for building PS at the manager-report level.

### vs. Scaling People (Claire Hughes Johnson)

- **CHJ:** operating manual for building high-functioning companies as they scale. Emphasizes context-setting, feedback rituals, and leader clarity.
- **Edmondson:** the substrate CHJ's rituals produce. CHJ's operating cadence is designed to produce Learning Zone conditions.
- **Compose:** natural pairing — Edmondson for the "why," CHJ for the specific "how."

### vs. Playing to Win / Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Martin / Rumelt)

- **Different problem entirely.** These are strategy frameworks. PS is a management framework.
- **The composition:** a great strategy executed in an Anxiety Zone team gets partial credit at best. Strategy in a Learning Zone team compounds.
- **The failure mode to name:** leaders who obsess over strategy and ignore team conditions get strategy execution that looks like Wells Fargo's cross-selling — technically hitting numbers, systemically hollow.

**Two operational notes for the skill builder:**

1. **The 2×2 with accountability is the anti-misread structural device — never present PS alone.** The single most common misapplication is presenting PS as a nice-to-have or a substitute for standards. The skill must always show the 2×2, name the four zones, and route toward Learning Zone specifically.
2. **Right Kind of Wrong (2023) is core method, not add-on.** The failure taxonomy (Basic / Complex / Intelligent) reframes how leaders respond to failures — which is the central lever for building PS at the team level. Treating 2023 as a companion misses half the current framework.
