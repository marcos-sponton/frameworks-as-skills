## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

Torres's method is a set of habits that fit inside an integrating visual artifact (the Opportunity Solution Tree). The habits are inseparable from the tree — teams that adopt the tree without the habits get a static poster; teams that adopt the habits without the tree lose visibility into how the pieces connect.

### Definition of continuous discovery

Torres's canonical definition, repeated across essays, podcasts, and the book:

> "At a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers by the team building the product, where they're conducting small research activities in pursuit of a desired product outcome." — *Continuous Discovery Habits*, 2021.

Three words matter:
- **Weekly** — the cadence. Not monthly, not "when we can". Non-negotiable.
- **By the team building the product** — not by a separate research team, not by a PM alone. The product trio (PM + designer + engineer) does it together.
- **Product outcome** — the discovery has a direction, set by a specific measurable outcome the team owns.

### The 5 continuous discovery habits

1. **Interview at least one customer per week as a full product trio.**
2. **Map opportunities using an Opportunity Solution Tree.**
3. **Surface and test assumptions before building.**
4. **Run small experiments to validate ideas.**
5. **Involve the full product trio (not just researchers) in weekly discovery.**

### The Opportunity Solution Tree (OST) — Torres's central visual artifact

Introduced in 2016, formalized in the 2021 book. A visual tree that maps the path from business outcome down to tested solutions.

**Structure — four layers:**

**Layer 1 — Outcome (root/top):**
- **Product outcome**, not business outcome, not traction metric.
- Product outcome measures **customer behavior** that drives business outcomes.
- Business outcome (lagging, e.g. "grow revenue 30%") is outside the trio's span of control. Product outcome (leading, e.g. "increase weekly active users completing checkout") is within it.
- One outcome per team. Focus is the point.

**Layer 2 — Opportunities (trunk & major branches):**
- Definition: *"unmet customer needs, pain points, and desires that, if addressed, will drive your desired outcome"* — Product Talk, canonical OST essay.
- Sourced from **story-based interviews with real customers** (see Habit 1). Torres's benchmark: 3–4 interviews before you can even start populating the tree honestly; update after every 3–4 new interviews.
- Framed as customer needs / pain points / desires, NOT solutions. Test: **"Is there more than one way to address this?"** If no, it's a solution disguised as an opportunity.

**Layer 3 — Solutions (secondary branches):**
- Definition: *"a product, a feature, a service, a workflow, a process, documentation, or anything else we offer to address an opportunity"*.
- **Consider at least 3 solutions per target opportunity** — the diversity forces the team past their first idea.
- Start small; one solution addresses one opportunity for continuous delivery.

**Layer 4 — Assumption Tests (leaves):**
- Each solution is broken down into the assumptions it depends on.
- Team identifies the **riskiest assumptions** and tests those first.
- Assumption tests ≠ full experiments (see below).

**Prerequisites Torres names:** a theory of the target customer, a defined product outcome, 3–4 story-based interviews before populating the opportunity space.

**Rules across layers:**
- Update opportunity space every 3–4 interviews.
- One outcome per team.
- Don't confuse opportunities with solutions ("is there more than one way to address this?").
- Riskiest assumptions first.
- The tree is **living** — it evolves weekly, not a poster.

### Story-based interviewing — the interview technique

Torres's most-detailed operational method. It has strict rules; paraphrasing into "just talk to users" loses the method.

**Opening question — the exact form:**
> "Tell me about the last time you [relevant behavior]."

Example: *"Tell me about the last time you watched Netflix."* This is not a template — it is the opening. It grounds the interview in **a specific past instance**, not a generalization.

**The core rules:**

1. **Ask about past behavior, never future or hypothetical.** Do NOT ask "would you use this?" — people answer as an idealized version of themselves.
2. **Never ask for opinions.** Opinions are cheap; behavior is data.
3. **Never ask for generalizations.** "Tell me about your experience on Netflix" invites System-1 fast thinking and cognitive bias. Torres's alternative: ground in a specific instance.
4. **Never ask closed / yes-no questions** in the story portion.
5. **Never rely on self-reported behavioral metrics.** People are unreliable narrators of frequency and duration. Use analytics for the metric; use interviews for the story.
6. **The participant does most of the talking.** *"The art of the interview is knowing what to ask when in a way that encourages the participant to open up and share their experience."* — Torres, Product Talk, Apr 2024.

**Follow-up technique — the timeline walk:**
Once the participant is telling a specific story, walk it as a timeline: "start at the beginning, then what happened next, then what happened next." Follow emotion (emotional memory is dense). Use silence (space for the participant to search their memory).

**The critical skill (Torres names it explicitly):** recognizing when a participant flips from a specific story into generalization ("...and that's usually what I do") and gently guiding them back to the specific instance.

**The four teaching dimensions of the Interview Coach** (revealed in her 2025 essay on the AI coach she built for her course): opening with story-based questions · setting the scene · building the timeline · redirecting generalizations. That's her operational rubric.

### Assumption Mapping — the 5 categories

For each solution on the OST, Torres has teams map the assumptions across 5 categories. Every product idea depends on assumptions in all 5.

1. **Desirability** — do customers actually want this? Will they engage with it?
2. **Viability** — does it make business sense? Adequate returns?
3. **Feasibility** — can we technically build it? Do we have the org capability?
4. **Usability** — can customers actually use it? Cognitive load, workflow fit?
5. **Ethical** — what harm could this cause? To customers, to the company, to third parties?

Torres added **"ethical"** as an explicit fifth category — an evolution from the classic 3 (desirability, viability, feasibility). It is a load-bearing move: ethical assumptions get missed when you only test the classic three.

**Story mapping as an assumption-surfacing technique:** lay out each step users need to take to get value from the solution. At every step, ask "what has to be true here?" Those are the assumptions.

**Team bias check:** most teams are biased toward testing one or two categories and blind to others. Engineers over-test feasibility; PMs over-test viability; designers over-test usability. Explicitly force the team to name assumptions in ALL five.

### Small Assumption Tests (NOT full experiments)

> "We rarely have time to run real experiments in discovery." — Torres, Product Talk (Assumption Testing).

Torres distinguishes **assumption tests** from **experiments**:
- **Experiment** = tests the whole idea (does the solution work?).
- **Assumption test** = tests one specific assumption in isolation. Faster, cheaper, teaches you which risk to worry about.

> "Assumption testing makes it clear that we're testing a single assumption and not the whole idea." — same essay.

**Her rule for teams that claim they don't have time:**
> "Take whatever solution you are working on right now... figure out something you can do in the next hour to evaluate that risk."

The point is the practice: small tests, run weekly, inside the same trio cadence that runs interviews. Not a research project.

### Continuous vs project-based discovery — the diagnostic contrast

Torres frames the industry's default as **project-based discovery**: research at the start of a project, usability test at the end, A/B test in production. Discovery is a phase, not a habit. She rejects this.

**Continuous discovery:**
- Weekly customer contact by the trio, indefinitely.
- Discovery infrastructure (like sprints or stand-ups), not project phases.
- Small research activities each week, not big-bang research sprints.

**Project-based discovery is where the build trap lives.** (Torres and Perri overlap explicitly here — see Perri Ep 269, May 2026.)

### The product trio

- **PM + designer + engineer**, jointly running discovery.
- Replaces serial handoff ("PM defines, designer mocks, engineer builds").
- All three participate in interviews, in mapping the OST, in surfacing assumptions.
- Not "the PM does discovery and reports back". The engineer being in the interview is not optional.

### Product outcome vs business outcome vs output — Torres's canonical distinctions

- **Business outcome** — what the business needs (revenue, retention). Lagging. Requires multiple functions. Outside the trio's control.
- **Product outcome** — customer behavior change that drives the business outcome. Leading. Within the trio's control. This is what the trio owns.
- **Output** — a thing shipped (feature, release, story point). Not an outcome regardless of framing.

Her canonical piece on this is **Defining Product Outcomes: The 8 Most Common Mistakes You Should Avoid** — https://www.producttalk.org/defining-product-outcomes/. The 8 mistakes:

1. Disguising outputs as outcomes (e.g., "build an Android app" as an outcome).
2. Not connecting outcomes to business value.
3. Giving teams outcomes outside their span of control (business outcomes assigned to product teams).
4. Hyper-focusing on a single traction metric that leaves no latitude for exploration.
5. Creating too many cross-team dependencies.
6. Measuring the action, not the value of the action.
7. Setting sentiment outcomes (NPS) with no behavioral direction.
8. Setting outcomes without an accountability model that encourages learning.

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

### Do's

- **Interview weekly, as a trio, without exception.** The cadence IS the practice. Once you skip a week, you're back to project-based discovery.
- **Ground every interview in a specific past instance.** Open with "tell me about the last time you [X]".
- **Build and update the OST as a living artifact.** Refresh the opportunity space every 3–4 interviews.
- **Force at least 3 solutions per target opportunity.** The diversity kills the "first idea = the idea" pathology.
- **Map assumptions across all 5 categories** (desirability, viability, feasibility, usability, ethical) — not just the 1–2 your team is biased toward.
- **Test the riskiest assumption first**, with the smallest test that would actually change your mind.
- **Set product outcomes at the trio's span of control**, not business outcomes.
- **Update the tree every 3–4 interviews** — freshness is part of the method.

### Don'ts

- **Don't ask opinions.** ("What do you think of X?" is not discovery.)
- **Don't ask about future or hypothetical behavior.** ("Would you use this?" is not discovery.)
- **Don't ask for generalizations.** ("Tell me about your experience with X" invites cognitive bias.)
- **Don't rely on self-reported behavioral frequency.** People misremember. Use analytics for the number; use interviews for the story behind it.
- **Don't put features on the OST as opportunities.** Test: "is there more than one way to address this?" If no, it's a solution disguised.
- **Don't do one-and-done research.** A single research sprint at the start of a project ≠ continuous discovery.
- **Don't assign business outcomes to product teams** and expect them to move the needle. Business outcomes require multiple functions; the trio can only move product outcomes.
- **Don't send the PM alone to interviews.** The whole trio goes. The engineer being present is not optional.
- **Don't skip assumption testing to go straight to A/B tests.** A/B tests are validation of a shipped solution; assumption tests are the discovery-phase risk-reducer.
- **Don't dogmatize the method.** Torres explicitly refuses to be turned into a recipe.

### Gotchas / common misapplications

- **Adopting the OST without the interview cadence** → static poster. The tree is only alive if it updates weekly from real interviews.
- **The "opportunity" that's actually a feature.** Recurring: "customers can fast-forward through commercials" (solution) instead of "customers don't like commercials" (opportunity).
- **Setting outcomes at the wrong altitude** — either business outcomes assigned to trios (Mistake #3 in the 8 mistakes essay) or hyper-narrow traction metrics that don't leave room for exploration (Mistake #4).
- **NPS as sole outcome** — Mistake #7. Sentiment metrics without behavioral direction.
- **Individual OKRs on top of the trio.** The trio is team-based; individual accountability breaks the shared learning loop.
- **"We already interview customers"** — usually project-based, not continuous. The diagnostic: is the interview happening this week, next week, and the week after, by the trio?
- **Skipping the "which assumption?" step** and jumping to build.
- **Falling in love with the first solution.** The 3-solutions-minimum rule exists to prevent this.
- **PM does interview, reports back to trio.** Loses the shared context that makes the trio work.
- **Treating story-based interviewing as "just being conversational".** The method has strict rules — past behavior only, specific instances, timeline walk, redirect generalizations. Paraphrasing into "have a conversation" is the anti-pattern.

### Torres's own pushback on how the method gets used

Torres has publicly acknowledged that *Continuous Discovery Habits* has been criticized for being used to justify diminished UX-designer roles and layoffs of dedicated user researchers. She pushes back: the method is about the **trio doing discovery together**, not replacing researchers. Researchers have specialist depth the trio can't replicate; the trio does weekly practice, researchers do deeper studies. Both, not either.

She also explicitly refuses to write about JTBD or use "MVP" — not because they're wrong, but because she wants to keep her method "methodology-agnostic" and avoid the tribal wars.

## 6. POST-BOOK MATERIAL (2021 → 2026)

The 2021 book laid down the frame. Post-2021 refinements:

### Ethical as the 5th assumption category

The book chapter on assumptions covers desirability/viability/feasibility/usability. She has since **added "ethical"** as an explicit fifth category — codified in 2024+ essays and LinkedIn posts. This is now the canonical five, not the classic four.

### Ladder of Evidence

A newer framework (introduced 2024–2025 via podcast and LinkedIn, discussed on *All Things Product*) for evaluating the quality of customer research. Ranks evidence types by strength, from opinion → self-reported behavior → observed behavior → measured behavior. Purpose: give teams a common language for "how strong is this signal?"

She explicitly does NOT compete with Cagan. Her essays [The Product Operating Model Explained (Oct 2025)](https://www.producttalk.org/the-product-operating-model/) and [Is Your Organization Ready to Adopt the Product Operating Model? Her position: the trio does the weekly cadence, researchers do the deep specialist studies. Both, not either.
- **OKRs.** Compatible. Product outcomes fit as the Objective; measurable behavior change as Key Results. She warns against business-outcome OKRs assigned to trios.
- **Shape Up (Basecamp / Singer).** Compatible on delivery cadence; Torres's frame is about discovery, Shape Up about shaping-then-betting delivery. Can run both.
- **Rumelt / Playing to Win.** Different altitude — those are business-strategy frames. Torres assumes the strategy exists and the trio is delivering product outcomes that serve it.
