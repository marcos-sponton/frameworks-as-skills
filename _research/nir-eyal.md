## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

### 2a. The Hook Model -- four-phase cycle

Eyal defines the Hook Model as a four-step process embedded into products to bring users back repeatedly without relying on costly advertising or aggressive messaging:

> "A hook is an experience designed to connect the user's problem to a solution frequently enough to form a habit." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Introduction

The goal is not a single transaction but a **loop** -- a cycle that repeats, deepens, and becomes increasingly automatic. Products that successfully create hooks change user behavior by creating **associations** between internal emotional states and the product's solution.

#### Phase 1: Trigger

The trigger is the actuator of behavior -- the spark plug in the engine. Two types:

**External triggers** -- environmental cues that tell the user what to do next:
- **Paid triggers** -- ads, SEM, paid social. Effective for acquisition, expensive to sustain.
- **Earned triggers** -- press, viral videos, featured app store placement. Hard to sustain.
- **Relationship triggers** -- word of mouth, referrals, invitations from friends. Viral loops.
- **Owned triggers** -- app icons on home screen, email newsletters, push notifications. The trigger the company controls.

**Internal triggers** -- emotional states and situations that become associated with the product through repeated use. They are the ultimate goal:
- Boredom -> scroll Instagram
- Loneliness -> open Facebook
- Uncertainty -> Google it
- Anxiety about missing something -> check email

**The critical distinction:** The entire Hook cycle exists to move users from external triggers to internal triggers. External triggers get the user to the product; internal triggers bring them back automatically. A product still dependent on external triggers has not yet formed a habit.

> "When users start to automatically cue their next action, the new habit becomes part of their everyday routine. Over time, users begin to associate the product with the internal trigger, and the external trigger is no longer needed." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 2

**The 5 Whys for trigger discovery:** Start with a surface behavior, ask "why?" five times to reach the emotional root. Eyal's email example from *Hooked*:
1. Why would he use email? To send and receive messages.
2. Why would he want to do that? To share and find information quickly.
3. Why would he want to do that? To know what's going on in the lives of coworkers, friends, family.
4. Why would he need to know that? To know if someone needs him.
5. Why would that matter? **Fear of being out of the loop.**

The internal trigger is fear -- not "sending email."

#### Phase 2: Action

The action is the **simplest behavior done in anticipation of reward**. Eyal builds on BJ Fogg's Behavior Model:

**B = MAT** (Behavior = Motivation + Ability + Trigger)

Three things must be present simultaneously:
1. **Motivation** -- sufficient desire to act. Six core motivators in three pairs (Fogg): seek pleasure / avoid pain; seek hope / avoid fear; seek social acceptance / avoid social rejection.
2. **Ability** -- the capacity to perform the action, measured by simplicity.
3. **Trigger** -- the cue (external or internal) from Phase 1.

**Key distinction:** Increasing ability (reducing friction) is almost always more effective than increasing motivation. Motivation is expensive and unreliable; simplicity is designable.

**Six factors of simplicity** (from Fogg):
1. **Time** -- how long does the action take?
2. **Money** -- what is the financial cost?
3. **Physical effort** -- how much exertion is required?
4. **Brain cycles** -- how much thinking is required?
5. **Social deviance** -- does it go against social norms?
6. **Non-routine** -- how much does it disrupt existing habits?

> "To increase the likelihood of a behavior occurring, Fogg instructs designers to focus on simplicity as a function of the user's scarcest resource at that moment." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 3

**Cognitive biases that influence action** (drawn from Kahneman, Cialdini, behavioral economics):
- **Scarcity effect** -- limited availability increases perceived value
- **Framing effect** -- context shapes perception of the action
- **Anchoring** -- initial reference points shape subsequent judgments
- **Endowed progress effect** -- people who feel they've made progress are more motivated to complete

#### Phase 3: Variable Reward

The variable reward is the hook's engine -- it creates desire by introducing **unpredictability** into the reward. Draws on B.F. Skinner's variable-ratio reinforcement schedules and the neuroscience of the nucleus accumbens.

**Key distinction:** It is the **variability** that creates desire, not the reward itself. Predictable rewards become boring. Variable rewards sustain engagement because the brain's dopamine system responds more strongly to the *anticipation* of an uncertain outcome than to the outcome itself.

> "What draws us to act is not the sensation we receive from the reward itself, but the need to alleviate the craving for that reward." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 4

**Three types of variable rewards:**

1. **Rewards of the Tribe** -- social rewards driven by connectedness. Validation, cooperation, competition, recognition. Examples: Facebook likes, Stack Overflow reputation, League of Legends team play.

2. **Rewards of the Hunt** -- the search for material resources and information. The thrill of the chase. Examples: Twitter/X feed, slot machines, Pinterest, Google search results. Root: same dopamine circuitry that drove foraging.

3. **Rewards of the Self** -- intrinsic rewards of mastery, competence, completion. Examples: video game leveling, completing a to-do list, Codecademy badges, inbox zero.

**Critical nuance -- autonomy:** Variable rewards must preserve the user's sense of autonomy. Reactance theory (Brehm, 1966) shows that when people feel freedom is threatened, they rebel. Variable rewards that feel manipulative or coercive backfire.

**Finite vs. infinite variability:** Experiences with finite variability (e.g., a game with 500 fixed levels) become predictable and lose appeal. Experiences with infinite variability (e.g., an algorithmic content feed) sustain engagement indefinitely.

> "Experiences with finite variability become increasingly predictable with use and lose their appeal over time. Experiences that maintain user interest by sustaining variability with use exhibit infinite variability." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 4

#### Phase 4: Investment

The investment phase is where users put something **into** the product -- data, content, followers, reputation, skill, or effort -- that (a) improves the product for next use and (b) loads the next trigger. Investment does NOT deliver immediate gratification -- it stores value for the future.

**Key distinction:** The investment phase closes the loop. Without it, the hook is open -- a one-time experience. With it, each cycle makes the next cycle more likely.

**Types of investment:**
- **Content** -- photos uploaded, messages sent, playlists created
- **Data** -- preferences, usage history, personalization signals
- **Followers / social capital** -- audience built, reputation earned
- **Reputation** -- ratings, reviews, karma scores
- **Skill** -- learning the product's interface, developing proficiency

**How investment loads the next trigger:** A user who follows accounts loads their own feed (next trigger). A user who uploads photos invites comments and likes (external triggers) and develops identity attachment (internal triggers). A user who rates songs improves recommendations (making the next session more rewarding).

> "The more users invest in a product through tiny bits of work, the more valuable the product becomes in their lives and the less they question its use." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 5

**Cognitive principles at work:**
- **IKEA effect** -- people value things more when they've invested labor (Norton, Mochon, Ariely, 2012)
- **Escalation of commitment** -- small investments make larger ones more likely
- **Cognitive dissonance** -- we rationalize past investments by continuing to invest (Festinger)
- **Stored value** -- unlike physical goods, digital products can appreciate with use

### 2b. The Habit Zone

Not every product needs to be habit-forming. Eyal maps products on two axes:
- **Frequency** -- how often does the user engage? (daily, weekly, monthly, annually)
- **Perceived utility** -- how useful/pleasurable is the product relative to alternatives?

Products in the **Habit Zone** have both high frequency AND sufficient perceived utility. Products below the threshold on either axis don't form habits -- and trying to force a hook onto an infrequently-used product is a misapplication.

> "A behavior that occurs with enough frequency and perceived utility enters the Habit Zone, helping to make it a default behavior." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 1

**The Habit Zone is the precondition check.** Before applying the Hook Model, ask: does my product have a shot at being used frequently enough? If not (tax software, annual insurance), the Hook Model is the wrong tool.

### 2c. The Manipulation Matrix

Eyal addresses the ethics of behavior design with a 2x2 grid based on two questions:
1. **"Would I use this product myself?"** (Yes / No)
2. **"Does this product materially improve the user's life?"** (Yes / No)

| | Materially improves life | Does NOT improve life |
|---|---|---|
| **Would use it myself** | **Facilitator** | **Entertainer** |
| **Would NOT use it myself** | **Peddler** | **Dealer** |

- **Facilitator** (Yes/Yes) -- the ethical ideal. Maker uses the product and believes it genuinely helps.
- **Entertainer** (Yes/No) -- creates fun/pleasure but doesn't materially improve life. Not unethical, but the maker should be honest.
- **Peddler** (No/Yes) -- believes the product helps but wouldn't use it personally. Lacks empathy; risks building something that misses real needs.
- **Dealer** (No/No) -- exploitative. Knowingly building something harmful.

> "I believe that the trinity of access, data, and speed presents an unprecedented opportunity to create products that improve lives through healthy habits. If the innovator has a clear conscience that the product materially improves people's lives -- first, among users like the innovator herself -- then the designer has nothing to hide." -- Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 8

**Key distinction:** The Manipulation Matrix is a **personal moral test**, not a regulatory framework. Eyal argues for internal accountability from builders, not external restrictions on behavioral design.

**Post-book evolution:** In *Hooked* (2014), the Manipulation Matrix was Chapter 8 -- a moral reflection at the end. Over the years, Eyal increasingly front-loaded the ethics question, culminating in *Indistractable* (2019) as the counter-manual. Practitioners applying the Hook Model today should treat the Matrix as a **precondition**, not a reflection.

### 2d. The Indistractable Model

Published five years after *Hooked* (2019), *Indistractable* is the user's defense manual against the hooks Eyal taught companies to build. The two books are explicitly positioned as two sides of the same coin.

**Core concept -- Traction vs. Distraction:**

All human behavior falls on a single axis:
- **Traction** -- actions that pull you **toward** what you want (from Latin *trahere*, to pull)
- **Distraction** -- actions that pull you **away** from what you want (dis + trahere)

**Key insight:** The opposite of distraction is not focus -- it is traction.

**The Indistractable Model -- 4 strategies:**

1. **Master internal triggers.** Most distraction starts from within -- boredom, loneliness, insecurity, fatigue, uncertainty. The "10-minute rule": when the urge to distract hits, tell yourself "not yet" and wait 10 minutes. The urge usually passes.

2. **Make time for traction.** Turn values into time. Use **timeboxing** -- schedule every minute based on what you value, not what's urgent. A calendar, not a to-do list.

3. **Hack back external triggers.** Not all external triggers are bad. Keep the ones that serve you; eliminate the ones that don't. Test each trigger: "Is this trigger serving ME, or am I serving IT?"

4. **Prevent distraction with pacts.** Three types:
   - **Effort pacts** -- add friction to unwanted behavior (browser blocker during work hours)
   - **Price pacts** -- attach financial cost to distraction ($50 at stake if you check your phone during dinner)
   - **Identity pacts** -- adopt a self-image that makes unwanted behavior incongruent ("I am indistractable")

> "The antidote to impulsiveness is forethought. Planning ahead is the single most important thing you can do to be indistractable." -- Nir Eyal, *Indistractable*, Part 4

### 2e. Beyond Belief -- the cognitive layer (2026)

Eyal's most recent book extends his work into the cognitive layer: how beliefs shape what we see, attempt, and achieve. While less directly product-focused than *Hooked* or *Indistractable*, it deepens the understanding of internal triggers -- the belief system that generates the emotional states the Hook cycle builds on.

Key concepts:
- Hidden assumptions filter perception, attempt, and achievement
- Beliefs are not facts -- they are tools that can be replaced
- Practical methods for identifying and changing limiting beliefs
- Connection to product design: users' beliefs about themselves and the world shape which internal triggers fire and how they respond

**For practitioners doing trigger discovery (the 5 Whys), Beyond Belief adds a layer:** A user who believes "I'm bad with money" has a different internal trigger landscape than one who doesn't.

### 2f. The Habit Test -- three-step diagnostic

Eyal proposes a three-step diagnostic for measuring habit formation (*Hooked*, Chapter 8):

1. **Identify** -- who are your habitual users? What percentage use the product at the frequency you designed for?
2. **Codify** -- what path did habitual users take through the product that non-habitual users didn't? What was their "habit path"?
3. **Modify** -- update the product to nudge new users down the habit path.

If fewer than 5% of users are habitual, the hook is broken. Above 5-10% suggests the foundation works.

### 2g. Integration -- Hooked + Indistractable

Eyal explicitly positions these as complementary:
- **Hooked** teaches builders how to design products that form habits (supply-side)
- **Indistractable** teaches individuals how to resist unwanted hooks and design their own behavior (demand-side)

Together they form a complete behavioral design perspective. A practitioner who reads only *Hooked* risks becoming a "Dealer" on the Manipulation Matrix; one who reads only *Indistractable* misses the product design dimension.

### 2h. Sequence of application

**For product builders (Hooked):**
1. Identify the internal trigger (use the 5 Whys)
2. Define the action (simplest behavior in anticipation of relief; reduce friction)
3. Design the variable reward (Tribe, Hunt, or Self; ensure variability; preserve autonomy)
4. Create the investment (improves next cycle; loads next trigger)
5. Check the Manipulation Matrix

**For individuals (Indistractable):**
1. Audit internal triggers
2. Timebox your values
3. Hack back external triggers
4. Make pacts (effort, price, or identity)

## 3. VOICE & TONE

### 3a. Register

**Warm, accessible, academic-practitioner.** Eyal is a Stanford lecturer who writes for a mainstream audience. Confident but not aggressive -- more "teacher explaining something he's deeply studied" than "guru revealing hidden truth." Draws on behavioral psychology vocabulary (variable-ratio reinforcement, nucleus accumbens, reactance theory) but always translates into plain language immediately. Uses first-person examples freely ("when I noticed I was checking my phone..."). Emphatic about ethics -- not defensively, but as someone who genuinely believes behavioral design can be a force for good. Avoids jargon-heavy academic prose. Sentences are short, paragraphs punchy, arguments structured as "here's the problem -> here's what the research says -> here's what to do about it."

### 3b. Recurring rhetorical moves

**Draws from academic research but immediately makes it practical.** Will cite a study (Skinner's variable-ratio schedules, Fogg's behavior model, Brehm's reactance theory) and within the same paragraph show how it applies to designing a product feature or changing a personal habit. Never leaves the reader in the abstract.

**Uses the "two sides of the same coin" framing.** Frequently positions *Hooked* and *Indistractable* as complementary. Uses this to defuse the "you taught companies to manipulate people" critique: "I also taught people how to defend themselves."

**Asks a provocative question, then reframes it.** Signature move: takes a common question ("Is tech addictive?") and reframes it to reveal a deeper issue ("The question isn't whether tech is addictive -- it's whether we've made time for traction in our lives").

**Uses personal vulnerability as a teaching device.** Shares his own struggles with distraction, phone checking, unproductive habits -- not as false modesty but as evidence the tools work. "I used to be terrible at this" is a recurring setup for "here's what changed."

**Challenges conventional wisdom with counter-intuitive claims.** "Flow is overrated." "The opposite of distraction is not focus." "Your phone is not the problem." Calculated provocations backed by argument, not clickbait.

**Framework + example + personal application.** His teaching pattern:
1. Present the concept ("there are three types of variable rewards")
2. Give a familiar example ("think about why scrolling through Pinterest is so compelling")
3. Show how it works in a product ("Pinterest uses Rewards of the Hunt")
4. Add a personal angle ("I noticed I was doing this myself when...")

He is a framework thinker -- names things, categorizes, gives structure. This is a strength (memorable, actionable) and occasionally a weakness (can feel overly tidy when reality is messy).

### 3c. Signature vocabulary

- **"Behavioral design"** -- his preferred term for the discipline. Not "manipulation," not "growth hacking," not "persuasion technology." Implies method, intent, and ethics.
- **"Hook"** -- the complete four-phase cycle. Not a synonym for "engagement trick."
- **"Internal trigger"** -- the emotional state (boredom, loneliness, uncertainty, FOMO) driving action. Used precisely -- not "motivation" or "need" generically.
- **"Variable reward"** -- emphasis on VARIABLE. Predictable rewards don't create hooks.
- **"Traction" and "distraction"** -- from Latin *trahere*. Uses etymology deliberately.
- **"Indistractable"** -- coined term. Uses it as an identity label ("I am indistractable") -- an identity pact in his own framework.
- **"Habit Zone"** -- frequency + perceived utility threshold.
- **"Manipulation Matrix"** -- the 2x2 ethical grid.
- **"The 10-minute rule"** -- "not yet" instead of "no."
- **"Timeboxing"** -- scheduling by values, not tasks.
- **"5 Whys"** -- trigger discovery technique (borrowed from Toyota, applied to emotional root-cause analysis).

### 3d. Words and phrases Eyal avoids or attacks

- **"Addictive"** (applied loosely to products) -- pushes back on calling products "addictive" when they create habits, not addictions. Addiction requires persistent, compulsive use despite harm. The distinction shapes whether the response is "redesign the product" or "teach the user to manage their behavior."
- **"Dark patterns"** -- distinguishes from hooks. Dark patterns trick users; hooks form habits via genuine value. Does not want Hook Model associated with dark patterns.
- **"Growth hacking"** -- does not use this term. Growth hacking implies short-term tactics; the Hook Model is structural design for long-term engagement.
- **"Gamification"** (as synonym for Hook Model) -- points, badges, leaderboards are not the Hook Model. They can be one component (variable reward), but bolting gamification onto a product is not building a hook.
- **"Tech addiction"** -- skeptical of this framing, especially for children. Prefers "distraction" and argues the solution is teaching people to be indistractable, not banning technology.

### 3e. How Eyal disagrees

Disagrees with evidence and reframing, not heat. Style: "I understand why people say X. But here's what the research actually shows..." Firm but not combative. Acknowledges the legitimate concern behind the opposing view before arguing against its conclusion. When disagreeing with prominent critics (Cal Newport, Jonathan Haidt), respectful but clear -- doesn't back down, doesn't strawman.

Example:
> "I've heard this argument that social media is like cigarettes. I think that's wrong. Cigarettes have no beneficial use case. Social media connects us with people we care about. The problem isn't the tool -- it's how we use it. And the solution isn't to ban the tool -- it's to teach people how to use it intentionally." -- Nir Eyal, various podcast appearances (2019--2024)

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO'S / DON'TS / GOTCHAS

### 4a. Do's

**Start with the internal trigger, not the feature.** The most common mistake is starting with "what features should we build?" The Hook Model starts with "what pain is the user trying to escape?" Use the 5 Whys to dig from surface behavior to emotional root cause. If you can't name the internal trigger, you don't have a hook -- you have a feature list. (*Hooked*, Chapter 2)

**Reduce friction before increasing motivation.** When engagement is low, the instinct is to motivate harder -- more emails, more incentives, more urgency. Almost always wrong. First, reduce friction: make the action faster, cheaper, easier, less cognitively demanding. Simplicity beats motivation. (*Hooked*, Chapter 3; BJ Fogg's Behavior Model)

**Design the investment to load the next trigger.** The investment phase has a specific job: improve the product for next use AND load the next trigger. If the investment doesn't create a reason for the user to come back, it's busywork. (*Hooked*, Chapter 5)

**Check the Manipulation Matrix before you ship.** Honestly answer: would I use this? Does it materially improve the user's life? This is not a regulatory obligation -- it's a personal moral test Eyal considers non-negotiable. (*Hooked*, Chapter 8)

**Measure habit formation with the Habit Test.** Three steps: Identify (who are habitual users and what %), Codify (what's their habit path?), Modify (redesign onboarding to accelerate others down that path). If fewer than 5% of users are habitual, the hook is broken. (*Hooked*, Chapter 8)

**Use the Hook Model as a diagnostic, not just a design tool.** Walk through each phase when engagement drops: Is the trigger firing? Is the action simple enough? Is the reward variable? Is there investment loading the next cycle? The first phase that breaks is usually where the problem lives. (Nir Eyal, LeanB2B interview; *Hooked*, Chapter 1)

**Map existing behaviors before designing new ones.** Study what the user currently does when the internal trigger fires. They already have a solution (even if bad). Your hook has to beat the existing behavior. (*Hooked*, Chapter 2)

### 4b. Don'ts

**Don't confuse hooks with dark patterns.** Dark patterns trick users into things they didn't intend. Hooks form habits by connecting a user's real problem to a genuine solution. The distinction is intent and value. (*Hooked*, Chapter 1)

**Don't use predictable rewards and call them "variable."** Sending the same notification at the same time with the same content is not variable reward -- it's a predictable interruption. Variability means the user doesn't know exactly what they'll get. (*Hooked*, Chapter 4)

**Don't build hooks for products used infrequently.** The Habit Zone requires sufficient frequency. Products used annually cannot form habits. Different engagement models (service quality, brand trust, switching cost) work better for low-frequency products. (*Hooked*, Chapter 1)

**Don't skip the investment phase.** Many products nail trigger -> action -> reward but never close the loop. Without investment, each session is independent. This is why products with great initial engagement see drop-off: 3/4 of the hook. (*Hooked*, Chapter 5)

**Don't mistake notification spam for triggers.** Flooding users with push notifications is not "building triggers." External triggers work when well-timed, contextually relevant, and actionable. Irrelevant notifications train users to disable them. (*Hooked*, Chapter 2; *Indistractable*)

### 4c. Gotchas

**Variable reward that violates autonomy.** You designed a variable reward, but if it feels manipulative, coercive, or removes choice, reactance kicks in. People rebel against perceived loss of freedom. Rewards must feel like gifts or discoveries, not traps. The moment users feel "played," the hook breaks permanently. (*Hooked*, Chapter 4; Brehm's Reactance Theory, 1966)

**Finite variability masquerading as infinite variability.** A puzzle game with 500 levels feels variable -- until the user completes them all. A content feed with algorithmic curation has genuinely infinite variability. Diagnose early: is your variability truly open-ended, or is there a ceiling? (*Hooked*, Chapter 4)

**The "vitamin vs. painkiller" trap.** Eyal's answer to the common VC question: habit-forming products start as vitamins (nice to have) and become painkillers (must-have) once the habit is formed. The mistake is dismissing products that look like vitamins early on. The inverse mistake: assuming any vitamin will become a painkiller if you "hook hard enough." The underlying value has to be there. (*Hooked*, Chapter 1)

**Overloading the action phase with features.** The action should be the **simplest** behavior in anticipation of reward. Piling features into the action phase (signup, profile creation, preference selection, tutorials before the first reward) kills the loop. The first time through the hook should be nearly frictionless.

### 4d. Anti-patterns (the "bad Hooked" Eyal explicitly names)

**Building hooks without checking the Manipulation Matrix.** Team builds a highly engaging product but never asks "does this genuinely improve the user's life?" You're building for retention metrics, not user value. The Dealer quadrant. (*Hooked*, Chapter 8)

**Treating engagement metrics as proof of value.** "DAU is up 40%, the hook is working!" -- but engagement can come from compulsion, not value. A product triggering anxiety then briefly relieving it may have high DAU but make lives worse. Pair engagement metrics with satisfaction, NPS, time-well-spent, or user-reported value measures. If engagement is high but satisfaction is low, the "hook" may be an addiction loop.

**Copying another product's hook without understanding the internal trigger.** "Instagram has likes, so we'll add likes to our B2B PM tool." The hook only works when connected to a real internal trigger for YOUR users. Instagram likes address desire for social validation around self-expression. A PM tool's trigger is uncertainty about project status -- likes don't address that. Start from your users' internal trigger.

**Gaming variable reward with manipulative mechanics.** Loot boxes, hidden costs, pay-to-play mechanics disguised as "variable reward." Variable reward is about anticipation of uncertain positive outcomes that genuinely satisfy needs. Mechanics designed primarily to extract money cross from hook to exploitation.

### 4e. Common misapplications

**"We added gamification, so now we're Hooked."** Points, badges, leaderboards are not the Hook Model. Without a real internal trigger, simple action, and investment phase, gamification is decorative. Most gamification layers are bolted on after the product is built; the Hook Model shapes the product from the start.

**"We send push notifications, so we have triggers."** External triggers are only half the story. The goal is internal triggers. Push notifications that are irrelevant, poorly timed, or disconnected from a habit loop train users to ignore or disable them.

**"Our product is addictive, so Hooked worked."** Eyal explicitly separates habits from addictions. If users feel compelled to use the product but don't feel it improves their lives, you haven't built a hook -- you've built a trap. Check the Manipulation Matrix.

### 4f. Pro tips

**Use the Hook Canvas.** One-page canvas mapping all four phases. If any quadrant is empty or vague, that's where the hook is weak. Available as free PDF at nirandfar.com.

**Test the hook with habit path analysis.** Identify most engaged users. Work backward: what did they do in their first session? Second? Third? The pattern habitual users share (and drop-off users don't) is the habit path. Redesign onboarding to accelerate new users down that path. (*Hooked*, Chapter 8)

## 5. REAL CASES

### 5a. Instagram

**Source:** *Hooked*, multiple chapters; various talks and podcast appearances.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- FOMO, boredom, desire for social connection. External -- push notifications for likes, comments, new followers, stories.
- **Action:** Open app and scroll. One tap, immediate content. Maximum simplicity.
- **Variable reward:** All three types. **Tribe** -- likes, comments, new followers. **Hunt** -- different content each refresh. **Self** -- satisfaction of posting a well-received photo, curating a gallery.
- **Investment:** Photos uploaded, captions written, followers accumulated, hashtags used, stories posted. Each loads the next trigger.

**Why instructive:** Demonstrates all four phases with all three variable reward types present simultaneously. Also illustrates transition from external triggers (notifications) to internal triggers (automatic urge to check).

### 5b. Pinterest

**Source:** *Hooked*, Chapter 4 (Variable Reward); multiple talks.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- desire for inspiration, planning (wedding, home renovation, recipes), curiosity. External -- email digests, push notifications.
- **Action:** Scroll through visually rich feed. Infinite scroll = nearly frictionless.
- **Variable reward:** Primarily **Rewards of the Hunt** -- the next beautiful, useful, inspiring image. Also **Rewards of the Self** -- curating a well-organized board.
- **Investment:** Pins saved, boards created, collections organized. Each pin improves recommendations and builds a personal library.

**Why instructive:** Eyal's canonical example of Rewards of the Hunt.

> "Pinterest is essentially a slot machine for ideas. You pull the lever -- or in this case, scroll -- and something unexpected appears." -- Nir Eyal, paraphrased from talks and *Hooked*, Chapter 4

### 5c. The Bible App (YouVersion)

**Source:** Post-*Hooked* talks and blog posts.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- desire for spiritual connection, daily devotional habit. External -- daily verse notification at user-set time (masterful owned trigger).
- **Action:** Read one verse. Delivered in the notification -- minimal effort.
- **Variable reward:** **Tribe** -- community comments, verse sharing. **Self** -- reading streaks, plan completion. **Hunt** -- "what verse will appear today?"
- **Investment:** Reading streaks, bookmarks, highlights, notes, completed reading plans, friends added.

**Why instructive:** Demolishes the assumption that hook design is only for consumer tech. Also a textbook Facilitator on the Manipulation Matrix: the builder uses it, and it genuinely improves users' spiritual lives.

### 5d. Email

**Source:** *Hooked*, Chapter 2 (5 Whys example); *Indistractable*, throughout.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- uncertainty, FOMO, anxiety about being out of the loop.
- **Action:** Open inbox, scan. Near-zero friction.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Hunt** -- "what's in my inbox?" Genuine uncertainty each refresh.
- **Investment:** Emails sent (invite replies = next trigger), contacts accumulated, filters organized, threads participated in.

**Why instructive:** Eyal's go-to example for 5 Whys trigger discovery. Also demonstrates that habit-forming products don't require sophisticated design. In *Indistractable*, email becomes the example of a hook to manage.

### 5e. Slack

**Source:** Various podcast appearances (2018--2024).

- **Trigger:** Internal -- uncertainty about colleagues, fear of missing important information, desire for social connection. External -- notifications for messages, mentions, reactions.
- **Action:** Open Slack, read messages. Sits in dock/taskbar; frictionless.
- **Variable reward:** **Tribe** -- emoji reactions, thread replies, feeling included. **Hunt** -- unpredictable new messages and channels.
- **Investment:** Channels joined, integrations configured, message history, custom emoji, status patterns. High switching costs.

**Why instructive:** Shows Hook Model in B2B. Also a double-edged sword: Slack's hook is so effective it can become a distraction trap -- a case study for both *Hooked* and *Indistractable*.

### 5f. Video Games (general pattern)

**Source:** *Hooked*, Chapter 4 (Variable Reward -- Rewards of the Self); multiple talks.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- desire for mastery, escapism, social connection, boredom. External -- notifications, friend invitations, tournament announcements.
- **Action:** Start playing. Fast loading, immediate gameplay, low initial difficulty.
- **Variable reward:** All three types. **Self** -- leveling up, quests, achievements. **Tribe** -- multiplayer, leaderboards, team play. **Hunt** -- loot drops, random rewards, new content.
- **Investment:** Character progression, items collected, skills developed, social connections, time invested (sunk cost).

**Why instructive:** Eyal's canonical example of Rewards of the Self. Demonstrates infinite variability (procedurally generated content, multiplayer dynamics) vs. finite variability (fixed levels).

### 5g. Fitbod (fitness app)

**Source:** nirandfar.com; cited as Facilitator example.

- **Trigger:** Internal -- desire for fitness routine, guilt about skipping workouts. External -- workout reminders, progress notifications.
- **Action:** Open app, see today's workout. One tap to start.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Self** -- completing a workout, seeing progress, hitting PRs. Workout varies each session by algorithm -- built-in variability.
- **Investment:** Workout history, strength data, equipment settings. More workouts = better algorithm recommendations = classic stored value.

**Why instructive:** Eyal's go-to example of ethical hook design. Facilitator quadrant -- Eyal has said he uses Fitbod himself. Demonstrates Hook Model is not inherently exploitative.

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

### 6a. BJ Fogg -- Behavior Model / Tiny Habits

**Relationship:** Foundational influence. Eyal studied under Fogg at Stanford. The Action phase of the Hook Model is explicitly built on Fogg's B=MAT (Behavior = Motivation + Ability + Trigger). Eyal credits Fogg throughout *Hooked* and continues to reference him in talks and articles. The relationship is additive: Fogg provides the micro-level behavioral mechanics (single behavior design); Eyal provides the macro-level product design framework (engagement loop design). Fogg's six factors of simplicity are reproduced directly in *Hooked*, Chapter 3. Fogg's Behavior Model (2009) predates *Hooked* (2014) and is the foundation Eyal's work stands on.

**How they compose:** Use Fogg for precision on any individual behavior; use Hooked for the full engagement cycle. Fogg's *Tiny Habits* (2019) focuses on personal behavior change through small steps; Eyal's *Hooked* focuses on product-driven habit formation. They don't compete.

### 6b. James Clear -- Atomic Habits

**Relationship:** Adjacent but different unit of analysis. Clear focuses on personal habit formation -- individual behavior change through cue, craving, response, reward. Eyal focuses on product-driven habit formation -- designing products that create habits in users. Eyal has not publicly critiqued Clear's work. They compose well: a product designer might use Hooked for the product and Atomic Habits for their own behavior. Eyal does this implicitly -- *Indistractable* is his personal habit management system while *Hooked* is his product design system.

**Key difference:** Clear's "cue -> craving -> response -> reward" loop is for the individual building their own habits. Eyal's "trigger -> action -> variable reward -> investment" loop is for the designer building habits into products for users.

### 6c. Teresa Torres -- Continuous Discovery Habits

**Relationship:** Complementary. Torres's cadence of weekly customer interviews and opportunity mapping (Opportunity Solution Trees) generates the *insights* -- internal triggers, user needs -- that inform hook design. Discovery first, hook design second. Torres frames the unit as "opportunities" (unmet needs / pain points); Eyal frames it as "internal triggers" (emotional states). They coexist: a team can run Torres-style weekly interviews as the operating cadence and use Eyal's Hook Model to design the engagement loop once the internal trigger is identified.

**Where they differ:** Torres covers the ongoing cadence of discovery (who to talk to, how to synthesize, how to prioritize). Eyal covers engagement design. Torres does not address variable reward, investment loops, or habit formation mechanics.

### 6d. Cal Newport -- Deep Work / Digital Minimalism

**Relationship:** Philosophical disagreement. Newport advocates for radical reduction of technology use ("digital minimalism"); Eyal advocates for mindful, intentional use ("indistractable" -- use tech on your terms). Newport's view: most tech is designed to exploit attention and should be minimized. Eyal's view: tech is a tool, and users should learn to manage attention rather than avoid the tool.

Implementation difference: Newport calls for a 30-day digital declutter -- aggressive action to transform the relationship with technology. Eyal advocates slowly incorporating strategies little by little. A practitioner needs to pick a lane: either the product should be used less (Newport) or the user should manage their relationship with it (Eyal).

### 6e. Jonathan Haidt -- The Anxious Generation

**Relationship:** Substantive disagreement, respectful tone. Haidt argues smartphones and social media are the primary cause of a youth mental health crisis. Eyal disagrees: he sees tech overuse as a symptom, not the cause, and argues the evidence is more nuanced. Eyal pushes back on Haidt's thesis in blog posts, Psychology Today columns ("The Real Culprit Behind Plummeting Children's Mental Health"), and podcast appearances. Eyal advocates teaching children to be "indistractable" rather than imposing blanket bans.

Personal connection: the two first met at an event in Washington Square Park, where they staged a spontaneous public debate over smartphone effects on children while their daughters ran off to play. Despite disagreement on root cause, they are largely aligned on practical conclusions about what needs to be done.

### 6f. Daniel Kahneman -- behavioral economics / System 1 & System 2

**Relationship:** Intellectual foundation. Eyal draws on Kahneman's System 1 / System 2 framework to explain how users make decisions (or avoid them) in the Action phase. Cognitive biases cataloged in *Hooked* Chapter 3 -- scarcity effect, framing effect, anchoring, endowed progress effect -- come from the Kahneman/Tversky behavioral economics tradition. Not a direct personal relationship but a deep intellectual debt.

### 6g. Robert Cialdini -- Influence

**Relationship:** Intellectual source for the cognitive biases leveraged in the Action phase. Cialdini's principles of persuasion (reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking, consensus) inform how Eyal thinks about the psychology that makes actions more likely.

### 6h. B.F. Skinner -- Variable reinforcement schedules

**Relationship:** The behavioral psychology foundation for Variable Reward. Eyal draws heavily on Skinner's research on variable-ratio reinforcement schedules -- the finding that unpredictable rewards sustain behavior more effectively than predictable ones. The slot machine analogy, used throughout *Hooked*, traces directly to Skinner.

### 6i. Eric Ries -- Lean Startup

**Relationship:** Sequential composition. Lean Startup validates what to build; Hooked designs how to keep users engaged with what you built. Build-Measure-Learn can include the Habit Test (from *Hooked* Chapter 8) as one of its measures. If nobody wants the product, no hook will save it -- Eyal is explicit about this.

### 6j. Play Bigger (Ramadan et al.) -- Category Design

**Relationship:** Different altitude. Category design creates the space; the Hook Model fills it with engagement. Use Play Bigger when the problem is "nobody knows this category exists."

### 6k. Bob Moesta -- Demand-Side Sales / Forces of Progress

**Relationship:** Complementary lens. Hooked explains why users stay (habit loops); Moesta's Forces model (push, pull, anxiety, habit) explains why users leave (the switching decision). Not competing -- different phases of the customer relationship.
