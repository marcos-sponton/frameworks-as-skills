## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

Goldratt's Theory of Constraints has three interlocking layers. Any faithful application of TOC uses all three; teams that reduce it to "find the bottleneck" have taken one slice of one layer.

### Layer A — The system's Goal, and the three measurements (the accounting reframe)

Before you optimize anything, you name **the Goal**. In *The Goal*, Jonah forces Alex to state it: for a for-profit business, the Goal is **to make money — now and in the future**.

Every operational metric must be checked against the Goal. Goldratt's reframe of accounting — **Throughput Accounting** — replaces cost accounting with three measurements:

- **Throughput (T)** — "the rate at which the system generates money through sales." Not production. Not shipped units. *Sales*. A finished good sitting in the warehouse has zero throughput.
- **Inventory (I)** — "all the money the system has invested in purchasing things it intends to sell." Includes raw materials, work-in-process, finished goods, equipment, facilities — everything the system bought.
- **Operating Expense (OE)** — "all the money the system spends turning Inventory into Throughput." Labor, overhead, utilities.

**The imperative:** simultaneously **increase Throughput, decrease Inventory, decrease Operating Expense.** The ranking is intentional — Throughput is unbounded; Inventory and OE have floors. Traditional cost accounting inverts the priority and destroys signal.

Two verbatim Jonah lines from *The Goal* that anchor this:
> "So this is the goal: To make money by increasing net profit, while simultaneously increasing return on investment, and simultaneously increasing cash flow."
> "Productivity is the act of bringing a company closer to its goal. Every action that brings a company closer to its goal is productive."

### Layer B — The Five Focusing Steps (POOGI: Process of Ongoing Improvement)

The operational engine of TOC. From the Wikipedia canonical wording:

1. **Identify** the system's constraint(s).
2. Decide how to **exploit** the system's constraint(s).
3. **Subordinate** everything else to the above decision.
4. **Elevate** the system's constraint(s).
5. **Warning!** If in the previous steps a constraint has been broken, go back to step 1 — but **do not allow inertia to cause a system's constraint.**

Notes on each:
- **Identify** — the constraint is where demand meets/exceeds capacity. Not the loudest problem; the actual bottleneck. Often internal (a machine, a person, a team); sometimes market (demand is the constraint); sometimes policy (a rule the org made up that limits flow).
- **Exploit** — before you buy anything, make sure the constraint is *never* idle. No lunch break at the bottleneck. No setup during peak. No quality-defective inputs reaching it. This step is 100% free.
- **Subordinate** — every non-constraint must operate at the pace of the constraint, not at its own local optimum. This is the counterintuitive core: **non-constraints running at full efficiency create inventory in front of the constraint and starvation behind it.** Running a non-constraint at 100% is a bug.
- **Elevate** — only after you've fully exploited and subordinated do you spend money to lift the constraint (add capacity, buy equipment, hire).
- **Repeat + inertia warning** — the moment you elevate one constraint, another becomes the constraint. If the org's rules are still shaped around the *old* constraint, you'll have policy constraints paralyzing new flow. Inertia is the enemy.

### Layer C — The Thinking Processes (how to change what you can't just re-engineer)

For situations where the constraint is a policy, a belief, or a conflict, not a machine. Five logical tools using two logic types: **Sufficient Cause** (if X then Y) for the trees; **Necessary Condition** (in order to X we must have Y) for the Cloud. All five answer three questions in sequence:

**What to change?**
- **Current Reality Tree (CRT)** — starts from a list of Undesirable Effects (UDEs), works backward via cause-and-effect until it converges on the small number (usually 1–3) of **root causes**. Solves the "what is the actual problem" question that groups usually fail at because they treat symptoms.

**What to change to?**
- **Evaporating Cloud (EC / Conflict Cloud)** — a five-box necessary-condition diagram used to dissolve a conflict rather than compromise. The two apparent options both serve underlying needs; both needs serve a common objective. The dissolution comes from surfacing the assumption that says the needs conflict, and invalidating it. Goldratt believed most stuck situations are stuck at a Cloud.
- **Future Reality Tree (FRT)** — takes the proposed change (the "injection") and traces cause-and-effect forward to show the desired future state. Also flags **Negative Branches** — bad side effects the change would create — so they can be pruned before implementation.

**How to cause the change?**
- **Prerequisite Tree (PRT)** — maps the obstacles that stand between now and the desired state, and defines the intermediate objectives that would overcome each. This is where org-buy-in gets structured.
- **Transition Tree (TRT)** — the ordered action plan, with the specific need + action + result at each step.

Efrat Goldratt-Ashlag's *The Choice* is essentially a father-daughter conversation about the mental model layer that sits under all five Thinking Processes — the belief that people are good, that every conflict can be removed, that every situation is exceedingly simple.

### Layer B applied to production — Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR)

TOC's manufacturing scheduling method. Three roles:
- **Drum** — the pace of the constraint. Nothing in the plant runs faster than this. The drum is the schedule.
- **Buffer** — a *time* buffer (not an inventory pile) in front of the constraint, protecting it from upstream disruption. Buffers are managed by color: Red = at risk of starving the constraint (act now); Yellow = watch; Green = safe.
- **Rope** — the mechanism that releases raw material into the plant only *when the drum needs it*. Not before. The rope pulls; the material doesn't push.

DBR keeps only three points instrumented (the drum, the buffer origin, the release gate) instead of every workstation. Simpler than Lean's kanban and MRP's push. Simplified DBR (S-DBR) reduces this further for less-complex flows.

### Layer B applied to projects — Critical Chain Project Management (CCPM)

TOC's project methodology, introduced in *Critical Chain* (1997). Key differences from Critical Path Method:

- **Task estimates are cut** — CPM estimates include hidden safety (padding to protect the task); CCPM cuts each estimate roughly in half and moves the aggregated safety into shared **buffers**.
- **The Critical Chain** — the longest sequence of *resource-constrained* dependent tasks (not the longest sequence of dependencies alone). Resource contention is a first-class concern.
- **Three buffer types:**
  - **Project Buffer** — a single time buffer at the end of the critical chain, protecting the project completion date.
  - **Feeding Buffers** — placed where non-critical paths merge into the critical chain, protecting the critical chain from being delayed by feeders.
  - **Resource Buffers** — advance-warning signals that ensure a resource is ready when the critical chain needs it.
- **Buffer management** — the project's health is read as buffer consumption vs. progress on the chain. If the chain is 50% done and the buffer is 80% consumed, alarm.

Goldratt's diagnosis of why CPM projects fail without CCPM:
- **Student Syndrome** — people don't start until the deadline is close; safety in the estimate is used up before real work begins.
- **Parkinson's Law** — work expands to fill the time available; even when a task finishes early, that early finish is not passed forward.
- **Multitasking** — the productivity killer. A resource split across three parallel tasks finishes all three later than doing them sequentially.

## 3. VOICE & TONE

**Two distinct voices to preserve — do not merge them.**

### The Jonah voice (dominant in the novels)

The Socratic teacher: never gives answers, only questions. Reframes the problem so the manager realizes the answer themselves. Deliberately withholding. Warm, patient, precise, willing to let the student sit in confusion. When Alex Rogo begs Jonah for solutions, Jonah insists on the sequence — first understand the goal, then measure correctly, then find the constraint.

Register: conversational, plain-language, allergic to jargon. Uses everyday analogies (the Boy Scout hike as production line, dice game as statistical fluctuation).

Rhetorical moves:
- **"What is the goal?"** — the universal opening question. Refuses to discuss any tactic before the Goal is named.
- **"Tell me how you measure me and I'll tell you how I'll behave"** — Goldratt's most-quoted line. The measurement is the leverage; the behavior is downstream.
- **Return the question** — Jonah almost never validates a claim; he asks the manager to defend it.
- **Show the counter-example before naming the principle** — the dice game shows that statistical fluctuations plus dependent events destroy any balanced line, *before* Alex is told the principle.

### The Goldratt-writing-non-fiction voice

Denser. Impatient with orthodoxy. Willing to insult (calls conventional cost accounting "the No. 1 enemy of productivity"). Physicist's contempt for imprecise language. Uses simple math and simple examples deliberately — "physics envy" turned into pedagogic clarity.

Register: assertive, near-polemical when attacking cost accounting or MRP II. Warmer when talking about people ("people are good" is his stated axiom).

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**The overriding principle:** in any system, the throughput of the whole is determined by the throughput of exactly one constraint. Local optimization anywhere else destroys global throughput. This is not a management preference; it's the arithmetic of the system.

### Do's

**Name the Goal before naming a metric.** Every metric you propose has to be tested against "does this move us toward the Goal?" If you can't answer, the metric is measuring the wrong thing. This is the Jonah opening move; imitate it.

**Instrument the constraint, not the line.** DBR needs three instrumented points. Kanban systems instrument every workstation. Fewer instrumented points = clearer signal.

**Buffer time, not inventory.** Buffers protect the constraint from upstream variability. Inventory piled in front of the constraint is not a buffer; it's a symptom of failing to subordinate upstream.

**Cut the padded estimate and pool the safety.** CCPM's core move — take each task estimate, cut it roughly in half, pool the aggregated safety into a project buffer. Individual overruns are absorbed by the shared buffer; the project completes faster than the sum of the safe estimates.

**Attack multitasking before adding capacity.** The single-highest-leverage move in most CCPM implementations. A resource doing three things "in parallel" finishes all three later than doing them sequentially. Sequence the work.

**When the constraint moves, question every rule.** After elevating, the org still runs the old rules. This is where TOC implementations die — the visible constraint moves but the *policy* constraint (the shaped-around-the-old-bottleneck rule) becomes the new limit.

**Use the Cloud on stuck disagreements.** Compromise loses; dissolution wins. Every stuck conflict rests on an assumption. Find it. Invalidate it. Both sides now agree without either giving up their need.

### Don'ts

**Don't try to keep every resource busy.** "A worker sitting idle" is only a problem if that worker is the constraint. Non-constraint workers *should* have idle time. Otherwise they're producing inventory in front of the constraint.

**Don't measure productivity as units produced.** Units in the warehouse are inventory, not throughput. Throughput requires the sale. This is the mistake that made UniCo look profitable while going bankrupt in *The Goal*.

**Don't push work into the plant early.** The Rope releases material only when the Drum needs it. Pushing early creates WIP that hides the actual constraint and delays true signals.

**Don't skip the Thinking Processes for a policy constraint.** If the bottleneck is a rule, buying a machine won't fix it. If the bottleneck is a conflict, adding staff won't fix it. Match the tool to the type of constraint.

**Don't run cost accounting alongside Throughput Accounting for the same decision.** The two answer opposite questions. Product-cost thinking will always resist obvious Throughput moves (like accepting an "unprofitable" order that fills constraint time and generates margin).

**Don't confuse activation with utilization.** A resource can be activated (running, doing something) without being utilized (moving the system toward the Goal). Activation feels productive; utilization actually is.

**Don't treat "the market" as the constraint too fast.** When capacity is idle, managers reflexively say "we need more sales." Sometimes true; often the internal constraint is a policy that keeps sales *unable* to be closed (long lead times, inflexible offers, bad terms). Test both.

**Don't sell DBR to a software team as-is.** Software delivery is closer to a project pipeline than a manufacturing line. Reach for CCPM + TOC-adapted flow (see Ching, Tendon, Kim) rather than force-fitting DBR.

### Gotchas — the classic misreads

- **"TOC = find the bottleneck" (the most common gutting).** TOC is Goal + Measurements + Five Steps + DBR/CCPM + Thinking Processes. The one-line summary loses 90% of the method.
- **Confusing constraint with problem.** Every organization has 100 problems; it has (approximately) one constraint. The constraint is the one whose relief moves the system forward. The rest are noise until the constraint is handled.
- **The elevated constraint that no one notices moved.** Elevated + old-rules-still-running = the classic stall. Every elevation triggers a rule audit.
- **Assuming the constraint is inside the plant.** It might be a customer, a supplier, a regulator, a policy of your own making. Look outside the walls.
- **Statistical fluctuations misunderstood.** A "balanced line" (every station rated for equal capacity) doesn't stabilize; it stalls, because normal statistical variation stacks with dependent events. Goldratt's dice-game chapter is the entire proof. Any manager arguing for perfect line balance hasn't understood the dice game.
- **Efficiency reporting as management theatre.** Reporting 98% utilization of non-constraint resources feels like management doing its job. It is producing inventory the constraint can't process, hiding the real problem, and adding OE. Efficiency-everywhere is a bug.

## 7. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

### vs. Lean / Toyota Production System
- **Common ground:** both attack local optima; both focus on flow through the system; both agree "activity ≠ productivity."
- **Divergence #1 — how you attack:** Lean attacks *waste* everywhere in the system, continuously, incrementally. TOC attacks *the constraint*, and treats waste elsewhere as second-order. Lean is horizontal; TOC is vertical.
- **Divergence #2 — pull vs. drum:** Lean pulls with kanban at every station. TOC releases with a rope from a central drum. Lean instruments every workstation; TOC instruments the constraint and its buffers.
- **Divergence #3 — balance vs. unbalance:** Lean pursues line balance. TOC argues balance is impossible (statistical fluctuations + dependent events) and pursues *deliberate imbalance* around the drum, with buffers absorbing variability.
- **Divergence #4 — speed to result:** TOC advocates argue TOC produces bottom-line results faster because effort concentrates on the one place that moves the number. Lean advocates argue Lean produces more durable results because it changes the whole system's habits.
- **Composability:** many practitioners combine them. Marris Consulting explicitly promotes "TOC boosting Lean" — use TOC to prioritize which Lean improvements to sequence first.

### vs. Six Sigma
- Six Sigma reduces variation as its primary objective. TOC accepts variation as inherent and buffers against it. Not opposed; different primary objectives. Six Sigma at the constraint is high-leverage; Six Sigma at a non-constraint is a waste of Sigma-project money by TOC lights.

### vs. DORA / Accelerate (see [[dora-accelerate]])
- **DORA measures are TOC measures for software delivery.** Deployment Frequency + Lead Time = Throughput (in software terms). Change Failure Rate + Recovery Time = Stability of the flow. The four DORA keys are the software-delivery restatement of Goldratt's *T* — sales become deploys, throughput becomes shipped value.
- Forsgren's insight that "speed and stability are not a tradeoff" is Goldratt's insight that "increasing Throughput, decreasing Inventory, decreasing OE" go together — from the same set of capabilities. Both push back against the "we're slowing down to be safer" misdiagnosis.
- The 30+ DORA capabilities are, in TOC vocabulary, the levers you use in Steps 2–4 (exploit / subordinate / elevate) once you've identified the delivery-pipeline constraint.
- *The Phoenix Project* is the explicit bridge — Kim/Behr/Spafford deliberately wrote it as *The Goal* for IT. Erik Reid quotes Jonah's questions. The Three Ways are the DevOps restatement of POOGI.

### vs. Lean Startup (Ries)
- Ries's Build-Measure-Learn is a Thinking-Processes-shaped loop for product discovery: identify what to change (validated learning replaces CRT), decide what to change to (pivot as injection replaces FRT/PRT), cause the change (MVP + measurement replaces TRT). Ries acknowledges the influence of both Lean and TOC directly.

### vs. Kanban (David J. Anderson)
- Kanban's WIP limits are TOC's subordination (Step 3) applied to knowledge work: cap the WIP so the constraint is protected from being flooded. Anderson has written explicitly about the TOC lineage; Kanban is TOC-compatible.

### vs. Systems Thinking (Senge)
- Both are systemic; TOC is more prescriptive. Senge gives archetypes; Goldratt gives a specific method (Five Focusing Steps + Thinking Processes). Complementary — Senge for framing why systems misbehave, Goldratt for the operating manual to fix them.

### vs. Rumelt's Kernel of Strategy (see [[good-strategy-bad-strategy]])
- Rumelt's "the crux" is TOC's constraint at strategy altitude — the single pivotal challenge on which the whole strategy turns. Different domain (strategy vs. operations), same underlying claim about leverage: one thing matters far more than others, and pretending it doesn't is what makes strategy bad.

### vs. OKRs / Playing to Win / Rockefeller
- These frameworks answer "what do we do?" (goals, aspirations, cascades). TOC answers "why does the system produce what it currently produces, and where's the one lever?" TOC precedes goal-setting — set OKRs after you know the constraint, not before, or the goals will be pulled toward non-constraints and produce local optimization.
