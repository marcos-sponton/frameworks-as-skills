## 2. POST-BOOK MATERIAL (2019 → 2026)

**The book (2019)** — *Shape Up: Stop Running in Circles and Ship Work that Matters*, self-published by 37signals; freely readable at https://basecamp.com/shapeup. This is the primary text. Everything post-book refines and adapts it rather than replaces it.

**The single biggest post-book refinement — Framing.** The 2019 book leaps from "raw idea" straight into shaping. In practice this created "underdone shaping" and rework: teams shape solutions to problems the business hadn't actually agreed were worth solving. Singer's 2022 article *Framing* introduces the missing upstream step:

- **Framing** — is this problem worth solving? Whose problem? What's the business value? — answered before any solution work begins.
- **Shaping** — given that a framed problem is real and worth solving, what's a viable technical/design approach at what appetite? — the work the 2019 book calls "shaping."
- **New sequence:** Framing → Shaping → **Package** (Singer's new term for the shaped, ready-to-bet output — replacing the older term "Pitch") → Betting Table → Building → Cool-down.

Singer's own words:
> "The output of a framing session is a well-framed problem: something where the business says 'if we can shape this into something doable and execute within X weeks, that will be meaningful to us.'"
> — *Framing*, 2022

This is post-book **canon**, not extension — Singer treats the 2019 book's "shaping" chapters as still correct in mechanics but incomplete in sequence.

**Shape Up 2.0 (informal label).** Singer used the phrase on the *Shapers & Builders* podcast (2023) to describe the evolved model — the framing prelude, the adaptation for non-Basecamp companies, tighter distinction between framing / shaping / betting / building. It is not a formal "second edition of the book" (though the second edition of the book is mentioned as an in-progress topic in the Lenny 2025 conversation; treat as unconfirmed until Singer publishes).

**Shaping in Real Life.** Formerly a paid online course; now Singer distributes the material as free articles and videos on ryansinger.co. Core delta: the 2019 book describes "an unusual company" (Basecamp — everyone technical, tiny team, unified skills, product-led CEO) and *Shaping in Real Life* adapts the method for typical companies (separate backend / frontend / designer / QA / product / marketing / sales, non-technical PMs shaping in isolation, legacy systems).

**Common Pitfalls (2025 article).** The single densest catalog of adoption failure modes Singer has published since the book. Three named failure modes:
1. **Undershaped work** — non-technical shapers produce packages that look complete but crumble in build; "The #1 failure mode of attempted Shape Up adoptions is 'undershaped' work."
2. **Blurred framing and shaping** — teams jump into solution work before agreeing the problem is worth solving.
3. **Mixing project work with reactive work** — same team owning maintenance, support, and projects loses focus mid-cycle.

**End-to-End case study (2025).** A ~30-minute worked example on a gym-management software product acquired by a real company. Singer walks the whole loop: candidate → framing (interview with a former gym owner) → shaping (two 2-hour whiteboard sessions with a senior engineer) → package written → build kickoff with a cross-functional team (backend, frontend, designer, QA, SME) → sequencing 9 vertical slices → shipping. Notable delta from the book: **narrow to one candidate before shaping** (don't shape multiple competing pitches).

**Related essays evolving the model:**
- *What's the right level of detail when shaping?* (2026) — detail level depends on the document's purpose (approval-seeking vs. direction-giving); post-book sharpening of the "fat marker sketch" idea.
- *When engineers say "that'll take months!"* (2025) — legacy systems as the hidden shape-breaker; when everything takes months, shaping alone won't rescue the team.
- *Not everyone needs to be talking to customers* (2024) — pushback against Torres-style "every team member interviews customers weekly"; specialization is legitimate.
- *We did all this discovery… now how do we decide?* (2024) — the framing → decision leap; discovery data doesn't tell you what to bet on.
- *The cost of not shaping* (2023) — projects without shaping either succeed by luck or stall mysteriously near the end.
- *Shape Up is for features, not all development work* (2021) — one of the earliest and most-cited post-book clarifications; the method is designed for **new feature bets**, not maintenance, infra, or bug work — which should live in cool-down or a separate operating mode.

**Nothing has been formally deprecated.** The 2019 mechanisms — 6-week cycle, 2-week cool-down, betting table, appetite, pitch/package, hill charts, circuit breaker, no backlog — are all still Singer's current teaching. The changes are:
- Framing has been added upstream of shaping (2022).
- "Pitch" is being renamed "Package" (2022+).
- The method is now explicitly framed as an adaptation problem for non-Basecamp companies (2023+), not a one-size-fits-all system.

**AI-era commentary — noteworthy caveat.** Little direct Singer commentary on how AI-native product development changes shaping. He has commented informally on Twitter/LinkedIn that AI shifts what "uphill" work looks like (unknowns collapse faster) but has not published a canonical post on it. If a user asks "how does Shape Up apply to an AI product," you are extrapolating; the primary source hasn't spoken definitively.

## 3. VOICE & TONE

**Register:** careful, systemic, ex-designer-turned-methodologist. Not polemical like DHH or Fried (who can be sharp, dismissive, and provocative in their public writing); closer to an engineer explaining why a specific mechanism prevents a specific failure mode. Singer teaches by walking through concrete artifacts (a fat marker sketch, a pitch, a hill chart) and letting the reader see the mechanism, rather than by argument or slogan. He is contrarian *to Agile* but the contrarianism is dry — "we don't do backlogs" said flatly, as a description of practice rather than a rallying cry.

**Rhetorical moves:**
- **"Not X, but Y" contrasts, quietly stated** — "not estimation, appetite"; "not tasks, scopes"; "not backlogs, bets"; "not sprints, cycles"; "not requirements documents, pitches"; "not burndown, hill charts." He rarely says "Agile is wrong"; he says "here is what we do instead."
- **Teach through the artifact.** Almost every explanation runs: name the mechanism → show an example artifact (drawn, in the book) → describe the disposition it enforces. Sketch-driven teaching.
- **The "fat marker" as a signature device.** Sketches deliberately imprecise so that reviewers focus on structure, not visual polish. "If you can't draw the idea with a fat marker, you're too deep in the details."
- **Refuses to over-claim.** Repeatedly notes when things at Basecamp were unusual: "everyone at Basecamp — including designers — was very technical"; the 2019 book is what worked at "an unusual company." The 2022+ writing is explicit that adaptation is required elsewhere.
- **Refuses to sell adoption as easy.** The 2025 *Common Pitfalls* piece opens by naming that "The #1 failure mode of attempted Shape Up adoptions is 'undershaped' work" — sets the bar honestly.
- **"Mentoring, pragmatic tone" (from 2025 pitfalls piece).** He positions himself as an experienced practitioner who has seen the failure modes, not as the inventor defending his invention.

**Signature vocabulary (drop verbatim into the skill's voice profile):**
- **Shape Up** (the frame — always two words, capitalized)
- **Shaping** (the pre-project design work by senior people)
- **Betting** (the go/no-go decision at the Betting Table)
- **Building** (the six-week execution)
- **Cool-down** (the two-week gap between cycles — for bugs, ad-hoc, and the Betting Table)
- **Appetite** (fixed time budget — replaces estimates)
- **Pitch** (the 2019 term for the shaped document — being renamed **Package** in 2022+)
- **Framing** (the 2022+ upstream step — is this problem worth solving?)
- **Betting Table** (senior stakeholders decide what gets six weeks)
- **Circuit Breaker** (default-kill for bets that run over — do not extend)
- **Hill Chart** — dots on a curve, uphill = figuring out, downhill = executing
- **Fat marker sketch** (deliberately low-fidelity UI sketch)
- **Breadboarding** (deliberately low-fidelity structural sketch — components, affordances, connections)
- **Scopes** (parts of a project that can be built, integrated, and shipped independently)
- **No backlog** (radical — decide fresh each cycle)
- **Rabbit holes** (details Singer forces the pitch to name so builders don't get stuck)
- **No-gos** (things intentionally excluded to fit the appetite)
- **Small batch / Big batch** — appetite framings (small = 1–2 weeks; big = full 6 weeks)
- **Uphill / Downhill / Plateau** — the phases of a scope's progress
- **The build team** (small — typically one designer + one or two programmers)
- **The shapers** (typically senior people; sometimes just the founder or head of product)
- **Two-track system** (shaping happens in parallel with building — not inside the cycle)
- **"Getting to done"** — Singer's preferred completion frame; "done means deployed."

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**The core disposition — fixed time, variable scope:**
- **Do** set the appetite *before* designing the solution. "How much time is this problem worth?" not "how long will this take?"
- **Do** hold the appetite as a hard constraint — cut scope to fit, don't extend time.
- **Don't** estimate. Appetite replaces estimation. Estimating and then time-boxing is the worst of both worlds.

**Shaping — mandatory ingredients of a Pitch/Package (from Ch. 6 "Write the Pitch"):**
1. **Problem** — a specific story that shows why the status quo doesn't work.
2. **Appetite** — how much time this deserves (small batch: 1–2 weeks; big batch: full 6-week cycle).
3. **Solution** — the core elements, at the fat-marker / breadboard level of fidelity. Not screens. Structural.
4. **Rabbit holes** — details worth calling out so builders don't get stuck (edge cases, tricky data, known unknowns).
5. **No-gos** — anything intentionally excluded to fit the appetite. Explicit scope boundaries.

**Do's for shaping:**
- **Do** shape in a small group (typically 1–2 senior people, usually including someone with real technical depth).
- **Do** use fat marker sketches and breadboarding to keep the fidelity low. Detail invites nitpicking; low fidelity keeps attention on structure.
- **Do** name the rabbit holes explicitly. If you know something will trip the team up, write it.
- **Do** name the no-gos explicitly. Un-named scope will re-inflate mid-cycle.
- **Do** frame first (2022+ addition). Before shaping a solution, confirm the business agrees the problem is worth solving.

**Don'ts for shaping:**
- **Don't** shape without technical depth in the room. Non-technical shaping produces "undershaped" packages that look complete but crumble in build.
- **Don't** shape ten pitches and pick at the Betting Table. Narrow to one candidate through framing *before* investing shaping effort.
- **Don't** ship high-fidelity mocks as shaping output. That's design work; shaping output is deliberately imprecise.
- **Don't** blur framing and shaping. Framing is about the problem; shaping is about a viable solution. Skipping framing is the "why did we just build this?" pattern.

**Betting — the Betting Table:**
- **Do** hold it at the end of cool-down, before the next cycle starts.
- **Do** treat the shaped package as the go/no-go artifact — no PowerPoint dressing, no salesmanship.
- **Do** be willing to say no. Most shaped work should not be bet on. Bets are commitments; commitments are scarce.
- **Do** commit fully once you bet — no interruptions to the team for six weeks.

**Betting Table failure modes:**
- **Approving anything shaped.** Shaping cost is sunk; that's not a reason to bet.
- **Committing but leaking scope.** "Just this one small addition" mid-cycle destroys the mechanism.
- **Betting on undershaped work.** If the shaping isn't clear enough to know the team can execute, the answer is "reshape," not "bet and hope."

**Building — the six-week cycle:**
- **Do** hand full responsibility to the small build team (typically 1 designer + 1–2 programmers).
- **Do** let them decompose the work into scopes themselves. Scopes are their tool, not the pitch's.
- **Do** wire functionality before high-fidelity design (from the 2025 case study).
- **Do** use hill charts to make progress visible — one dot per scope, moving uphill (figuring out) then downhill (executing) then flat (done).

**Building failure modes:**
- **Task lists instead of scopes.** A hundred small tasks lose the structure. Scopes are integrated slices — each one shippable in principle.
- **Micro-managed cycles.** The point of the shaped pitch was to hand the team autonomy. Reasserting control mid-cycle defeats the mechanism.
- **Hill charts as performance theater.** Moving dots to look like progress instead of showing real position. "Stuck at the top" is honest data; move dots when the work moves, not when the manager wants to see movement.

**The Circuit Breaker (Ch. 14) — the mechanism most teams get wrong:**
- **Do** default to killing bets that run over six weeks. Extension is the exception, not the norm.
- **Do** treat the deadline as a forcing function for scope choices. Cut scope. Ship less. Ship what's ready.
- **Don't** extend because "we're so close." Uphill work at the deadline usually signals a conceptual flaw, not execution slowness.
- **Singer's own words:** "If the work is uphill, it's better to do something else in the next cycle and put the troubled project back in the shaping phase."

**No Backlog (Ch. 7) — the radical idea:**
- **Do** decide fresh each cycle from a small pool of shaped candidates.
- **Do** trust that important ideas will resurface. If it isn't worth re-shaping when it comes back up, it wasn't worth doing.
- **Don't** maintain a JIRA backlog of hundreds of tickets. The maintenance cost is real; the ideas rot; nobody trusts the ordering.
- **Don't** replace the backlog with a fancier tool. The point is *no backlog*, not "backlog with better UX."

**Common misapplications (Singer's explicit "gotchas," most from *Common Pitfalls* 2025):**
- **Treating cycles as sprints.** Cycles are not sprints. Sprints are short (1–2 weeks), repeating, small scope, tightly managed by a Scrum Master. Cycles are long (6 weeks), non-repeating in content, whole-project scope, autonomously executed. Same word "iteration" doesn't mean same thing.
- **Keeping a backlog.** Violates the model. If you have a backlog, you're not running Shape Up; you're running Scrum with 6-week sprints.
- **Shaping inside the cycle.** Shaping must happen *before* betting. Shaping inside the cycle steals build time and produces undershaped work.
- **Estimation habits sneaking back in.** "But how big is this appetite really?" is estimation with a new label. Appetite is a *budget*, not a *prediction*.
- **Hill Charts as vanity metric.** Moving dots because leadership wants to see movement. The mechanism only works if dots reflect real position.
- **Shaping without technical depth.** Non-technical PMs shaping in isolation produce packages that look complete but the build team can't execute. Shaping is not the same as writing a PRD.
- **Applying Shape Up to bugs, maintenance, infrastructure.** Shape Up is for **feature bets**. Cool-down and other operating modes handle everything else.
- **Fractional build teams.** The 6-week cycle assumes one designer and 1–2 programmers, fully dedicated. Matrixed / fractional teams break the cadence.

**The two most important post-book warnings:**
- **Undershaped work is the #1 adoption failure.** Everything downstream depends on the package. A weak package produces a weak build.
- **Framing must precede shaping.** The 2019 book jumped straight from "raw idea" to shaping. In practice this creates rework. Ask "is this problem worth solving?" before "how would we solve it?"

**Cross-cutting adoption gotchas — why companies fail to adopt Shape Up:**
- **Not everyone at your company is technical.** Basecamp's designers all coded; the shaping-with-a-senior-engineer prescription is easy because there's no seam. Elsewhere, you must engineer the technical presence explicitly.
- **Leadership CEO commitment is required for "no backlog."** Removing the backlog is a political act. Middle management can't do it unilaterally.
- **Six weeks feels long to VCs and stakeholders who want weekly demos.** Ship-nothing weeks 1–5 is scary if you haven't seen the pattern work. Ship in week 6, honest.
- **Cool-down feels like slacking.** It isn't. Bugs get fixed, exploration happens, the Betting Table meets. Skipping cool-down destroys the cadence.
- **Applying Shape Up to teams below ~5 people is often overkill.** The ceremony (Betting Table, 6-week commit, cool-down) needs enough teams to load-balance. Very small teams can borrow ideas (appetite, no backlog, circuit breaker) without adopting the full cadence.

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**vs. Scrum:**
- **Scrum:** short sprints (typically 2 weeks), backlog-driven, story-point estimation, standups, Scrum Master, product owner grooming a prioritized queue.
- **Shape Up:** 6-week cycles, no backlog, appetite (not estimation), no assigned Scrum Master role, no daily standup ritual, small autonomous build team.
- Shape Up is **explicitly a rejection of most Scrum ceremony.** Singer doesn't attack Scrum by name much; he describes what Basecamp does instead and lets the contrast speak. DHH is more explicit ("we don't do sprints" is a standing 37signals line).
- **The most important disagreement is on backlogs.** Scrum requires a product backlog; Shape Up rejects the backlog as an idea. This is not a superficial disagreement.
- **The second most important is on estimation.** Scrum uses story points and velocity; Shape Up rejects both. Appetite is not story points with a new name.

**vs. Kanban / continuous flow:**
- **Kanban:** WIP-limited pull system, continuous flow, no fixed cadence.
- **Shape Up:** fixed 6-week cadence with a hard circuit breaker. The cadence is the point.
- Not compatible — Shape Up's mechanism *requires* the cycle-and-cool-down rhythm.

**vs. Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres):**
- **Torres:** weekly customer interviews, opportunity solution trees, small experiments, cross-functional discovery.
- **Shape Up:** shaping is closer to design work than to discovery. Framing (2022+) is closer to what Torres calls opportunity identification, but done by a small senior group rather than a cross-functional trio-and-team.
- Singer's 2024 essay *Not everyone needs to be talking to customers* is a mild pushback on the Torres orthodoxy that every product team member interviews users weekly. Not a rejection — Singer respects the work — but a defense of specialization.
- **They compose.** Torres-style discovery can feed the "raw idea" and "framing" inputs to Shape Up. But the two operate at different scales and dispositions.

**vs. Marty Cagan's Inspired / SVPG model:**
- **Cagan:** empowered product teams, product manager + designer + engineers, discovery + delivery split, outcome-oriented objectives, no output-based commitments.
- **Shape Up:** small build teams with high autonomy (compatible) but shaped bets that *are* commitments to specific solutions (partial disagreement with Cagan's "outcome, not output").
- The philosophical delta: Cagan trusts empowered teams to figure out both problem and solution; Singer separates shaping (senior, upstream) from building (team, downstream). This is *the* big philosophical split.
- Both agree on small autonomous build teams. Both reject waterfall PRDs. They disagree on where the shaping / discovery work happens and who owns it.

**vs. Working Backwards (Bill Carr / Colin Bryar / Amazon) — see [[working-backwards]]:**
- **Working Backwards:** PR/FAQ + 6-page narrative memo + Single-Threaded Leader + Input Metrics; heavyweight document-driven pre-work.
- **Shape Up:** Pitch/Package + Betting Table + small autonomous build team + Hill Charts; medium-weight structured pre-work.
- **What they share:** the conviction that heavyweight *thinking before building* is worth weeks of investment; the rejection of ship-fast-and-iterate as a universal answer; small autonomous execution teams; a single artifact (PR/FAQ or Pitch) that is the go/no-go decision object.
- **Where they diverge:**
  - Working Backwards writes for the customer's future perspective (press release from a launch date); Shape Up writes for the builder's perspective (fat marker sketch, breadboard, rabbit holes, no-gos).
  - Working Backwards is aggressively narrative prose (no bullets, silent reading meeting); Shape Up is sketch-and-artifact driven.
  - Working Backwards has explicit CEO-level review (Bezos-shaped); Shape Up has a small Betting Table (Fried/DHH-shaped) — different power structures.
  - Working Backwards has no fixed cycle; Shape Up's 6-week cycle + circuit breaker is load-bearing.
  - Working Backwards has input metrics + Weekly Business Reviews; Shape Up has hill charts and cool-downs.
  - Working Backwards presupposes Amazon-scale ($$$/bet); Shape Up scales down to any small-team feature bet.
- **Both are "structured pre-work before building"** — but they represent different resolutions of the same core intuition. A team could plausibly use Working Backwards to decide *whether* to bet and Shape Up to shape *how* to bet; more commonly, teams pick one or the other and adapt.

**vs. Roger Martin's Playing to Win / Rumelt's kernel:**
- Different altitudes. Martin/Rumelt are strategy-making at the corporate/business-unit level. Shape Up is product execution at the feature/initiative level.
- Compatible — a company can use Playing to Win for corporate strategy and Shape Up for how features get built inside it.

**vs. OKRs:**
- Singer has not written a canonical "Shape Up vs. OKRs" piece. In practice, OKRs run above Shape Up cycles: quarterly outcomes are set (OKR territory), specific feature bets that ladder up are shaped and bet on (Shape Up territory).
- The tension is around commitment: OKRs commit to outcomes; Shape Up commits to solutions inside a fixed appetite. These are different objects and can coexist without conflict.

**vs. Lean Startup (Ries):**
- **Lean Startup:** minimum test that validates a hypothesis; build-measure-learn.
- **Shape Up:** shape a solution to a known problem worth solving; ship in 6 weeks.
- Different pre-conditions. Lean Startup is pre-PMF; Shape Up assumes the team knows what business they're in and is now building the next feature bet. In a pre-PMF context, Shape Up is overkill.
