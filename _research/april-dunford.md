## 2. POST-BOOK MATERIAL (refinements since Obviously Awesome 2019)

### 2a. Sales Pitch (2023) — the sales-conversation extension of positioning

**Core thesis (Lenny's Podcast, Oct 22, 2023):** "The pitch was never about 'pushing' our products on buyers. It was always about helping customers make confident purchase decisions." Buyers "are more worried about messing up than they are about missing out." She cites 40–60% of B2B deals ending in **"no decision"** as the real enemy — not competitors.

**The 8-step pitch, in two halves — "Setup" + "Follow-Through"** (canonical structure from Sales Pitch book + Lenny's ep):

**SETUP (get aligned on the market first, before you talk about your product):**
1. **Insight** — "The thing we understand about the market that the others do not." Ask: "What do best-fit prospects need to know to understand why our differentiated value is important?" This is the "hardest and most crucial step".
2. **Alternatives** — Grouped by *approach*, not vendor names. Include status quo, DIY, spreadsheets, doing nothing. Present pros/cons of each approach objectively. Warning: don't introduce approaches the buyer hasn't already considered — that stalls the deal further.
3. **The Perfect World** — What the ideal purchase criteria *should* be for a buyer who shares your worldview.

**FOLLOW-THROUGH:**
4. **Introduction** — Name the product and the market category positioning.
5. **Differentiated Value** — Features that are BOTH unique AND valuable. Grouped into 1–4 "value themes". "The value that only you can deliver."
6. **Proof** — Case studies, testimonials, quantified customer results — matched to prospect's industry/size.
7. **Objections** (optional) — Address unspoken concerns proactively.
8. **The Ask** — Explicit next step.

**Why other common pitch shapes fail (from Lenny's summary + book):**
- Walkthrough pitch — assumes buyer knowledge, kills discovery.
- Problem/solution pitch — competitors define the same problem identically; you lose differentiation.
- Vision narrative — visions aren't proven; multiple "new ways" exist.
- Hero's journey — engaging but doesn't help the buyer decide.

**Rollout test (Sales Pitch book):** Pitch is ready when (1) reps stop requesting changes, and (2) reps prefer the new pitch to their old deck.

### 2b. Refinements to the 5 Components (Obviously Awesome 2nd edition, 2026)

Restructured from 10 process steps to **5 steps** (matching the 5 components) because "the first few steps were really pre-work for a positioning exercise, and the final steps described work that needed to happen after the exercise" (Substack announcement, Jan 12, 2026). New material added:
- **Pre-work** — decisions teams should make before a positioning exercise (audience, scope, readiness).
- **Multi-product** — a full section for companies with more than one offering (see 2d below).
- **Differentiated value** — significantly expanded ("most teams told me they got stuck there").
- **What happens after** — expanded guidance on translating positioning into sales pitch + messaging (this is where Sales Pitch bolts on).
- New market-category thinking (see Ep 21 "New Thinking on Market Categories", Mar 2026).

### 2c. Positioning exercise structure (workshop)

Cross-functional team, not marketing alone. Required participants:
- Founder/CEO (must sponsor for it to stick)
- Marketing head
- Sales leadership + 1–2 experienced AEs
- Product management
- Customer success

**Core exercise sequence (5 steps, in order):**
1. **Let go of where you came from** — release the default position that comes from the original product idea. "Weak positioning is usually the result of hanging on to a 'default' market position that is rooted in the history of the product idea."
2. **Isolate your uniqueness** — list unique attributes.
3. **Map attributes to value themes** — group into 1–4 clusters.
4. **Who cares?** — identify best-fit customer segments who value the differentiation most.
5. **Choose your best market frame of reference** — the market category where value becomes obvious.

Optional 6th layer: **Trend** — layer a relevant trend on top only if it makes the value more obvious to the target.

### 2d. Multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona (from Substack "A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas")
- **Single product stage** → don't split company vs product positioning. Consolidate.
- **Multi-product** → 3 options: (a) umbrella positioning, (b) lead-product model (secondary products assume buyer owns primary), (c) product family/platform.
- **Multiple segments** → one positioning suffices if the differentiated capabilities are consistent across segments; tailor content and customer examples per segment, not the positioning itself.
- **Multiple personas** → position for the **champion** (the internal deal advocate); handle other stakeholders through **objection handling**, not separate positioning.

### 2e. Repositioning cases she uses
- **Janna Systems** (her own past company) — the canonical case. Pivoted from generic "Enterprise CRM" competing against Siebel to "CRM for Investment Banks". Grew from <$2M to ~$80M ARR in 18 months, acquired for $1.3B. Used constantly to show that "market category" is the highest-leverage lever.
- **Help Scout** — insight: "Customer service is a growth driver, not a cost center." Nick Francis (CEO) is a guest on her podcast walking through the evolution.
- **Postman** — repositioning as "API platform" after 3 years of category confusion. Cited as "didn't know what to call themselves".
- **Segway / Magic Leap** — cautionary tales of hyped positioning with no clear customer application.
- **Sampler, Watcom** — smaller examples she uses to illustrate "point of view".

### 2f. Critique of common positioning failures

- **Positioning-as-tagline**: "Take all the 'marketing' words out of the positioning statement. It's NOT messaging."
- **Positioning-as-mission**: "Storytelling, messaging, a tag line, your vision, branding — these are all examples of things that happen *after* positioning. Each of these things requires positioning as an input."
- **Positioning statement fill-in-the-blank template**: "not only pointless but potentially dangerous". Assumes there's one right answer for each blank when most products could sit in multiple categories with different competitors and value.
- **Feature-list positioning**: features on their own are just features; you have to translate to value the buyer actually cares about.
- **Positioning-as-brand-positioning**: "I think marketers like to make stuff up… branding is probably the most poorly defined marketing term I can think of."

### 3b. Recurring rhetorical moves
- **"Here's how it went wrong" opener** — starts a lot of talks/essays with a client (or her own Janna Systems) horror story, then reframes it.
- **Elephant/blindfold parable** — used constantly to argue for cross-functional positioning: "The guy on the tail says, 'It's a snake,' and the guy on the leg says, 'It's a tree.'"
- **Movie-opening-scene analogy** — "Positioning is like the opening scene of a movie" — context-setting for the product.
- **"Sell what's on the truck"** — position what you actually have today, not the roadmap.
- **"What would customers do if we didn't exist?"** — the diagnostic question she comes back to repeatedly.
- **The 40–60% "no decision" statistic** — anchor number she uses in almost every sales-pitch talk.

### 3c. Signature vocabulary (use verbatim in skill)
- **Competitive alternatives** (never "competitors")
- **Best-fit customers / best-fit prospects**
- **Differentiated value** (never just "value")
- **Value themes** (grouped features → 1–4 clusters)
- **Market category / market frame of reference**
- **Unique attributes** or **unique capabilities**
- **Insight** and **point of view** (in the Sales Pitch context)
- **The perfect world**
- **The setup / the follow-through**
- **The ask**
- **Phantom competitors** (theoretical rivals you never actually meet in deals)
- **Status quo** (the "do nothing" default)
- **Product pessimism**
- **Whale bias** (sales overweighting one big lost deal)
- **Horizon competitors** (product's fixation on rivals sales never sees)
- **Champion** (the internal deal advocate)
- **Sell what's on the truck**
- **Positioning is context-setting**

### 3d. Words / framings she pushes back on
- "**Value prop**" as a substitute for positioning
- "**Positioning is what you say about yourself**" — no; positioning is what puts you in a competitive frame
- "**Positioning is a tagline / statement**"
- "**Brand positioning**" (conflates two distinct concepts)
- "**We're the best**" / "**We're the leader in…**" — meaningless without a specified frame
- "**Everything you marketers cook up over there**" (a CEO-said-this line she uses as the anti-example)
- "**We need to educate the market**" — usually a red flag that you're trying to create an unnecessary new category (90% of recent public tech companies won in existing categories, per her Quickstart Guide)
- "**Positioning statement**" as a Mad-Libs template ("pointless but potentially dangerous")

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

### 4a. When to reposition (symptoms of weak positioning)
- Existing customers love the product; new prospects "don't get it" — the mismatch is the tell.
- Sales requires an extra call just to explain what you are.
- Customers compare you to the wrong things ("Oh, you're like Salesforce" / "I could just do this in a spreadsheet").
- Different functions (founder, marketing, sales, CS) describe the product differently.
- Losing to "no decision" more than to competitors.
- Market has shifted (new entrants, tech disruption like AI, category consolidation).
- Regular cadence: check-in a couple times a year even without symptoms.

### 4b. Anti-patterns / gotchas
- **Positioning as internal opinion battle** — "Marketing never wins the battle of opinions." Get everyone in the room together first.
- **Fighting phantom competitors** — waste of positioning capital.
- **New category creation as default** — usually the wrong bet; first-movers often lose (Google beat Ask Jeeves, FB beat Myspace).
- **Assuming value is self-evident** — "so what?" is the bridge from features to business outcomes.
- **Too many value themes** — cap at 1–4.
- **Value confused with objection handling** — see essay.
- **Positioning by copy** — a bad homepage doesn't mean bad positioning; you can't diagnose from outside without knowing the buyer + competitive set.
- **Investor pitch = sales pitch** — she has a whole essay: "Why Your Investor Pitch Is Ruining Your Sales Pitch."
- **Positioning around a trend** — layer trend on top only if it makes value obvious; don't lead with it.
- **Assuming novelty > clarity** — "always better to be a little boring than completely baffling."

### 4c. How to run a positioning workshop (operational skeleton)
- Pre-work: agree scope (what's being positioned), agree audience, gather win/loss data, sales call recordings, top customer interviews.
- Assemble cross-functional team (see 2c).
- Session 1 — vocabulary alignment + let go of the default position + list competitive alternatives.
- Session 2 — isolate unique attributes; group into value themes.
- Session 3 — who cares? (best-fit segmentation) + pick market frame.
- Session 4 — capture on one-page positioning canvas.
- Post-workshop — build sales pitch (with sales in the room, or it won't stick); translate to messaging; instrument tests via live sales conversations.

### 4d. Positioning vs Messaging vs Branding (the crisp distinction)
- **Positioning** — strategic; defines the market and why you win it. Input to everything else.
- **Messaging** — tactical; the specific phrases that communicate the positioning across channels/personas.
- **Branding** — visual identity, tone, colors, imagery. Downstream of positioning ("If I sell security software to banks, my branding should convey trust.").

### 4e. Common misapplications
- Teams saying they're "doing positioning" but actually doing:
  - Copywriting (rewriting the homepage)
  - Branding (rebrand, new logo)
  - Messaging (new taglines)
  - Vision-setting
  - Category creation as a shortcut to differentiation

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

### 6a. Ries & Trout (Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind, 1981)
Acknowledges them as foundational: they defined the concept but "didn't give a how-to manual." Her critique: their era assumed companies broadcast positioning top-down; today customers co-shape it, and their fill-in-the-blank statement template is "pointless but potentially dangerous." Sees herself as executing on their idea, not replacing it.

### 6b. Geoffrey Moore (Crossing the Chasm)
Direct influence — technology adoption cycle informs her segmentation and how she thinks about market category. Explicitly names Moore in the synthesis behind Obviously Awesome.

### 6c. Clayton Christensen (Jobs to Be Done)
Named directly as an input. JTBD sensibility shows in her "what would customers do if you didn't exist?" question and her insistence that alternatives include non-product options (status quo, DIY, "hire an intern").

### 6d. Michael Porter (Competitive Strategy)
Rarely cited by name — Porter operates at the strategy-of-the-firm altitude; Dunford is one altitude below (product positioning within a strategy). Compatible, not competing. She would call his "differentiation strategy" the input; her framework is the operational method for expressing it.

### 6e. Andy Raskin (Strategic Narrative)
Complementary, not overlapping. Community shorthand: **Dunford is the gold standard for B2B positioning; Raskin is the gold standard for strategic narrative arc.** Positioning tells you *where you fit in the market*; strategic narrative tells you *why now, why this change matters*. Positioning comes before the narrative. Raskin's structure is closer to Dunford's "Setup" phase (especially Insight + Perfect World) than to positioning itself.

### 6f. JTBD (Kalbach / Moesta / Ulwick)
Compatible. Christensen already sits inside her lineage. JTBD is upstream input (what job is being hired for) → Dunford framework operationalizes how you position the product doing that job against alternatives. Notably: her "competitive alternatives" question is essentially a JTBD move — buyers are hiring *something* to do the job; anything they might hire is an alternative.

### 6g. Roger Martin (Playing to Win cascade)
Also compatible. Playing to Win asks "where to play / how to win" at the corporate level; Dunford's method is what "how to win" looks like at the product/market interface. She never cites Martin directly, but her insistence on trade-offs ("If you decide to go north, you cannot also go south.") is Martin-shaped.

### 6h. Category Design / Play Bigger
Explicitly skeptical of "you must create your own category" as a default. Her 90%-of-tech-IPOs-were-in-existing-categories datapoint is a direct counter to Play Bigger orthodoxy.
