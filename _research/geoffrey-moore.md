## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

Moore's method is a **staged theory of market development**. Every concept below fits into the meta-cycle: an innovation enters through visionaries (Early Market), stalls at a **chasm**, is dragged through it by a **beachhead** attack (D-Day discipline), then rolls up adjacent verticals (**Bowling Alley**), then goes vertical-agnostic in the **Tornado**, then matures on **Main Street**, then declines. Each stage has its own playbook; **mixing playbooks kills the campaign**.

### Technology Adoption Life Cycle (TALC)
Moore's core taxonomy, adapted from Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962). Five psychographic segments along a bell curve:
- **Innovators** ("technology enthusiasts") — buy for the tech itself, tolerate anything, ~2.5% of market.
- **Early Adopters** ("visionaries") — buy for strategic breakthrough / competitive discontinuity; tolerate immaturity; ~13.5%.
- **Early Majority** ("pragmatists") — buy incremental productivity gains, require proof + references from peers; ~34%.
- **Late Majority** ("conservatives") — buy only once the standard is set, price-sensitive, want turnkey; ~34%.
- **Laggards** ("skeptics") — buy involuntarily, if at all; ~16%.

**Moore's addition to Rogers:** the *chasm* — a discontinuity between visionaries and pragmatists that Rogers did not name. Also smaller cracks between adjacent segments (between innovators and visionaries, and between pragmatists and conservatives). The chasm is the killer.

### The Chasm — why it exists
The chasm exists because **pragmatists will not accept visionaries as references**. Visionaries seek discontinuous change; pragmatists seek continuity. A pragmatist buys because *other pragmatists* have bought, not because a visionary evangelist tells them to. Therefore a company that has cleared the Early Market has *zero* transferable credibility for the mainstream, and typically discovers this only after the pipeline has already collapsed.

> "The point of greatest peril in the development of a high-tech market lies in making the transition from an early market dominated by a few visionary customers to a mainstream market dominated by a large block of customers who are predominantly pragmatists in orientation." — *Crossing the Chasm* (via Goodreads). https://www.goodreads.com/author/quotes/34517.Geoffrey_A_Moore

### D-Day analogy
The strategic frame for crossing. Long-term goal = enter the mainstream (Europe), held by an entrenched competitor (the Axis). The chasm is the English Channel. **You cross by concentrating overwhelming force on one narrow beachhead (Normandy) rather than a broad-front invasion.**

> "The D-Day strategy keeps everyone on point—if we don't take Normandy, we don't have to worry about how we're going to take Paris." — *Crossing the Chasm*.

**Consequence:** the discipline is negative as much as positive — refusing every attractive-looking segment that dissipates force is the point. A broad front kills the campaign.

### Beachhead segmentation
The chosen segment must be:
- **Big enough to matter** — Moore's Lenny 2024 rule of thumb: "potential to reach $100 million in revenue within five years."
- **Small enough to lead** — you must plausibly dominate it end-to-end; "a big fish in a small pond."
- **A good fit with your crown jewels** — your differentiated capability must map to their compelling pain.

Segments are **psychographic + vertical + use-case**, not just industry. Moore's canonical technique is **Target-Customer Characterization (TCC)** — the team generates ~50 one-page target-customer scenarios (customer profile + use case before + use case after), then filters down.

### Compelling Reason to Buy / Compelling Reason to Act
The pragmatist's threshold. Not "nice to have"; not "productivity gain"; a **broken process where the pain is currently costing the customer real money and the customer knows it**. Documentum's beachhead was pharma regulatory affairs where new-drug applications of 250,000–500,000 pages cost ~$1M/day in lost patent life — a textbook Compelling Reason.

> "You don't have to evangelize the technology. You just have to take their problem off the table." — Geoffrey Moore, Forbes / Randy Bean interview, Jul 14, 2024. https://www.forbes.com/sites/randybean/2024/07/14/crossing-the-ai-chasm-with-technology-thinker-and-visionary-geoffrey-moore/

### Whole Product
Adapted from Ted Levitt's generic/expected/augmented/potential model. The **whole product** = the minimum set of products and services required to satisfy the *pragmatist's* compelling reason to buy — not just what you ship. It includes integrations, services, training, partner ecosystem, references. In the Early Market visionaries fill the whole-product gap themselves; **pragmatists refuse to**.

> "Pragmatists evaluate and buy whole products." — *Crossing the Chasm* (canonical formulation).

**The whole-product gap is the operational reason the chasm exists.** Crossing = closing the gap for one beachhead segment via partnerships and services.

### Bowling Alley
Post-chasm, pre-tornado. The company owns a beachhead vertical; adjacent segments are "pins." Each new pin is won using **references from the prior pin** (either the *same customer type in a new industry* or *a new use case for the same customer*). Vertical-focused expansion. Whole-product investment leverages across pins. Documentum's post-pharma cascade into chemicals, oil refineries, and financial services is the archetype.

> "Note that we are still at the departmental level, still a point-product app, not a platform, not a suite… You want your team to be plowing in the adjacent field, not harvesting in the initial one." — Moore, "After the Chasm—Scaling Beyond the Beachhead," geoffreyamoore.com.

### Tornado
Book: *Inside the Tornado* (1995). Mass-market hypergrowth when the pragmatist herd tips *en masse*. Playbook **reverses** the Bowling Alley:
- Sell horizontal, not vertical.
- Ship product, not solutions.
- Grab market share above all — price/margin is secondary.
- Standardize aggressively; kill customization.

Post-tornado power-law market-share distribution:
- **Gorilla** — dominant vendor, ~50% share, earns 75–80% of industry profits.
- **Chimps** — the challengers who wanted to be gorillas and failed; 15–30% share each; must discount 20–40% to win deals; perpetually contest features without closing the gap.
- **Monkeys** — low-cost clones; single-digit share; survive on niches the Gorilla ignores.

> "The winning strategy does not just change as we move from stage to stage, it actually reverses the prior strategy." — *Inside the Tornado*.

> "Infrastructure, to be useful, must be standard and global, so once the market moves to switch out the old for the new, it wants to complete this transition as rapidly as possible." — *Inside the Tornado*.

### Main Street
Post-tornado maturity. Growth slows, category solidifies. Playbook = **share-of-wallet expansion** within installed base: extensions, up-market moves, customer intimacy, "+1" niche modifications. This is where the customer-success operating model lives (Moore explicitly maps this in Gainsight Pulse 2022).

### Category Maturity Life Cycle (CMLC / TMLC) — Chasm Institute canonical form
The consolidated life-cycle Chasm Institute teaches. Six phases:
1. **Early Market** — visionaries, immature product
2. **Chasm** — extended sales cycles, no referenceable base
3. **Bowling Alley** — pragmatists inside vertical niches
4. **Tornado** — mainstream stampede, price wars, feature wars
5. **Main Street** — saturation, niche modifications, share-of-wallet
6. **End of Life** — obsolescence / replacement

Reference: https://www.chasminstitute.com/Resources/Frameworks/tabid/309/Default.aspx

### 9-Point Strategy Checklist (Chasm Institute)
Applied differently per TMLC stage:
1. Target Customer
2. Compelling Reason to Buy
3. Whole Product
4. Partners & Allies
5. Distribution
6. Pricing
7. Competition
8. Positioning
9. Next Target

**Same nine slots, different content per stage.** This is the operational template Moore's consulting practice runs on.

### Hierarchy of Powers (5 powers, from *Escape Velocity*, 2011)
Investor-lens diagnostic. Descending from most general to most specific:
1. **Category Power** — growth from being in a category the world is adopting.
2. **Company Power** — growth from partners routing business to you (gravitational pull).
3. **Market Power** — growth from being in the hot *segments* within a category at the right time.
4. **Offer Power** — growth from a demonstrably superior offer vs. alternatives.
5. **Execution Power** — growth from strategic initiatives taken past the tipping point.

An investor evaluates top-down; an operator often over-weights offer + execution and under-weights category + company.

> "Contemporary management protocols and practices overweight a focus on performance and underweight a focus on power. The primary purpose of this book is to rectify the balance by creating a vocabulary and a context for addressing the current and future state of power of your enterprise, and driving innovation initiatives to ensure that power is replenished instead of exhausted." — Moore, *Escape Velocity* (via LinkedIn post, Aug 22, 2014). https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/20140822002750-649711-geoffrey-moore-escape-velocity

### Zone to Win — 4 zones (2015 book)
Organizational operating model for incumbents managing disruption. Four zones, each with a distinct operating model, funding logic, KPI structure:
1. **Performance Zone** — the revenue-producing lines of business. "Make the plan, make the numbers, report to the board."
2. **Productivity Zone** — shared services (IT, HR, marketing, customer success). Process-oriented — onboarding, NPS, journeys.
3. **Incubation Zone** — venture-style trial balloons for disruptive innovations. Funded on VC-style milestones; explicitly *exempt* from Performance / Productivity metrics.
4. **Transformation Zone** — the specific bet chosen from Incubation and scaled aggressively to either (a) defend against a disruptor or (b) create a new performance line ≥10% of company revenue.

> "Transformation is hard. You should invoke the Transformation Zone rarely." — Moore, Gainsight Pulse 2022 (paraphrased across the Diginomica coverage). https://diginomica.com/gainsight-pulse-2022-geoffrey-moore-maturity-model-customer-success

Full 4-zone summary via Gainsight: https://www.gainsight.com/blog/geoffrey-moore-applies-his-four-zones-to-customer-success/

### Core vs. Context (from *Dealing with Darwin*, 2005)
Innovation-portfolio filter. **Core** = work that drives your differentiation vs. competition; **Context** = everything else, however necessary. Rule: transfer resources *from* context *to* core continuously — including via outsourcing or automation. Three innovation types:
- **Differentiation** — creates competitive separation.
- **Neutralization** — closes a competitive gap to reach parity.
- **Optimization** — makes existing processes more efficient.

Core-context is the operational sibling of the Hierarchy of Powers.

### Digital Systems Maturity Model (LinkedIn Pulse, May 2017)
Sequential "stairway" for enterprise digital investment. Sequence matters — no leapfrogging:
1. **Systems of Record** (ERP, CRM)
2. **Systems of Engagement** (mobile, omni-channel)
3. **Engagement Analytics** (dashboards)
4. **Systems of Intelligence** (ML-driven)
5. **Systems of Disruption** (proprietary insight → new market entry)

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**Beachhead sizing — the two-part test:**
- **Big enough to matter** — Moore's operational floor on Lenny (Jan 2024): capable of $100M revenue within 5 years.
- **Small enough to lead** — you must plausibly dominate it, not just play in it. If the largest incumbent already owns >30% you're not going to displace them from a beachhead position.
- **Big-enough alone is a trap** — chasing a huge segment guarantees dissipation of force.

**The whole-product test — pragmatist edition.** A pragmatist buys only when the whole product (integrations, services, references, training, ecosystem) is *there* — not "on the roadmap." If any component of the whole product is missing, the pragmatist will either wait or write you off. Ship the whole product for one segment before you ship a partial product for many.

**Sequencing rule:** Bowling Alley precedes Tornado precedes Main Street. Skipping stages is fatal:
- If you try to Tornado before a Bowling Alley base, you cannot ship whole product at scale.
- If you try Main Street plays (customer intimacy, retention) during a Tornado, competitors take share.
- If you keep Bowling Alley plays during a Tornado, you cede horizontal share to a rival who standardized faster.

**Mixing playbooks kills the campaign.** Named explicitly on Lenny (Jan 2024). One stage, one playbook, one segment. "Mixing playbooks undermines effectiveness—teams must commit to one stage's approach consistently."

**Discounting warning (Lenny 2024).** Cutting price does not reduce a pragmatist's perceived risk. Worse: it usually forces the vendor to cut corners on scope or support, which *increases* risk. "You don't need to discount your product or service, but you do need to make a guaranteed commitment to solving the problem." — Lenny 2024.

**Marquee / lighthouse customer discipline.** One prominent reference customer in your beachhead vertical is worth ten mid-tier logos. "Getting a lighthouse customer can put a company on the map and attract attention." — Lenny 2024.

**Positioning is the largest single influence on the buying decision** (his direct claim). Positioning is per-segment: a Bowling Alley positioning statement and a Tornado positioning statement should look different, because the audience psychographic is different.

**Bowling-pin adjacency logic.** The next pin should be knock-downable by *the current pin's references*. If a new segment can't use your existing case studies, it isn't an adjacent pin — it's a second beachhead you can't afford.

**"After the chasm" trap — going too broad too fast.** Moore's 2024 post is explicit: don't try to sell to a CFO or become a platform prematurely. Stay departmental. Ally with a process owner with a broken process. Grow adjacency by adjacency.

**Zone to Win — anti-pattern: running Transformation as a rounding error.** A Transformation must be scaled aggressively enough to *matter* — Moore's threshold is ≥10% of company revenue. Anything less is Incubation dressed up. Related anti-pattern: funding Transformation from Performance Zone's operating budget (kills both).

**Zone to Win — anti-pattern: metricizing Incubation like Performance.** Incubation must be run on VC-style milestones, not P&L. Applying revenue-growth KPIs kills the experiments before they show signal.

**Core / Context anti-patterns.**
- Treating context like core (over-investing in what doesn't differentiate).
- Failing to *repatriate* labor: context work should be automated or outsourced continuously to free capacity for core. Moore is unusually blunt: liberating core from context is the CEO's job, not a middle-management optimization.

**AI-era specific gotchas (Moore, 2024–2026):**
- *AI as a category* has already crossed the chasm for narrow, high-value use cases (HFT, ad placement, regulatory compliance). Don't confuse category chasm with *use-case* chasm.
- Agentic AI **has not yet crossed** the chasm as of Feb 2026 — beachheads (coding, customer service) exist but "neither possesses enough trapped value in the process to cause the complete takeover." — Moore, Diginomica Feb 6, 2026.
- Enterprise AI adoption pattern: "one more like the Apple iPhone, less like The World Wide Web" — faster than historical enterprise-software adoption due to LLM capex and integration ease. Forbes 2024.
- **Trapped value** is the deciding variable — if the current process doesn't accumulate enough pain, agentic AI will not displace it, however cool the demo.

**Common misreadings and gotchas — how skill authors get Moore wrong:**
- **Confusing TALC with a marketing funnel.** TALC is a market-adoption bell curve, not a per-customer buying-stage funnel. Different customers *are* different segments; a single customer does not "move through TALC."
- **Assuming pragmatists become visionaries with better copy.** They don't. Pragmatism is a stable disposition, not a copy problem.
- **Chasing every visionary deal because "logos matter."** Visionary logos convince other visionaries, not pragmatists — pragmatists discount visionary references entirely.
- **Building a platform pre-chasm.** Moore is explicit: pre-chasm you're a departmental point product. Platform ambition here just splits scarce whole-product investment.
- **Treating Zone to Win as an org chart.** It's an operating-model overlay, not a re-org. Same employees can serve multiple zones; the *rules of engagement* differ per zone.
- **Using the 1991 Blockbuster/HP examples in a 2020s deck.** Moore rewrote most cases for the 3rd ed (2014). Default to Salesforce, Documentum, Aruba, Workday, VMware, Mozilla, Lithium.

**"The most conservative buyer is the pragmatist looking sideways at another pragmatist."** — commonly attributed, aligned with his framework; not verifiable as a direct book quote in search. Flag as *paraphrase in the spirit of Moore* if quoted.

## 7. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**vs. Everett Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations (1962):**
- Rogers = academic diffusion theory (the bell curve, the 5 adopter segments, the S-curve).
- Moore = Rogers *plus* the chasm (a discontinuity Rogers did not name) *plus* practitioner playbooks (D-Day, Bowling Alley, Tornado).
- Skill note: attribute the segment names to Rogers, the chasm and playbooks to Moore.

**vs. April Dunford — Obviously Awesome / Sales Pitch:**
- Dunford *builds on* Moore. Her book explicitly identifies "positioning change is often needed to cross the chasm" and treats her 5-component positioning method as the missing operational recipe.
- Moore says *what* has to happen (segment, position, whole-product); Dunford says *how* to actually re-position when the market has moved. Skill cross-link: `[[obviously-awesome]]`.
- Both operate on beachheads. Both are practitioner-first, not academic.

**vs. Play Bigger — category creation (Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, Maney, 2016):**
- Play Bigger reframes Moore for the modern SaaS category-king era: same TALC bones, but the "category king" thesis (winner takes 76% of category economics) generalizes Moore's Gorilla concept.
- Moore treats category-creation as one path; Play Bigger insists it is the *only* path worth pursuing.

**vs. Brian Balfour — Four Fits:**
- Four Fits (product-market, market-model, model-channel, channel-product) is a *later-stage* growth-loop framework — assumes you are past chasm and building the growth engine.
- Moore's TALC lens is upstream: are you even the right *type* of business for a growth-loop model at this stage? Skill cross-link: `[[four-fits]]`.

**vs. Hamilton Helmer — 7 Powers:**
- Different lens. Helmer = *why does this business persist in earning above-average returns?* (moats). Moore = *how does this category evolve, and where is this business in its arc?* (adoption).
- 7 Powers is largely stage-agnostic; TALC is stage-explicit.
- Complementary: Helmer's Counter-Positioning maps well onto Moore's Bowling Alley / early-tornado plays where the incumbent can't respond without cannibalizing.
- Skill cross-link: `[[7-powers]]`.

**vs. Clayton Christensen — The Innovator's Dilemma / Disruption:**
- Christensen: incumbents fail because rational resource-allocation systems reject low-margin disruptive tech.
- Moore: startups fail because they can't move the *pragmatist* mainstream, regardless of the incumbent's behavior.
- They address different failure modes in the same historical cases (e.g. steel mini-mills, disk drives) but from opposite sides of the market.
- Zone to Win is Moore's *incumbent* answer that partially overlaps with Christensen's autonomous-unit prescription — but Moore is more prescriptive about organizational metrics and funding.

**vs. Roger Martin — Playing to Win:**
- Martin's cascade (winning aspiration / where to play / how to win / capabilities / management systems) is a *making* framework.
- Moore's TALC/CMLC is a *timing and staging* framework the cascade should be conditioned on.
- Where Martin's cascade says "where to play," Moore forces the answer to name a psychographic segment + a life-cycle stage.
- Skill cross-link: `[[playing-to-win]]`.

**vs. Richard Rumelt — Good Strategy / Bad Strategy / The Crux:**
- Rumelt = diagnosis-first (what's the pivotal challenge?).
- Moore = staging-first (where are we in the adoption arc, and what's the corresponding playbook?).
- Complementary: Rumelt tells you *what problem to solve*; Moore tells you *which playbook the market currently rewards*.
- Skill cross-link: `[[good-strategy-bad-strategy]]`.

**vs. Andy Raskin — Strategic Narrative:**
- Raskin's "Name a Big Relevant Change → Winners & Losers → Promised Land → Magic Gifts → Evidence" is a narrative shell that maps naturally onto Moore's Compelling Reason to Buy for a pragmatist audience.
- Skill cross-link: `[[strategic-narrative]]`.

**vs. Ted Levitt (Moore's antecedent):**
- Whole Product is Moore's adaptation of Levitt's generic/expected/augmented/potential product model.
- Levitt's "people don't buy drills, they buy holes" is invoked directly in Moore's "customers buy outcomes" post (2024).

**1. Freshest primary text to reconstruct current voice.** In order of recency and voice-fidelity:

- **Forbes / Randy Bean, Jul 14, 2024** — dense with direct AI-era quotes.

For pre-AI-era voice fidelity, the 3rd ed *Crossing the Chasm* (2014) prose is canonical. For his most operational voice, the *Inside the Tornado* stage-reversal doctrine is his sharpest single formulation.

**2. Handling 1991 vs 2014 canon — always default to the 3rd edition.**
- The 3rd ed (2014) is the operational canon. When a skill needs to name an example, prefer Salesforce, Documentum, Aruba, Workday, VMware, Mozilla, Lithium, Infusion over the 1991-era HP / Oracle / Blockbuster cases.
- The 1991 core *framework* (TALC, chasm, whole product, D-Day) is unchanged in the 3rd ed. Only the case bank is refreshed.
- If a skill exemplifies with a 1991-era case, note it explicitly ("original 1991 example") so downstream users don't assume it's Moore's current preferred illustration.
- Moore adds — but does not subtract — from the framework across books. Zone to Win / Escape Velocity / Hierarchy of Powers extend, they do not replace, the chasm playbook. Present them as *organization-scale complements* to the *category-scale* TALC/chasm model.
- For AI-era commentary, Moore's *own* essays (LinkedIn Pulse + blog) are far more reliable than third-party summaries applying his framework — many secondary posts misuse his terminology (e.g., "generative AI has crossed the chasm" claims that don't match Moore's own use-case-by-use-case distinction).
