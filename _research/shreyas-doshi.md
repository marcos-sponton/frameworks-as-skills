## 2. METHOD IN DETAIL

Shreyas does not have a single unified method. He has a constellation of distinct frameworks, each addressing a specific PM challenge. What unifies them is a worldview: product management is about judgment under ambiguity, not process compliance. Below is each major framework in his own terms.

### The LNO Effectiveness Framework

**Source**: Twitter thread (Jun 2021), Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2022).

The framework that Shreyas says "improved the quality of my life as a PM & my work more than anything else I've encountered."

All tasks are not created equal. There are three types:
- **Leverage (L) tasks**: Create 10x to 100x returns. Give these your best effort. Perfectionism is welcome here.
- **Neutral (N) tasks**: Create roughly 1x return. Required functions done at standard efficiency. Time-intensive but not ability-intensive.
- **Overhead (O) tasks**: Less than 1x impact for effort. Must be completed but don't require high quality. Done is better than perfect.

**How to apply**: Prefix every item on your to-do list with L, N, or O. Then allocate time and energy accordingly. The healthy distribution target is roughly 40% L / 35% N / 20% O. Most PMs discover they spend disproportionate effort on N and O tasks.

**Decision formula**: (Value created - Cost of your time) / Time taken. The key question shifts from "Am I doing the right thing?" to "Is this the best thing I could be doing?"

### Pre-mortems (Tigers, Paper Tigers, Elephants)

**Source**: Twitter thread (Jan 2021), Medium essay, Coda/Superhuman Docs template, Lenny's Podcast.

Shreyas modified the standard pre-mortem (Gary Klein, HBR) because "the standard pre-mortem meeting was very engaging while the team was in the room. But I frequently saw everyone forget about the progress made in the meeting."

His innovation: a metaphorical lexicon that creates psychological safety and stickiness:
- **Tiger**: A clear threat that will hurt if we don't act. Real, significant, requires mitigation.
- **Paper Tiger**: An ostensible threat that you personally are not worried about (but others might be). Important to identify to avoid panic and misallocation.
- **Elephant**: The thing you're worried the team is not talking about. The uncomfortable truth being avoided.

**One-hour meeting structure**:
1. Kickoff & prompt (10 min) -- transport the team 3 months forward, assume spectacular failure
2. Quiet brainstorming (10 min) -- each person writes at least 2 Tigers + any Paper Tigers and Elephants
3. Quiet voting & group discussion (30 min) -- review threats, vote with up to 5 "+1s", share reflections
4. Draft action plan (10 min) -- leader shares top themes and next steps

**Post-meeting**: Leader creates an action plan document with top 3-5 Tigers and Elephants, verbatim threats, proposed mitigating actions, and DRIs (Directly Responsible Individuals).

**Timing**: One to three months before launch.

### Three Levels of Product Work (Impact, Execution, Optics)

**Source**: Twitter thread (Aug 2021), Lenny's Podcast, Knowledge Project #175, Amplitude conversation.

Shreyas calls this "a very fundamental framework that spans most product contexts."

- **Impact**: The long-term brand and business consequences. C-level thinking. What matters strategically.
- **Execution**: The tactical delivery. Day-to-day effort. PM-level detail.
- **Optics**: How success is communicated and perceived. Building awareness and internal support.

**Key insight**: Most organizational conflict arises because team members are operating at different levels without recognizing it. "If you come into a product review as the product manager and you drown the group in execution-level detail... you are going to lose the audience." The trick is figuring out at which level you ought to communicate with a given stakeholder.

### High Agency

**Source**: Twitter thread (Jun 2020), Knowledge Project #175.

High Agency is "finding a way to get what you want, without waiting for conditions to be perfect or otherwise blaming the circumstances." Not aggression -- resourcefulness.

**The Agency-Talent Matrix**:
- **Game Changers**: Highly talented + high agency. Rare and expensive.
- **Frustrated Geniuses**: Talented but low agency. Eventually capitulate to systemic pressures.
- **Go-Getters**: Can transition to game-changer status through developing high agency.

Hiring wisdom: "Given a choice between hiring a frustrated genius and hiring a go-getter, if it is a role that has a high degree of ambiguity, I would rather hire the go-getter." But: "Never compromise on Integrity, not even for Game Changers."

**How to cultivate**: (1) Study Circle of Influence vs Circle of Concern (7 Habits). (2) Monitor self-talk -- language patterns reveal and reinforce agency levels. (3) Develop an ownership mindset.

### Execution Problems Are Strategy Problems

**Source**: Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2022).

Most things that look like execution problems are actually strategy or culture problems in disguise. Before assuming your team has an execution issue, investigate: Is the strategy unclear? Are teams misaligned? Are incentive structures working against the desired outcome?

### Opportunity Cost vs ROI Thinking

**Source**: Twitter thread (Jun 2021), Lenny's Podcast.

"Opportunity Cost = Value of optimal option - Value of chosen option."

The problem with ROI thinking: in a high-leverage role, hundreds of things provide positive ROI, so ROI doesn't differentiate. People with an ROI mindset naturally gravitate toward decreasing cost -- a task with tiny cost will have high ROI even if it creates low value.

When you reprogram to think in opportunity cost, you instinctively explore very high-value alternatives you wouldn't have considered otherwise.

### Three Types of Product Leaders (Operator, Craftsperson, Visionary)

**Source**: Twitter thread (Mar 2021), 20 Product podcast.

Not career levels but operating modes:
- **The Operator**: "It's about scale." Skilled at scaling teams and managing alignment.
- **The Craftsperson**: "It's all about the product." Driven by product insight and customer understanding.
- **The Visionary**: "It's about the future." Gifted at imagining what's next.

Everyone has a preference, and that preference influences everything. Founders should choose hires based on their startup's stage and needs.

### Customer Problems Stack Rank (CPSR)

**Source**: Twitter thread (May 2020), OpinionX blog.

Addresses the Focusing Illusion -- the tendency for customers to amplify the importance of whatever you're currently discussing. Instead of asking "how important is this problem?", ask customers to stack rank it against all other problems they face.

Get the CPSR from multiple personas (VP Support, VP Marketing, VP Sales). The CPSR acts as "a great test of the TRUE priority of the customer problem & the product solution you're discussing."

### The Antithesis Principle

**Source**: Substack essay (Jul 2026), Knowledge Project #175.

Truths about human nature can be viewed two ways: pointed outward (tactics for influencing others) and pointed inward (warnings about defaults to eliminate in yourself). "Everyone who's smart finds the outward tactic. Only the wise see the inward antithesis."

Examples:
- Outward: Make content entertaining to help others learn. Inward: Develop ability to learn from un-entertaining content.
- Outward: Cultivate charisma. Inward: Build resistance to being swayed by others' charisma.
- Outward: Be a good manager. Inward: "You should turn yourself into a person who can do really good work even without having a great manager."

### Product Sense

**Source**: Maven course, Substack essay (Mar 2026).

"The ability to usually make correct product decisions, both macro & micro, even in the face of major ambiguity." A learnable skill, not innate talent.

Five components: (1) Strong empathy, (2) Excellent simulation skills, (3) Stellar strategic thinking, (4) Great taste, (5) Creative execution.

Key argument for the AI age: "The only real long-term career moat for product people is how you can improve on the already-brilliant inputs and outputs that AI will provide for you." Tools have never been a significant source of alpha.

### The Focusing Illusion (in product context)

**Source**: Twitter threads, OpinionX blog.

Success = quality execution x problem-solution fit x problem importance. Most teams nail the first two and miss the third. The antidote: stack rank problems holistically via CPSR. "Nobody goes out of their way to find solutions for a mild inconvenience."

## 3. VOICE AND TONE

**Register**: Direct, warm, collegial. Thread-native -- his ideas were built for Twitter's constraints, which gives them a punchy, numbered-point cadence even when expanded to essay form.

**Rhetorical signature moves**:
- **The numbering move**: Almost every framework arrives as a numbered thread (3 types, 5 components, 4 questions). The numbers aren't arbitrary -- they constrain and sharpen.
- **The lowercase confession**: Often starts from a personal admission ("this framework improved the quality of my life as a PM more than anything else I've encountered") before universalizing.
- **The naming move**: Creates sticky vocabulary -- Tigers/Paper Tigers/Elephants, LNO, Game Changers/Frustrated Geniuses/Go-Getters. The labels do the teaching.
- **The "simple, but not easy" closer**: Acknowledges that his frameworks look simple and the difficulty is in applying them consistently.
- **The direct imperative**: "Never compromise on Integrity, not even for Game Changers." Short sentences for maximum weight.
- **The reader-empowerment move**: "People should think for themselves." "The words above are a mere prompt for you to extract the truth that already lies within you."
- **The self-counter**: Frequently preempts criticism ("don't believe everything I write," "don't take everything and just implement it").

**Signature vocabulary**: high agency, leverage tasks, game changer, frustrated genius, product sense, taste, optics, the elephant in the room, focusing illusion, opportunity cost, pre-mortem, Tigers.

**What he does NOT do**:
- Academic jargon or citation-heavy prose. He references sources (Gary Klein for pre-mortems, Covey for Circle of Influence) but doesn't write like an academic.
- Cynicism or snark. Even when calling out bad behavior ("Route Around Big Egos"), the tone is pragmatic, not contemptuous.
- Excessive qualification. He states positions with conviction and invites disagreement rather than hedging everything.

**How he teaches**: Through concrete examples, metaphorical naming (Tigers!), and personal experience at Stripe/Google/Twitter. He rarely uses abstract theory without immediately grounding it in a recognizable PM scenario.

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO'S / DON'TS / GOTCHAS

### Do's

1. **Prefix your to-do list with L, N, or O** before starting each day. The classification itself changes behavior. (Source: LNO thread)
2. **Run a pre-mortem 1-3 months before launch**, not the week before. You need response time. (Source: Pre-mortem template)
3. **Ask "is this the best thing I could be doing?" not "is this worth doing?"** The second question accepts ROI logic; the first forces opportunity cost thinking. (Source: Opportunity Cost thread)
4. **Match the communication level to the stakeholder.** C-suite wants Impact; team wants Execution. Drowning executives in execution detail loses the audience. (Source: Three Levels)
5. **Stack rank customer problems holistically**, not in isolation. The Focusing Illusion makes every problem seem important when you discuss it alone. (Source: CPSR thread)
6. **Create sticky vocabulary for risks.** "I recently thought of a Tiger" is more natural and less pessimistic than "I have a concern about the project." (Source: Pre-mortem template)
7. **Hire go-getters over frustrated geniuses** for ambiguous roles. Agency beats raw talent when conditions are imperfect. (Source: High Agency thread)
8. **Point the truth inward, not just outward.** Before using an insight to influence others, ask what it means about your own defaults. (Source: Antithesis Principle)

### Don'ts

1. **Don't perfectionism-spray across all tasks.** Perfectionism on O tasks is waste. Save it for L tasks. (Source: LNO thread)
2. **Don't run a standard pre-mortem without the Tiger/Paper Tiger/Elephant labels.** Without the lexicon, people forget the meeting's output within days. (Source: Pre-mortem Medium essay)
3. **Don't confuse ROI with opportunity cost.** A task with tiny cost and low value has infinite ROI but near-zero importance. (Source: Opportunity Cost thread)
4. **Don't assume execution problems are execution problems.** Investigate whether the real issue is strategy clarity, team alignment, or incentive misalignment. (Source: Lenny's Podcast)
5. **Don't discuss a customer problem in isolation.** Ask them to rank it against everything else they face. (Source: CPSR thread)
6. **Don't chase humility as a goal.** "Chasing humility will surely keep you busy, but it is pointless." It perpetuates the status game it claims to escape. (Source: Humility Trap essay)
7. **Don't believe "bias for action" uncritically.** "Speed does not equal velocity. If you speed in the wrong direction, it only takes you farther from where you want to be." Weigh total research costs against total build costs. (Source: 5 Big Ideas)
8. **Don't delegate negotiation to combative PM-vs-PM dynamics.** Route critical alignment through EMs, TLs, or Designers when ego is in the way. (Source: Route Around Big Egos essay)

### Gotchas

- **LNO classifications shift.** A task that was Leverage last week can become Neutral this week. Reassess regularly.
- **Pre-mortems can become theater.** If the leader doesn't create the follow-up action plan with DRIs and verbatim threats, the meeting was performative.
- **"High agency" is not "bulldoze everyone."** Shreyas explicitly separates resourcefulness from aggression. Agency without integrity is dangerous.
- **Product Sense sounds vague but Shreyas defines it precisely.** Five components: empathy, simulation, strategic thinking, taste, creative execution. If someone claims "product sense" without these, they're hand-waving.
- **The Three Levels can be misused to dismiss execution work.** Shreyas doesn't rank Impact > Execution > Optics. They're different planes of operation, not a hierarchy.

## 5. REAL CASES

Shreyas draws primarily on his own experience at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo, plus patterns from coaching hundreds of PMs. He uses cases differently from book authors -- less "here's Apple's story" and more "here's what happened when I was at Stripe."

### Stripe pre-mortem culture
Shreyas introduced pre-mortems at Stripe, where they became part of the product culture. The Tigers/Paper Tigers/Elephants vocabulary stuck because team members could naturally reference risks in everyday conversation ("I recently thought of a Tiger") without sounding alarmist. (Source: Lenny's Podcast, Pre-mortem template)

### The PM who perfected overhead tasks (LNO)
Shreyas describes the pattern of a PM who spends enormous energy on meeting notes, workspace organization, and status updates -- all Overhead tasks -- and wonders why their impact reviews are disappointing. The LNO realization: they were applying Leverage-level effort to Overhead tasks. (Source: LNO thread, Lenny's Podcast)

### The B2B discovery story (CPSR)
A visual story thread where a PM discovers that the problem they'd been building for ranked dead last when customers stack-ranked all their problems. The pivot to the top-ranked problems led to multiple paying customers within a week and 3x landing page conversion improvement. (Source: B2B PM Story thread, OpinionX blog)

### Low Agency Bob vs High Agency Alice
A recurring teaching device: Bob externalizes problems ("that's someone else's responsibility"), Alice focuses on controllable factors and personal responsibility. Same talent, radically different impact. (Source: High Agency thread)

### Two combative PMs (Route Around Big Egos)
When two type-A PMs negotiate directly, organizational results suffer because each prioritizes winning the argument. The solution: route alignment through EMs or TLs who care less about status and more about outcomes. "The smarter the person, the better the excuse." (Source: Route Around Big Egos essay)

### Product review gone wrong (Three Levels)
A PM drowns the executive team in execution-level detail during a product review. The executives want Impact-level discussion. Neither side understands the mismatch. Shreyas's diagnosis: "Oftentimes conflict between people and teams arises mainly because we're not talking at the same level." (Source: Knowledge Project #175)

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

### What Shreyas endorses/references
- **Gary Klein's pre-mortem** (HBR): Shreyas built directly on Klein's concept, adding the Tigers/Paper Tigers/Elephants vocabulary and the structured one-hour format.
- **Stephen Covey's Circle of Influence / Circle of Concern** (7 Habits): Explicitly recommends this for cultivating high agency.
- **15 Commitments of Conscious Leadership** ("To Me" vs "By Me" thinking): References as another frame for understanding agency.
- **Rick Rubin, Dee Hock, Peter Drucker on listening**: Referenced in the "Am I really listening?" question.
- **Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers**: Referenced in the Product Sense essay for competitive advantage analysis.

### What composes well with Shreyas
- **Teresa Torres's Continuous Discovery Habits**: The CPSR framework is a natural complement to Torres's opportunity-first discovery. Stack rank before you solution.
- **Marty Cagan's Empowered / Inspired**: Shreyas's three types of product leaders can be mapped onto Cagan's empowered-team model. Both emphasize agency and judgment over process.
- **Rumelt's Good Strategy Bad Strategy**: The "execution problems are strategy problems" insight is Rumelt-adjacent. Both insist on diagnosis before action.
- **Playing to Win (Roger Martin)**: The Three Levels framework (Impact/Execution/Optics) maps loosely onto Martin's "where to play / how to win" at the Impact level.

### Where Shreyas diverges
- **Process-heavy PM frameworks** (SAFe, Scrum-as-methodology): Shreyas explicitly values judgment over process. His frameworks are mental models, not ceremonies.
- **OKR orthodoxy**: While not anti-OKR, Shreyas's opportunity cost thinking challenges the ROI logic that underpins most OKR-setting exercises.
- **"Move fast and break things" culture**: His emphasis on pre-mortems, taste, and "speed is not velocity" pushes back against uncritical bias for action.
