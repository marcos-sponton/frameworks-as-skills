## 2. THE METHOD IN DETAIL

*Scaling People* is organized as a **reference book**, not a linear read. CHJ says explicitly: "The book is designed so you can go into the table of contents and not expect to read it cover to cover, allowing you to look up a topic and consult that chapter or section." Over 100 pages are worksheets, templates, exercises, and example documents.

### The 4 Essential Operating Principles (Chapter 1)

These are the load-bearing meta-principles that run through the whole book. CHJ frames them as pre-requisite to any specific management tactic.

1. **Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness.** *The most important of the four.* Self-awareness has three components: (a) understanding your underlying value system, (b) identifying your innate preferences — your work style and decision-making tendencies, (c) being clear about your own skills and capability gaps. Tools she recommends: Myers-Briggs, Enneagram, DISC, StrengthsFinder, Hogan, and the "energy map" (track good days and bad days on a calendar for a month to see which activities enhance or drain performance). The point of assessments is not the taxonomy — it's articulating your defaults so others can work with you.

2. **Say the thing you think you cannot say.** Adapted from Fred Kofman's "detoxify the left-hand column" (from *Conscious Business*). In every meeting, there is something people are thinking that no one is saying. Naming it early prevents a compounding tax of unsaid tension. The framing move: separate the person from the "it" — the task, work, or issue. *"I'm not criticizing you, I'm criticizing the communication."* CHJ took 10 years to get comfortable doing this — she treats it as a learnable skill, not a personality trait.

3. **Distinguish between management and leadership.** Two different jobs, both required. Management is the knowable process from A to B — stability, structure, execution, "creating stability" (Marty Linsky's phrasing, which CHJ endorses). Leadership is driving change — vision, setting standards you don't know are achievable yet, pulling the organization forward. Most managers are asked to do both. Confusing them produces bad outcomes: managers who mistake themselves for leaders skip the execution work; leaders who mistake themselves for managers stop pulling.

4. **Come back to the operating system.** The company's operating system (mission, values, planning cadence, decision rights, review rhythms) is not a one-time setup. It's the thing you return to every quarter, every re-org, every crisis. When something feels off, the diagnostic move is: *"Which part of the operating system broke, and how do we return to it?"* — not *"who screwed up?"*

### Foundations and Planning (Chapter 2)

**Founding documents (the "guiding papers").** Four components:
- **Mission** — a concise statement of why the company exists. Example: Stripe's *"Increase the GDP of the internet."* CHJ warns not to force a mission statement prematurely — "listen for the crystallization of what you're trying to be" rather than writing one on a whiteboard the first week.
- **Long-term goals** — 3-to-5-year plan; Stripe called them "long-term goals" publicly, not "vision," because the concrete framing forces specificity.
- **Values** — cultural markers, authentic (not aspirational-only). Test: would you fire someone for violating them? If not, they're marketing, not values.
- **Team charters** — team-level documents defining team mission, strategy, customers (internal + external), why the team exists, and what it will and won't do. Kept short. Refreshed annually.

**The operating system.** Three interlocking subsystems:
- **Strategy** — resource allocation decisions. Where you invest and what you say no to.
- **Accountability** — clear responsibility assignment. Who decides, who executes, who is informed.
- **Communication** — candid, accessible information. Meeting notes transparent by default; leadership email cadence; source-of-truth documentation locations.

**Operating cadence.** CHJ's recommended layers, roughly:
- **Charters (founding docs)** — refreshed annually.
- **OKRs / goal-setting** — quarterly or bi-annual. CHJ shifted Stripe from 6-month to quarterly. Her actual view: the framework matters less than "clarity of the goals and the repetition in their communication."
- **Company metrics targets** — annual.
- **Business reviews** — variable cadence per team, driven by team state. Written memos first; narrative quality valued over aesthetics.

**Goal-setting: FOCUS(S)** — CHJ's rubric for whether a goal is well-formed:
- **F**ocus on the most important things
- **O**bjectively assessable (shared understanding of success)
- **C**hallenging but possible (credible and feasible)
- **U**ser-oriented (customer-focused)
- **S**tates, not activities (outcomes over processes)
- **S**ensitivity and specificity (clear success and failure criteria)

**Metrics categories to track separately:**
- Input metrics (leading indicators)
- Operational metrics (current health)
- Strategic metrics (numbers that move over quarters/years, e.g., share of global payment volume)

### A Comprehensive Hiring Approach (Chapter 3)

**Recruiting pipeline:**
- Transparent job descriptions (what will actually be evaluated, what year-one success looks like).
- Employee referrals as first-order signal.
- Target the "forever user" of the company brand (for Stripe: developers) and cluster the hiring brand where they cluster.
- **Bar-raiser / Elevate** style programs to maintain hiring standards as the company scales. The bar-raiser sits on the interview loop but is not the hiring manager — they gatekeep the bar.

**Structured interviews with scorecards:**
- Standardized interview rubric assigned before the loop starts. Each interviewer owns a specific dimension.
- Behavioral questions grounded in specific past situations. Open with something *off* the résumé that isn't recent — reveals depth vs. rehearsed narrative.
- "Peel the onion" — follow-up "why" questions to test introspection.
- CHJ's signature self-awareness question: *"If I interviewed Peter next, what would he say it's like working with you?"*
- Resilience/ownership assessment: ask about hardest assignments; watch whether they take ownership or shift blame. Bonus if they can describe a subsequent situation where they applied the learning.
- **The "learning organism" filter** — is the person curious, do they introspect, do they change trajectory based on feedback? CHJ treats this as the single most predictive attribute for scaling talent.
- Collaborative decision-making — interview committee submits scores; hiring manager makes final call. Manager owns the decision — recruiters are critical but not solely responsible.

**References — do them yourself:**
- *"If you are delegating the references, you're making a mistake."* Diligence, not box-checking.
- Push for recent references: *"Is there someone who left your company recently who could talk about you?"*
- The ranking question: *"Are they top 50%, top 20%, top 10%?"* Follow up: *"Does that mean they're top 5%?"* People reveal truth when asked for specificity.
- The coaching question: *"What can I do as their manager to help them succeed?"* Immediately gets to performance, blind spots, development.
- Prime the referee with context: describe the role, year-one success, capabilities needed. Then: *"Do you think they'll thrive in this?"*
- Ask candidate permission before back-channel calls. Usually back-loaded — avoid rushing due to confirmation bias.

**Executive hiring — the specific failure mode:**
- **Red flag:** an executive hired as a leader who doesn't hire anyone good in their first 3–6 months. Followership doesn't attach.
- CHJ names her own weaker area: exec hiring at Stripe. Her diagnosis: she was "trying to be liked" and didn't push hard enough on diligence.
- Question to ask executive candidates: *"Tell me about your current team and why you chose the structure you have."* Red flag: *"Well, this is the team I got."*
- Common executive-hiring assumption error: *"If you got to be this successful in your career, you're gonna adapt."* You don't know that. Test it.

**Onboarding:**
- Lightweight monthly manager surveys to track ramp productivity.
- A/B testing onboarding improvements requires consistent measurement.
- Under-invested in, high ROI on ramp time.

### Intentional Team Development (Chapter 4)

- **Team structures** — reversible where possible; ship the org chart as an evolving artifact, not a monument.
- **Diagnosing team state** — Tuckman's Forming / Storming / Norming / Performing as the lens. Different agenda for each stage. The offsite toolkit is built around this.
- **Team changes and restructuring** — CHJ's position: *"There's no such thing as an optimal org structure."* Re-orgs every 3–6 months are normal in hypergrowth. Frame as iteration, not resistance.
- **The skill/will matrix** — evaluate direct reports on two axes; delegate strategically. High-consequence + irreversible tasks: manager keeps or pairs on them.
- **Building the team environment** — offsites, regular meetings, team rituals. Assess team state before booking an offsite — they aren't universally appropriate.
- **Diversity and inclusion** — an operational responsibility, not a slogan. The Golden Rule fails for management: *"You have to study people and figure out what gets the best out of them."* Manage different people differently.
- **Team communication** — the meeting hygiene is the culture. Notes transparent, decisions logged, action items owned.

### Feedback and Performance Mechanisms (Chapter 5)

**Hypothesis-based coaching (aka "inquisitive coaching"):**
- Three steps: (1) observe carefully across situations, (2) identify persistent patterns, (3) share tactfully, framed as behavior not character.
- Start with a *hypothesis*, not a verdict. *"Here's what I'm noticing — does this match your experience?"*
- Let the person self-assess first. Creates a growth-mindset frame rather than a defense-mindset frame.
- **"Be an explorer, not a lecturer."** The manager's job is not to tell — it's to explore what happened, why, and what they'll do differently.

**Giving hard feedback:**
- Separate the person from the "it." Judge the work, not the character.
- Direct opening line: *"I am worried this is not working out for you."* Clear, solution-oriented, doesn't hedge.
- Distinguish tactical feedback (on the work) from developmental feedback (on career growth and blocking patterns).
- All feedback is valid, even if incorrect — the fact that someone perceived it that way is data.
- CHJ warns that feedback *"so direct that it feels like an attack"* causes people to stop listening. Her framing: *"From my seat, 10,000–20,000 feet away, I have a couple of things I'm worried about."*

**Managing low performers — the four-phase escalation:**
- **Phase 0:** Pattern recognition (under 3 weeks). Notice the pattern; don't yet act.
- **Phase 1:** Align on challenge (1–3 conversations, ~2 weeks). Name the pattern, hear their perspective.
- **Phase 2:** Agree on next steps (1–2 conversations, ~1 week). Concrete changes and timeline.
- **Phase 3:** Create action plan (1–3 months). Formal improvement plan with milestones; parallel-track backup planning.

**Creating a culture of informal feedback:**
- Frequency > formality. Peer feedback matters more than manager feedback in most workflows.
- Manager solicits feedback on self first, publicly and repeatedly, then delivers up-the-stack.
- *"People do not learn by being told answers... you're going to lead them through learning with you and they are going to get to the answer and you are going to celebrate them doing that."*

**Formal review process:**
- Multi-source (self + peers + manager).
- Data-driven calibration to adjust for bias across managers.
- Explicit link to compensation (with mechanisms to avoid single-manager bias driving comp).

**Compensation:**
- Tied to merit and to the calibrated review, not to negotiation loudness.
- Structural transparency about bands so calibration means something.

### The Personal Operating Manual ("Working with X")

CHJ's most-cited artifact. Not a chapter unto itself — it lives inside the self-awareness chapter and reappears throughout. Structure (from Fortune's write-up + CHJ's Lenny appearance):

**Section 1 — Work preferences (operational):**
- Communication channels per message type (Slack for X, email for Y, doc-in-1:1 for Z).
- Response timeframes for docs, decisions, reviews.
- Contact-method norms (phone/personal-number thresholds).

**Section 2 — Behavioral style:**
- Decision-making approach (default to consensus? To fast call?).
- Behavior under stress.
- Work patterns (morning vs. afternoon deep-work).
- Management style with new projects vs. established work. CHJ's own line: *"I am not a micromanager necessarily, I'm gonna give you space to do your work. But the first time that we're doing something, I will get really into the details."*

**Section 3 — Optional (for new hires):**
- Important personal dates.
- Accomplishments the manager should know about.

CHJ's origin move: she wrote "Working with Claire" at Google as a private doc for her closest reports, then made it open. It spread organically. At Stripe she brought it with her and the pattern became viral inside the company. *"You're actually completely cutting off all of that studying and reading between the lines and jumping right to work."*

### Decision-Making

CHJ does NOT use RAPID or DACI as the primary vocabulary. Her framing:
- Be **explicit about the decision type** upfront. State: *"I'm ultimately making this — I want input"* vs. *"Help me explore options — this is a group decision"* vs. *"Group discussion, no decision yet."*
- Confusion happens when the group thinks they're the decision-maker and the goal is consensus but the leader has already decided.
- She endorses **Gokul Rajaram's SPADE framework** as a compatible tool (Setting, People, Alternatives, Decide, Explain).
- CEO/founder specifically owns: hiring senior leaders, stopping projects, retrenchment decisions. These cannot be delegated.

### Managing Managers (via Chapter 4 + Chapter 5 threads)

- Managers set the terms for how their managers manage. Modeling matters more than telling.
- Skip-levels — CHJ endorses "crashing meetings" pattern (from Zannya Mittenbottles at *The Economist*) — showing up unannounced to listen, not to intervene.
- Build the manager's confidence in their own instinct: *"80 percent of the way there. They're just not confident. My job is to build their confidence, then we brainstorm the last 20 percent."*

### Building & Writing Culture

- **Written communication first.** Business reviews are written memos before they are meetings. Narrative quality > slide aesthetics.
- **Meeting notes transparent by default** — the org compounds trust when the default is open.
- **Team charters are living documents**, not one-time deliverables.
- **"Say the thing you think you cannot say"** is a written-culture principle as much as a verbal one — if you're avoiding writing something down, that's the signal to write it.

## 4. HEURISTICS / DO's / DON'Ts / GOTCHAS

**The 4 Operating Principles as a diagnostic.** When a management situation feels stuck, run through them:
- *Did I do the self-awareness work? Do they know how I work?*
- *Is there something I'm not saying? What's the thing I think I cannot say?*
- *Am I confusing management and leadership? Which one is called for here?*
- *Which piece of the operating system broke — and how do we return to it?*

**Do:** Build the personal operating manual (Working with X) BEFORE you need it. Share with direct reports and cross-functional peers. Update annually.

**Do:** Do references yourself. Ask the ranking question. Ask the coaching question. Prime the referee with the role's context, not just the name.

**Do:** Write everything down. Memos > slides. Meeting notes transparent by default. If you're avoiding writing something, that's the signal to write it.

**Do:** State the decision type upfront. *"I'm making this — I want input"* vs. *"We're deciding together"* vs. *"We're exploring, no decision today."*

**Do:** Make founding documents in this order: mission → long-term goals → values → team charters. Refresh annually.

**Do:** Reward learning-organism traits over pedigree. Learning organisms compound across roles; pedigree does not.

**Do:** Delegate strategically using the skill/will matrix. Keep the high-consequence + irreversible tasks close.

**Do:** Assume re-orgs every 3–6 months during hypergrowth. Frame as iteration, not failure.

**Don't:** Force a mission statement in the first month. *"Listen for the crystallization"* — the honest mission emerges from work, not from a whiteboard exercise.

**Don't:** Hire senior executives on pedigree. Ask them to describe their current team and why they chose the structure — the answer diagnoses whether they develop people or accept status quo.

**Don't:** Assume "if they were successful there, they'll adapt here." Adaptation is not guaranteed. Test it.

**Don't:** Skip the "did you build followership?" test on senior hires. If an executive doesn't bring or attract strong people in 3–6 months, that's the red flag.

**Don't:** Apply the Golden Rule to management. Manage each person by what gets *their* best, not by what would get yours.

**Don't:** Confuse "being nice" with being a leader. CHJ names her own trap ("I was trying to be liked") as the reason her exec-hiring was weaker than her other hiring.

**Don't:** Deliver hard feedback so bluntly it lands as attack. The receiver stops listening. Use the neutral-distance line: *"From my seat, I have a couple of things I'm worried about."*

**Don't:** Let unspoken tension compound. *"Say the thing you think you cannot say"* is a manager's tax-payment tool.

**Gotchas:**
- **Personality assessments as identity.** MBTI/DISC/Enneagram results are *starting points* for self-awareness, not permanent labels. Use them to explain your defaults; don't hide behind them.
- **Template-worship.** The templates are scaffolding. Following them without doing the underlying work (self-awareness, honest feedback, real hiring diligence) produces theatre.
- **Written culture without accountability.** Docs + memos + notes are worthless if decisions don't get logged, revisited, and enforced.
- **The "learning organism" filter used lazily.** Every candidate says they're curious. The test is behavioral: what did they change their mind about, and why, and when?
- **Op-cadence over-engineering.** CHJ's warning: *"You need planning structures earlier than you think"* — but she also warns against premature infrastructure. The heuristic: add structure at the specific moment things break, not preemptively.
- **Confusing self-awareness with public vulnerability.** Self-awareness is internal work you then translate into shared vocabulary (the personal operating manual). It is not "be vulnerable in every meeting."

**The two hardest gaps to close in adults (CHJ's line):**
1. **The victim** — cannot stop attributing outcomes to external factors.
2. **The self-awareness gap** — believes they're the best in the world.

Both are trainable in the interview loop (ask for changes-of-mind, ask for failure ownership) but very hard to remediate post-hire.

- **The Collison brothers' hiring process for Claire.** Extensive multi-round, all-hands Q&A, joint-work sessions (drove to office to write goals together), sent work samples. Example of exec-hiring done well: not selling, investigating a match.
- **"Chief of Business Operations" hire (not COO).** She joined Stripe with a portfolio title that showed respect to the incumbent Billy Alvarado. Portfolio grew over years. Cited as the model for exec-fit conversations.
- **Reid Hoffman on feedback culture** — the "I wouldn't hire you to manage a McDonald's" line from a new head of ops, and Reid's superpower in creating an environment where people aren't afraid to say that.
- **Dan Cohen (Met Museum President)** — Saturday-morning creative work as a leader's non-negotiable input to their day job.
- **Dominique Crème (Michelin-star chef)** — *"I'm strict, but I'm also kind."* High standards without abuse.
- **Zannya Mittenbottles (The Economist)** — "crashing meetings" as a skip-level pattern.
- **Sam Hagood (UCSF Chancellor)** — *"More and more all I can do is influence."*
- **Her father's response to being robbed in Paris** — *"What's our action plan?"* not *"Why me?"* Player-orientation modeled early.

## 6. RELATIONSHIP TO OTHER FRAMEWORKS

**vs. Andy Grove — High Output Management** ([[high-output-management]]):
- CHJ treats Grove as canonical infrastructure. Grove defines the plumbing (1:1s, task-relevant maturity, meeting types, leverage math). CHJ builds on top: the templates that fill Grove's 1:1 slot, the founding docs that give Grove's OKRs their content, the personal operating manual that makes Grove's task-relevant maturity work with a specific human.
- **When Grove is better:** you're designing the manager mechanics from first principles.
- **When CHJ is better:** the mechanics exist and you need the actual templates, docs, and failure-mode diagnostics.
- **Compose:** Grove is the load-bearing structure; *Scaling People* is the furnished interior.

**vs. Kim Scott — Radical Candor** ([[radical-candor]]):
- Overlap: both live in the manager-report feedback space. Both defend against the "just being honest" failure mode.
- **Kim's method** is the 2×2 diagnostic + the confession-first pedagogy. Care Personally is load-bearing.
- **CHJ's method** on feedback is *inquisitive coaching* — observe → identify pattern → share as hypothesis. Less taxonomic than Kim's 2×2, more procedural.
- **When Radical Candor is better:** the specific conversation is the unit of analysis; you need to diagnose whether a piece of feedback landed as care+challenge or as OA/RE.
- **When CHJ is better:** the operating rhythm around feedback is the unit — you need the review process, the informal feedback culture, and the escalation framework for low performers.
- **Compose:** Radical Candor is the *quality* of a single feedback move; CHJ is the *system* that makes many good feedback moves happen.

**vs. Salesforce / Benioff — V2MOM** ([[v2mom]]):
- V2MOM is a specific goal-setting artifact (Vision, Values, Methods, Obstacles, Measures) — Benioff's tool for aligning Salesforce as it scaled.
- CHJ's founding documents (mission + long-term goals + values + team charters) plus FOCUS(S) goal criteria cover the same territory with more decomposition.
- **When V2MOM is better:** you want a single-page alignment tool the whole org can memorize.
- **When CHJ is better:** you want the full doc set with role-specific charters, plus the operating cadence around them.
- **Compose:** run V2MOM as the company-level artifact; use CHJ's team charters at the sub-org level.

**vs. Christina Wodtke — Radical Focus (OKRs)** ([[radical-focus]]):
- Wodtke gives you the OKR mechanic + the weekly cadence (Monday commitments / Friday wins).
- CHJ endorses OKRs, moved Stripe from 6-month to quarterly, but is agnostic on framework: *"the framework matters less than the clarity of the goals and the repetition in their communication."*
- **When Wodtke is better:** you need the specific weekly OKR ritual to make quarterly goals bite.
- **When CHJ is better:** you need OKRs inside a larger operating system (charters + reviews + comp + hiring aligned).
- **Compose:** Wodtke inside CHJ's cadence.

**vs. Amazon / Bezos — Working Backwards** ([[working-backwards]]):
- Working Backwards is Amazon's PR/FAQ-then-build ritual + the 6-page memo culture.
- CHJ's memo-first business-review culture is a direct sibling. She endorses written communication as pre-requisite to good decisions.
- **When Working Backwards is better:** the question is *what product to build*.
- **When CHJ is better:** the question is *how to run the org that builds it*.
- **Compose:** WB is the product-decision ritual; CHJ is the meta-org that repeats WB reliably.

**vs. Fred Kofman — Conscious Business:**
- CHJ explicitly cites Kofman as a source for *victim/player*, *left-hand column detoxification*, and the underlying philosophy of ownership. Read Kofman if you want the philosophical roots.
- CHJ operationalizes Kofman for a working COO: the templates and rituals that make Kofman's principles bite at scale.

**vs. Ben Horowitz — The Hard Thing About Hard Things:**
- Horowitz gives you *war stories* + tactical rules for CEO moments (layoffs, wartime CEO, demoting a friend).
- CHJ gives you the *system that reduces the number of Horowitz-moments* by baking honesty and structure in early.
- CHJ endorses Horowitz's "the struggle" — she extends it: *"the more senior you become, the more creative reality gets at finding ways to beat you up every day."*
- **Compose:** read both; CHJ preempts, Horowitz survives.

**vs. Elad Gil — High Growth Handbook:**
- Same audience (scale-stage operators). Gil's book is a curated interview compilation + tactical playbook; CHJ was interviewed for it.
- CHJ's book is deeper and more system-oriented; Gil's is broader across topics (fundraising, board mgmt, PR, legal).
- **Compose:** Gil for coverage; CHJ for depth on the people-and-operating-system slice.

**vs. Camille Fournier — The Manager's Path:**
- Fournier is engineering-manager-specific. CHJ is function-agnostic.
- Compose in engineering-leader reading lists.

**Two operational notes for the skill builder:**
