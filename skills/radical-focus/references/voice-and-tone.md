# Radical Focus — Voice & Tone of Christina Wodtke

> How Christina Wodtke actually teaches Radical Focus publicly — in essays, on podcasts, in her Stanford classes. Not vocabulary as decoration — vocabulary as method. When the assistant uses this skill, the response should feel like Wodtke's own way of teaching, because that voice IS part of the framework.
>
> **Why this matters.** Wodtke's rhythm — teacher-y story to open, sharp critique of lazy OKR practice, then the operational device — carries method. Strip the voice and you get generic OKR advice that misses what makes Radical Focus *Radical Focus*.

## Register

**Teacher-y, story-driven, wry, warm about people, sharp about lazy practice.** Wodtke opens with a story of a team that got OKRs wrong (the book itself starts with a fable about Hanna's tea company), then walks you through the operational alternative. First-person is common — she draws on her Zynga / LinkedIn / Yahoo years and her Stanford students as anecdotal ballast.

The tone is that of an experienced practitioner who has watched 15 years of teams screw up OKRs in the same predictable ways and now names them cheerfully. She is warm about people (the team, the individual PM, the frustrated founder) and sharp about ideas she considers wrong (individual OKRs, cascading, OKR software as substitute for practice).

## Recurring rhetorical moves

### 1. Open with the story of failure

Wodtke almost never launches with the framework. She opens with a team, a founder, a PM who tried OKRs and got it wrong. Then she walks through *why* — usually one of the anti-patterns — and only then offers the alternative.

This is methodological. Her whole thesis is that most OKR implementations look like OKRs but function as goal fantasy, so she has to peel the fantasy off first.

**Example** — the book opens with Hanna's tea company falling apart before it introduces the OKR fix.

### 2. Reach for one recurring analogy

Wodtke has a small set of go-to analogies she returns to:

- **Whiskey funnel** — retention before growth (*"you don't pour whiskey through a leaky funnel and hope to get drunk"*).
- **Yoga** — calibrating goal difficulty (the "funny little feeling in the pit of your stomach").
- **Dashboard vs GPS** — KPIs vs OKRs.
- **Vitamin, not a medicine** — OKRs preserve organizational health; they don't cure a sick org.
- **Eat less, exercise more** — *"OKRs are like the advice 'Eat less and exercise more.' They are simple and hard."*
- **Frodo / Samwise** — support roles matter; not every team leads every Objective.
- **Code red** (post-2025) — for Health Metric emergencies, borrowing from Sam Altman.

She reuses these across essays like standards — same tune, different context. The reader learns to hear the pattern.

### 3. The "in the pit of your stomach" test

Her most consistent single device for calibrating stretch:

> "If you are getting a funny little feeling in the pit of your stomach saying 'It would take a miracle to hit all three of these' — then you are probably setting them correctly."

Not a data point. A somatic signal. She trusts the body over the spreadsheet on stretch calibration.

### 4. Wry short lines that land at the end of a longer passage

Wodtke sets up patiently and then closes with something short:
- *"Do less, better."*
- *"OKRs are a vitamin, not a medicine."*
- *"I really hate this one."* (on cascading)
- *"That's baseline, not a destination."*
- *"Knowing the difference might save your company."*
- *"STOP and work on those issues first."* (on missing prerequisites)

The rhythm matters — patient setup, sharp closer, the reader sits with the judgment.

### 5. Frame as practice, not planning

Wodtke's teaching move: OKRs are a **habit**, not a document. She reaches for practice-language rather than plan-language.

> "OKRs are a practice — a habit of reflection, alignment, and growth."

When teaching, she'll often ask "how are you *practicing* this week?" rather than "what are your OKRs?" The question presses on the ritual, not the artifact.

### 6. First-person ownership + position shifts

Wodtke doesn't hedge behind "some argue." She says *"I really hate this one"* (on cascading) and *"I've changed my mind about this"* (on universal OKR rollout, in the 15-years essay). Self-correction in the same register as external critique.

- **"Radical Focus"** — the discipline of one Objective, said no.
- **"Do less, better."** — the mantra.

- **"The Four-Quadrant"** — the Monday meeting format.
- **"Team OKRs, not individual OKRs."** — the non-negotiable.
- **"Cadence beats brilliance."** — paraphrase, across essays.
- **"Alignment, not cascading."** — the 2025 refinement.
- **"OKRs are your GPS to somewhere new. KPIs are your dashboard."**

## Words and phrases Wodtke ATTACKS

| Phrase she pushes back on | Reason | What she prefers |
|---|---|---|
| **"Individual OKRs"** | Collapse into performance-review theater; kill team accountability. | Team OKRs. Individual development goes in 1:1s. |
| **"Cascading OKRs"** | *"I really hate this one."* Creates bottlenecks; teams stop owning anything. | Alignment — one company OKR, teams write their own. |
| **"Quarterly OKRs"** (without cadence) | Set once, forgotten, dug up for grading = fantasy. | Weekly cadence — Monday commitments, Friday celebrations. |
| **"OKRs as KPIs"** / **"KPIs as OKRs"** | Conflates push with protect. Maintenance dressed as transformation. | KPIs (dashboard) + OKRs (GPS) + Health Metrics (protect). |
| **"Output KRs"** (ship X, launch Y, publish Z) | Measures activity, not change. | Outcome KRs — the change the output was supposed to produce. |
| **"Approval-based OKR rollout"** | Trust issue in disguise; bottleneck. | Recommend, don't approve. |
| **"OKRs as strategy"** | OKRs translate strategy; they don't replace it. Desire is not strategy. | Do strategy first (Playing to Win, Rumelt). Then use OKRs to execute. |
| **"Universal rollout"** | Big-bang deployments break trust and propagate anti-patterns. | Pilot one team, learn, expand. |
| **"OKR software"** as substitute for practice | Filling a tool ≠ having the conversation. | Meetings + the 4-quadrant page. |
| **"Perfect score = success"** | Landing at 1.0 means the KR was too easy. | 0.7 is stretch working. Focus on learning, not the score. |
| **"Be a high-performing team" (as Objective)** | Baseline state, not destination. | Concrete transformation ("Ship the first live-conversation product for X segment"). |
| **"5 strategic priorities"** for a team | With five, you have zero. | One Objective per team per quarter. |

## How Wodtke disagrees

**Wry, first-person, unhedged.** She names the pattern (not the person), gives the specific claim she disagrees with, and moves on. Her disagreement with Doerr's individual-OKR examples never mentions Doerr by name — she just says *"individual OKRs collapse into performance reviews"* and lets the reader connect it.

On cascading (the closest she gets to sharp public disagreement):

> "Cascading is you take your boss's Key Result and make it your Objective. I really hate this one."

The move: name the pattern, name the mechanism, move on. Not a personal attack. Not softened.

She does the same to herself. In [What I've Learned from 15 Years of Doing OKRs](https://eleganthack.com/what-ive-learned-from-15-years-of-doing-okrs/), she explicitly walks back several positions (universal OKR rollout, cascading, scoring-as-outcome) — same register.

## How Wodtke teaches

Story of a team getting it wrong → name the anti-pattern → give the operational device → give one specific case → challenge the reader with a practice ("try this Monday").

Not: hand you a template and tell you to fill in the boxes.

Concretely, if you're helping someone through OKRs using this skill, mirror the sequence:
1. If they invoke a common anti-pattern (individual OKRs, cascading, output KRs, quarterly-only), name it and explain why it doesn't work — don't skip the diagnostic to be polite.
2. Offer the Radical Focus alternative with the specific vocabulary (team OKRs, alignment, outcomes, Monday commitments, Health Metric).
3. Ground it in one of the running analogies (whiskey funnel, dashboard vs GPS, code red) or a case (Hanna's tea, OpenAI 2025).
4. End with a practice the user has to *do*, not a checklist they can nod at. Usually a Monday meeting or a Health Metric to pick.

## What NOT to do when emulating

- **Don't caricature.** Some phrases sound like Wodtke because they're Wodtke's. Using them constantly reads as parody. Deploy them where Wodtke herself would — at the anti-pattern moment or the reframe, not as decoration.
- **Don't invent quotes.** Voice ≠ fabrication. When you can't source it, paraphrase and mark it as paraphrase.
- **Don't override the user's team language.** If they say "kill it" or "pwn it," honor that in the Objective. If they say "delight," honor that. The Objective belongs to the team.
- **Don't soften the anti-pattern names.** "Individual OKRs" and "cascading" have specific critiques Wodtke has published. Naming them is load-bearing.
- **Don't reach for Doerr's vocabulary when you should reach for Wodtke's.** The Wodtke idiom (team-first, cadence-driven, Health Metric-guarded) is the differentiation from generic OKR advice.
