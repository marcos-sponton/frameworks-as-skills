## Hanna's tea company — the fable in the book

**Where Wodtke uses it:** the first half of *Radical Focus* (2016 / 2nd ed 2021) is a narrative fable about Hanna and Jack, co-founders of a Silicon Valley tea startup, watching their company fall apart before they discover OKRs.

**The situation:** Hanna and Jack raise money, hire fast, ship in every direction, and burn out the team. They have opinions about tea, growth, retention, and features, but no shared way to say what "winning this quarter" means. Their board demands focus. They discover OKRs and — through mistakes — figure out the method Wodtke then teaches in the book's second half.

**How the method applies in the fable:**
- **Objective:** *"Prove Teabee is the go-to premium tea for Silicon Valley."* (Qualitative, inspirational, time-bound, team-actionable.)
- **Key Results:** balanced set (customer acquisition + retention + revenue) with confidence 5/10.
- **Cadence:** Monday commitments + Friday celebrations become the ritual that finally lets the team feel progress.
- **Health Metric:** team burnout — protected while pushing.

**Why the case is instructive:** Wodtke deliberately writes the fable so every anti-pattern shows up. Hanna and Jack try individual OKRs (fail), try cascading (fail), try too many OKRs (fail), and gradually work back to the Radical Focus method. It's the anti-pattern gallery in narrative form.

**Quotable moment (paraphrase from the fable):**
> "We had OKRs. We wrote them down. Then we forgot them until the end of the quarter and asked, 'why didn't they work?' Because we never talked about them."

## OpenAI 2025 "code red" — Health Metric case

**Where Wodtke uses it:** [What Sam Altman Just Taught Us About OKRs (Without Meaning To)](https://eleganthack.com/what-sam-altman-just-taught-us-about-okrs-without-meaning-to/) (Dec 2025) — her clearest single essay on Health Metrics.

**The situation:** OpenAI in 2025 was pushing on multiple product OKRs — advertising, shopping agents, health assistant, the Pulse feature. Model capability + reliability (their Health Metric) started degrading. Altman called a **code red** — paused OKR work, redirected the org to fix the health metric.

**How the method applies:**
- **Objective (implicit):** "Ship the next generation of consumer products on top of the model."
- **Key Results (implicit):** ad revenue, shopping agent adoption, health assistant deployment, Pulse engagement.
- **Health Metric:** model capability + reliability. When it turned red, everything else paused.

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the mechanic of Health Metrics in the wild, at a company everyone in the industry watches. It shows what "protect vs push" looks like when it costs you real OKR progress. Wodtke's read: *"That's Radical Focus in the wild. Knowing the difference might save your company."*

## Wodtke's Stanford students — recurring anecdotal source

**Where Wodtke uses it:** across essays and both podcast appearances (Lenny 2023, Perri 2023). Also in her Stanford course CS177 (Human-Centered Product Management).

**The situation:** Wodtke's Stanford students are usually building or advising on small startups or product teams. She uses them as source material for anecdotes about OKR-writing — where they get the Objective right, where they slip into output KRs, where they add or omit the Health Metric.

**Common Stanford-student mistakes she names:**
- Writing an Objective that sounds strategic ("Be the most innovative team") — she pushes back: baseline, not destination.
- Writing an Objective that's a KPI ("Achieve 95% NPS") — she pushes back: that's what you protect, not push.
- Writing KRs that are outputs ("Ship the beta") — she pushes back: what's the change the beta produces?
- Setting confidence at 8/10 because it feels safe — she pushes back: too easy, tighten.

**Why the cases are instructive:** they show the anti-patterns first-time OKR-writers reliably reproduce, and Wodtke's specific reframes for each.

## LinkedIn early team OKRs

**Where Wodtke uses it:** episodic references across podcast appearances. Wodtke was at LinkedIn during a phase where OKRs were being taken seriously and used well.

**The situation:** LinkedIn used OKRs at team level, not individual level, with weekly rituals resembling what Wodtke would later codify. Teams could point at their Objective and their KRs and know why they were doing what they were doing.

**Why the case is instructive:** Wodtke uses LinkedIn as an example of OKRs working — as counter-evidence to the common "OKRs don't work, look at all these failed rollouts" claim. The rollouts that fail almost always missed the cadence, the team-level scope, or the prerequisites. LinkedIn had all three.

## Zynga — cautionary examples

**Where Wodtke uses it:** several essays and both podcast appearances. Wodtke was at Zynga during its rapid growth and used the experience as source for what NOT to do.

**The situation:** metrics-obsessed culture where the wrong things got measured, gamed, and optimized against the spirit of the product. Wodtke's takeaway: without Health Metrics guarding against gaming, KRs become their own enemy.

**Why the case is instructive:** the origin of Wodtke's insistence on Health Metrics. If you push only on metrics without protecting anything, you build software that hits the KR and destroys the customer relationship.

## "Launch an Awesome MVP" — the canonical KR example

**Where Wodtke uses it:** [The Art of the OKR](https://eleganthack.com/the-art-of-the-okr/) and reused across teaching.

**The situation:** a team is building a new product. First instinct is to write "ship the MVP" as the KR. Wodtke walks them into outcome-based KRs.

**Objective:** *"Launch an Awesome MVP."*

**Key Results (balanced set — this is the pattern to memorize):**
- 40% of users return 2× within one week
- Recommendation score of 8
- 15% conversion

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the canonical output → outcome rewrite. "Ship the MVP" is a task. The three KRs above measure whether the MVP was actually *awesome* — did people come back, would they recommend it, did they convert? Balance across dimensions prevents gaming any one metric.

## "Pwn the direct-to-business coffee retail market in the south bay" — the canonical Objective example

**Where Wodtke uses it:** [The Art of the OKR](https://eleganthack.com/the-art-of-the-okr/).

**Why the case is instructive:**
- Uses the team's own language ("pwn").
- Qualitative and inspirational (someone actually cares).
- Time-bound (one quarter).
- Actionable independently (the team owns direct-to-business coffee sales in the south bay).
- Not a KPI in disguise — it's a transformation goal.

Wodtke uses this as the archetype of a good Objective.

## Cases from Wodtke's books beyond *Radical Focus*

Wodtke has written 2 other books beyond *Radical Focus*. Each contains cases:

- ***The Team That Managed Itself*** (2019, with Martin Eriksson) — extended narrative about a team lead who inherits a broken team and rebuilds it using OKRs, the Team Health Monitor, hiring practices, feedback loops, and coaching. This is the source of the Team Health Monitor concept.
- ***Pencil Me In*** (2017) — visual thinking / sketching; not OKR-focused but part of her teaching context.

If the user asks for cases outside the Radical Focus roster, *The Team That Managed Itself* is the source of truth for team-level dynamics that surround OKR work.
