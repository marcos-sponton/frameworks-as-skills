# Five Dysfunctions — Worked Cases Lencioni Uses Publicly

> The recurring cases Patrick Lencioni returns to across his books, podcast, and talks. His pattern is different from Kim Scott's (who uses real personal failures) or Helmer's (who uses named public companies). Lencioni teaches primarily through **fable characters** — fictional but archetypal executives at fictional companies that the reader can map their own team onto. Real client anecdotes appear in the podcast, usually anonymized.

## The canonical fable characters

### 1. Kathryn Petersen at DecisionTech — the canonical Five Dysfunctions case

**Source:** *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2002).

**The setup:**
- DecisionTech, a fictional Silicon Valley tech startup, two years old, well-funded, cash-burning.
- Kathryn Petersen is brought in as CEO after the founder is removed. She's older, less "tech" than the executive team, and considered an unusual pick.
- The executive team: brilliant individuals, working at cross-purposes, meetings that produce nothing, decisions that don't stick, no one hitting the collective revenue number.
- Every dysfunction in the pyramid is visible on the exec team on Kathryn's first day.

**The arc:**
- Kathryn runs a series of off-sites at Napa Valley resorts.
- Off-site 1: introduces the pyramid concept and the personal-histories exercise. Starts by going first with her own story.
- Off-site 2: names the pyramid explicitly. Runs the team-effectiveness exercise (each member names the most important contribution and the most-needed-behavior-change for every other member).
- Off-site 3: real productive-conflict practice on the team's actual work.
- Fires one executive who refuses to hold the pyramid; the team gets stronger.
- End state: the team is holding the pyramid, hitting collective results, and one member has moved on because the fit wasn't there.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Explaining any layer of the pyramid to a user encountering the framework for the first time.
- Any leadership team off-site scenario — the DecisionTech off-sites are the archetype.
- The "someone refuses to play by the new rules" moment on a leadership team. Kathryn's response (patient, direct, then decisive) is the template.

### 2. Rich O'Connor at Telegraph Partners — organizational health

**Source:** *The Four Obsessions of an Extraordinary Executive* (2000).

**The story:** Rich O'Connor is an executive at Telegraph Partners who obsesses over four disciplines: build a cohesive leadership team, create clarity, overcommunicate clarity, reinforce clarity. Contrasted with a competitor whose leader is smart but obsesses over strategy without health.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Explaining the Four Disciplines (from *The Advantage*, which formalized what Rich embodied).
- Contrasting smart with healthy.

### 3. Casey McDaniel at Yip Software — Death by Meeting

**Source:** *Death by Meeting* (2004).

**The story:** Casey is a founder-CEO whose meetings are boring precisely because he's removed conflict from them. His employees dread meetings; decisions don't happen. A film-industry advisor (Casey has a side hustle in film production) diagnoses the meetings using film-industry principles: every good scene has conflict. Casey redesigns meetings using four types: daily check-in, weekly tactical, monthly strategic, quarterly off-site.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Explaining why meetings are boring — usually because leaders remove conflict, not because meetings are inherently bad.
- The four meeting types as a redesign template.

### 4. Jeff Shanley at Trinity Systems — Silos and cross-functional dysfunction

**Source:** *Silos, Politics and Turf Wars* (2006).

**The story:** Jeff Shanley is a consultant brought in to fix cross-functional dysfunction at Trinity Systems. Departments are at war; each pursuing local metrics; no unifying goal. Jeff introduces the "thematic goal" — a single time-bound rallying point that requires all departments to work together.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Any cross-functional or matrix-team dysfunction.
- The thematic goal as the unifying device.

### 5. Jeff Shanley at Valley Builders — The Ideal Team Player

**Source:** *The Ideal Team Player* (2016). Same character name as Silos — Lencioni reuses names across fables (part of his signature).

**The story:** Jeff has left consulting to take over Valley Builders, his uncle's construction company, after his uncle's illness. The company is stable but the leadership team has a Skillful Politician (Ted Marchbanks — Hungry + Smart, not Humble) whose behavior is poisoning the team. Jeff has to diagnose the humble/hungry/smart pattern and eventually remove Ted.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Any hiring or firing decision, especially the Skillful Politician diagnosis.
- Explaining humble/hungry/smart as a hiring rubric.

### 6. Bull McKay in The Motive

**Source:** *The Motive* (2020).

**The story:** Bull McKay is a first-time CEO who represents the reward-centered leader archetype. He delegates the difficult parts of leadership — developing the leadership team, hard conversations, running great meetings — and gets in trouble. Contrasted with a responsibility-centered peer who mentors him.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Explaining the reward-centered vs. responsibility-centered distinction.
- Any leader who's complaining that leadership is exhausting.

## Real client examples Lencioni references publicly

**Note:** Lencioni is much more anonymous with real cases than Kim Scott or Kalbach. Public references are usually the fictional fable characters. When real client stories appear (in podcast and keynotes), they're anonymized ("we were working with a CEO who...").

**Known named-source references:**

- **Southwest Airlines (Herb Kelleher era)** — repeated example of organizational health at scale. Lencioni cites Southwest as an exemplar in *The Advantage* and podcast episodes.
- **Chick-fil-A** — repeated example of vulnerability-based trust at the executive level and disciplined clarity. Lencioni has worked with them and cites publicly.
- **Consulting anecdotes about Bay Area tech CEOs** (unnamed) — Silicon Valley's classic pathology of confusing smart with healthy. Recurring in podcast.
- **Church / faith-community executive teams** — Lencioni's own Catholic faith background means he does significant work here; stories appear regularly on podcast. The Willow Creek Association engagement is one public thread.
- **Family-business executive teams** — a common client type, especially for The Ideal Team Player content. Valley Builders in the fable is drawn from this pattern.

## The client-anecdote pattern (in podcast)

**Structure of a typical At The Table podcast case:**
1. Cody frames a question (*"Hey Pat, someone wrote in asking about..."*).
2. Pat starts: *"You know, we were working with a CEO who..."* — sets up the situation, no name or industry.
3. Names the dysfunction or framework element that fits (*"That's classic Fear of Conflict."*).
4. Contrasts with what a healthy team would do differently.
5. Names the specific move to fix it.
6. Cody adds a counter-case or extension.

**When constructing a case in Lencioni's register:**
- Set up a specific, ordinary business situation (not extraordinary or dramatic).
- Name the roles (CEO, CFO, VP of Sales) not the people.
- Describe the behavior in plain language (not consulting jargon).
- Name the layer of the pyramid the behavior belongs to.
- Give the specific move to fix it.

## The DecisionTech-shaped archetypal case

When a user brings a scenario that doesn't fit a canonical Lencioni case, construct a DecisionTech-shaped archetype. Structure:

- **Fictional but ordinary company** — startup, mid-market, family business, faith community, department of a larger org.
- **Named roles, not people** — CEO, CFO, VP of Sales, Director of Product.
- **Specific behavior** — "the CFO and the VP of Sales never disagree in the executive meeting."
- **Named pyramid layer** — "that team is stuck at Fear of Conflict."
- **The specific move** — "the CEO's job is to mine for conflict actively — direct question in the meeting: 'CFO, you don't look convinced. What's the objection?'"

Do NOT invent quotes from Lencioni. If constructing a case, don't attribute it to him. Say *"In Lencioni's method, this would be..."* rather than putting words in his mouth.

## When NOT to reach for these cases

- **When the user's situation is legally sensitive** (harassment, discrimination). Don't collapse it into a Lencioni case — direct the user to HR / legal counsel first. The framework informs; it doesn't replace them.
- **When the user is fishing to label a colleague** — don't use the Skillful Politician diagnosis to name a specific real person by extension. The diagnostic is about behavior pattern; if the user is looking for permission to fire someone, redirect to the actual decision criteria.
- **When the situation is a group, not a team** — don't force a DecisionTech-shaped case onto a set of independent contributors. The archetype presumes interdependence.
