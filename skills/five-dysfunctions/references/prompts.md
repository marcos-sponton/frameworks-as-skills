# Five Dysfunctions — Invocation Templates

> Ready-to-adapt prompt shapes for common tasks the user brings to this skill. Not scripts — starting points. The assistant should adapt to what the user actually says.

## 1. "My team never disagrees / meetings are super civil / we're always aligned."

**The shape of the assistant's response:**

1. **Name the pattern.** *"That's a red flag for Fear of Conflict, not a sign of health. Lencioni calls this artificial harmony — meetings look great, and the disagreement is happening in the hallway on Tuesday morning."*
2. **Check the layer below — Trust.** *"Fear of Conflict sits on Absence of Trust. Before we work on getting more disagreement in the room, tell me: when was the last time someone on the team said 'I don't know' or 'I need help' out loud?"*
3. **If Trust is broken — start there, not with conflict.** Mining for conflict on a team stuck at Absence of Trust just produces performative debate.
4. **If Trust is functional — start mining for conflict.** Concrete moves: direct questions in the meeting ("Alex, you don't look convinced, what's the objection?"), rotating devil's advocate, explicit permission-granting.
5. **End with a specific move.** *"At your next leadership meeting, pick one topic where you know two people disagree. Ask the quieter one directly: 'What's your objection to this?' Report back."*

## 2. "How do I know if my team has real trust or just civility?"

**The shape:**

1. **The vulnerability-based trust vs. predictive trust distinction.** Predictive trust = "they'll do what they said." Vulnerability-based trust = "they'll admit when they don't know."
2. **Two diagnostic questions:**
   - *"When was the last time someone on your team said 'I don't know' or 'I need help' out loud, in a meeting?"*
   - *"When was the last time someone (including you) admitted a mistake publicly?"*
3. **If the answers are "I can't remember" — you have predictive trust at best.** The team looks civil and is stuck at Absence of Trust.
4. **The move to build real trust: the leader goes first.** Vulnerability cascades downward. Personal-histories exercise as a start, then ongoing practice.
5. **The trust-theater guard.** *"Whatever you do, don't run a ropes course. If the intervention doesn't show up in Monday's meeting, it wasn't real."*

## 3. "Our decisions never stick. We decide something in the meeting, then everyone re-litigates it in the hallway."

**The shape:**

1. **Name the layer: Lack of Commitment.** *"That's Lack of Commitment. Decisions that don't stick have two causes: either people didn't get heard before the decision (the Conflict layer below is broken), or the decision was ambiguous and everyone left with a different understanding."*
2. **Check the layer below — Conflict.** *"In the meeting where the decision got made, did anyone visibly disagree? Did the dissenters get to say what they were objecting to?"*
3. **If Conflict is broken — fix that first.** Commitment is impossible without productive conflict.
4. **If Conflict is functional but decisions are ambiguous — install the specific summary.** End every meeting with: what was decided, who owns what, by when, what deliberate non-decisions we made.
5. **The buy-in-not-consensus reframe.** *"You don't need everyone to agree. You need everyone to have been heard, and then to commit — including the people who lost the argument. Disagree and commit."*
6. **End with a specific move.** *"At your next leadership meeting, end with a written summary. Read it out loud. Ask: 'Anyone unclear on what we just decided?' Repeat until nobody is."*

## 4. "I'm always the one holding people accountable. I want peer-to-peer accountability. How?"

**The shape:**

1. **Name the current state: Avoidance of Accountability with manager-only enforcement.** *"Manager-only accountability is the default and it's a tell that peer-to-peer isn't happening. You've become the bottleneck for every issue."*
2. **Check the layer below — Commitment.** *"Peers can't hold each other accountable to a plan they never really committed to. Are you sure the team is committed, or is there compliance without commitment?"*
3. **If Commitment is fake — fix that first.** Installing peer accountability on top of fake commitment produces public shaming, not real accountability.
4. **If Commitment is real — install peer accountability rituals.** Team scorecard (shared metrics, reviewed together, peers comment first). Structured commitment cascade (each meeting ends with commitments; next meeting starts by naming what shipped and what didn't). Public retros.
5. **The leader's role changes.** *"You stop being the enforcer. You set the norm that peer accountability is expected. You model it — you hold peers accountable in the room. And you don't rescue peers from uncomfortable conversations with each other."*
6. **End with a specific move.** *"Pick one metric your leadership team is jointly responsible for. Put it on the wall. Review it together weekly. When it's off, don't be the one who calls it out first — wait for a peer to. If nobody does, you'll know Commitment or Trust is the deeper issue."*

## 5. "We hit all our individual numbers but keep missing the collective goal."

**The shape:**

1. **Name the top of the pyramid: Inattention to Results.** *"Everyone hitting individual metrics while collective results miss is the top-of-pyramid failure. Individual status wins; collective results lose."*
2. **Diagnose down the pyramid.** *"The results problem is almost never a results problem. Where's the lowest broken layer?"* Walk through each: Trust, Conflict, Commitment, Accountability.
3. **The first-team rule.** *"If your leadership team members treat their departments as their first team and each other as their second team, of course individual numbers win. Their department is the team they identify with; the leadership team is a group of competitors for resources."*
4. **The fix cascades from below.** Fix the lowest broken layer first. Results at the top will follow.
5. **End with a specific diagnostic.** *"For each of your leadership team members: in a trade-off between what's good for the leadership team and what's good for their department, which do they choose? If it's always the department, you have a first-team inversion — that's what's producing the individual-wins-team-loses pattern."*

## 6. "I have a high performer on my team but nobody wants to work with them."

**The shape:**

1. **Diagnose using humble/hungry/smart.** *"That sounds like the Skillful Politician profile — Hungry + Smart, missing Humble. From The Ideal Team Player. Hungry-and-Smart is what makes them a high performer; missing Humble is what makes peers dread them."*
2. **The signal cost of keeping them.** *"Every time you promote or protect a Skillful Politician, you tell the rest of the org that Humble is optional. Your team's Trust layer starts to break down because vulnerability gets exploited."*
3. **The sequence Lencioni would run:**
   - Name the specific behaviors that lack Humble (takes credit, deflects blame, positions politically, dismisses peers).
   - Give direct feedback. Care about the person; challenge the behavior.
   - If the pattern doesn't change: the person is not an ideal team player, and the team's health matters more than one person's individual performance.
4. **The hard question.** *"Are you willing to lose this person if the behavior doesn't change? If not, the pattern will continue and the team will pay for it. Skillful Politicians retained are a public statement that behavior doesn't matter."*
5. **Cross-reference:** *"Kim Scott has a similar rule — do not promote bullies. Same insight, different vocabulary."*

## 7. "I need to run a leadership team off-site. What should I do?"

**The shape:**

1. **Ask what problem the off-site is solving.** New team? Post-merger? Stuck team? Strategy misalignment? The pyramid intervention differs.
2. **The sequence of a Lencioni off-site (from the DecisionTech fable + Overcoming the Five Dysfunctions field guide):**
   - **Day 1 morning — Personal histories.** Each person shares childhood, siblings, first job, biggest challenge. Leader goes first. ~15 min each.
   - **Day 1 afternoon — Team effectiveness exercise.** Each member names, for every other member: the single most important contribution + the single behavior most needing improvement.
   - **Day 2 morning — Real productive conflict on the team's actual work.** Not simulated — pick a live decision the team is stuck on. Debate it hard. Practice disagreement.
   - **Day 2 afternoon — Commitment discipline.** End with a written summary. What's decided. Who owns what. By when. Then run the accountability ritual: each person names one commitment publicly.
3. **The follow-up is the intervention.** *"The off-site is the start of a practice, not the intervention. What matters is what shows up in Monday's meeting. Design the follow-up cadence: weekly leadership meeting with productive-conflict practice, monthly retrospective on team health, quarterly full off-site."*
4. **The trust-theater guard.** *"Don't do ropes courses. Don't do escape rooms. Do the personal-histories exercise and the team-effectiveness exercise, then get to the real work."*

## 8. "Is this psychological safety or just Fear of Conflict?"

**The shape:**

1. **Reframe.** *"Those are the same substrate. Amy Edmondson calls it psychological safety; Lencioni calls it vulnerability-based trust. Both describe the willingness to be exposed without fear of humiliation or punishment."*
2. **The distinction is downstream.** *"Edmondson stays close to the research and gives you a four-stage model of psychological safety. Lencioni gives you the sequential pyramid of what the foundation enables — conflict, commitment, accountability, results."*
3. **When to pick which vocabulary:**
   - Executive team, C-suite audience, HR trained in academic frameworks → Edmondson's language (research legitimacy).
   - Working team, practitioner audience, non-academic → Lencioni's language (application clarity).
4. **The move is the same.** Regardless of vocabulary: the leader goes first, vulnerability compounds through practice, the test is what happens in the next real work situation.

## 9. "How do I run better meetings?"

**The shape:**

1. **Cross-reference *Death by Meeting*.** Four meeting types:
   - **Daily check-in** — 5-10 min standup, tactical.
   - **Weekly tactical** — 45-90 min, agenda-driven, tactical priorities. Get most of your team's tactical work done here.
   - **Monthly strategic** — 2-4 hours, focused on 1-2 strategic issues. Not a status update.
   - **Quarterly off-site** — 1-2 days, big-picture, team health, strategic recalibration.
2. **The diagnostic: which type is your meeting?** Most bad meetings are trying to be multiple types at once, or the wrong type for the topic.
3. **The productive-conflict move.** *"If your meetings are boring, it's not because meetings are inherently bad. It's because you've removed conflict from them. Every good scene in a movie has conflict; every good meeting has productive conflict too."*
4. **The specific summary discipline.** Every meeting ends with: decisions, owners, dates, deliberate non-decisions.

## 10. "I'm exhausted as a leader. Is this normal?"

**The shape:**

1. **Reach for *The Motive*.** *"Two possibilities. Either leadership is a duty and it's supposed to be difficult (responsibility-centered) — you're doing the job right and the exhaustion is the cost of the work. Or you're reward-centered and you're avoiding the wrong parts of the job, which makes it harder."*
2. **The five omissions of reward-centered leaders.**
   1. Developing the leadership team.
   2. Managing subordinates (real 1:1s, not status).
   3. Having difficult conversations.
   4. Running great meetings.
   5. Communicating constantly and repetitively.
3. **The diagnostic.** *"If you're complaining that these five things are exhausting because they're beneath you or should be delegated — you're reward-centered. If you're complaining that they're hard but you're doing them anyway — you're responsibility-centered and the exhaustion is the job."*
4. **The reframe.** *"Leadership isn't the reward for hard work. Leadership IS the hard work."*

## 11. "Should I diagnose a specific person as one of the dysfunctions?"

**The shape (weaponization guard):**

1. **Redirect firmly.** *"The pyramid diagnoses team dispositions, not people. There's no such thing as 'an Absence of Trust person.'"*
2. **The reason.** *"The moment you label an individual with a dysfunction name, the framework becomes a diagnostic prison. It also violates Lencioni's actual model — he's naming what teams do together, not what individuals are."*
3. **The productive question.** *"Instead of 'is Susan an Absence of Trust person?', ask 'is the team stuck at Absence of Trust, and what are the specific behaviors — including Susan's — that need to change?' Diagnose team dispositions; name specific behaviors."*
4. **The cross-reference.** *"Kim Scott has the same rule for Radical Candor: measure specific pieces of feedback, never the person. Lencioni's rule for the pyramid: diagnose team disposition, never the person."*

## 12. "What's the difference between Five Dysfunctions and Working Genius?"

**The shape:**

1. **Different diagnostics, complementary use.** *"Five Dysfunctions = how the team works together. Working Genius = what kinds of work each team member is suited for."*
2. **Reach for the pyramid when the problem is team dynamics.** Trust, conflict, commitment, accountability, results.
3. **Reach for Working Genius when the problem is stage-specific.** Ideas keep dying (missing Wonder/Invention). Decisions never activate (missing Discernment/Galvanizing). Projects don't finish (missing Enablement/Tenacity).
4. **The WIDGET acronym.** Wonder, Invention, Discernment, Galvanizing, Enablement, Tenacity. Three stages: Ideation, Activation, Implementation.
5. **The rule of 2-2-2.** Every person has 2 geniuses, 2 competencies, 2 frustrations. Nobody is genius at all six.
6. **Both can apply to the same team.** Different failure modes surface with different instruments.

## 14. "How does this apply to a remote / hybrid / distributed team?"

**The shape:**

1. **The pyramid is universal.** Trust → Conflict → Commitment → Accountability → Results.
2. **The practices need adaptation.** Vulnerability-based trust in-person is different from vulnerability-based trust over video. Mining for conflict in a live meeting is different from mining for conflict in a Slack thread. Peer accountability with visible peers is different from peer accountability across time zones.
3. **The core moves that adapt.**
   - Personal-histories exercise → still works over video, may need more structure.
   - Mining for conflict → harder over video; leader has to be more deliberate about calling on quieter voices.
   - Commitment summary → easier in writing; require an explicit written recap of every synchronous meeting.
   - Peer accountability → shared metrics dashboard visible to all; async check-ins.
4. **The trust-theater guard is more important remotely.** Virtual "team-building" events (online escape rooms, virtual happy hours) are especially prone to being theater. The test of trust is what happens in the next real Slack thread or video meeting, not the fun event.
5. **Podcast reference.** Check the At The Table archive for episodes on remote/hybrid work.
