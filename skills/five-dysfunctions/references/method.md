# Five Dysfunctions — Method

> The canonical description of Patrick Lencioni's method in his own terms. Fidelity is the point. The framework's power comes from **the sequential structure of the pyramid** — each dysfunction enables the next — and from the load-bearing role of **vulnerability-based trust** as the foundation. Flatten either and the framework collapses into generic team-building advice.

## Definition — a team, in Lencioni's terms

Before the pyramid, the definition. Lencioni is strict:

> "A team is a relatively small number of people (anywhere from three to twelve) that shares common goals as well as the rewards and responsibilities for achieving them."
> — Lencioni, paraphrased across *The Five Dysfunctions* and *The Advantage*

**Two implications the skill enforces:**

1. **Interdependence is required.** A "team" whose members don't need each other to succeed is a *group*. The pyramid presumes interdependence; force-fitting it to a group creates artificial dysfunction diagnoses.
2. **The senior leadership team is where the pyramid is most consequential.** From *The Advantage*: the discipline of building a cohesive leadership team is the first of the Four Disciplines because everything else rests on it.

## The pyramid — bottom to top, sequential

The five dysfunctions stack. Each one, when unresolved, causes the next. Each one, when overcome, enables the next.

```
                       ▲
                    /     \
                  /  RESULTS \             5. Inattention to Results
                /             \               ↑ needs #4
              /  ACCOUNTABILITY \
            /                    \         4. Avoidance of Accountability
          /                        \          ↑ needs #3
        /       COMMITMENT           \
      /                                \    3. Lack of Commitment
    /                                    \     ↑ needs #2
  /            CONFLICT                    \
/                                            \  2. Fear of Conflict
─────────────────────────────────────────────      ↑ needs #1
                 TRUST                         1. Absence of Trust
```

**The Table Group's current-language definitions** (verbatim from [tablegroup.com](https://www.tablegroup.com/topics-and-resources/teamwork-5-dysfunctions/)):

### 1. Absence of Trust *(foundation)*

> "The fear of being vulnerable prevents team members from building trust with each other."

**The load-bearing distinction: vulnerability-based trust vs. predictive trust.**

- **Predictive trust** — confidence that a colleague will behave as expected. They'll meet deadlines. They won't leak the strategy. They'll show up. This is the baseline of any functional workplace and gets confused with trust in the sense Lencioni means.
- **Vulnerability-based trust** — willingness to be completely exposed with one another. To admit *"I don't know."* *"I need help."* *"I was wrong."* *"I'm bad at this."* Without fear of retaliation, humiliation, shame, or later use against you.

**Only vulnerability-based trust unlocks the next layer.** Teams can look civil (predictive trust) and be stuck at Absence of Trust because vulnerability is missing. The tell: nobody ever says *"I don't know"* or *"help me."*

**Lencioni's own words:**
> "Members of great teams trust one another on a fundamental, emotional level, and they are comfortable being vulnerable with each other about their weaknesses, mistakes, fears, and behaviors."
> — *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*

### 2. Fear of Conflict

> "The desire to preserve artificial harmony stifles productive ideological conflict."

**The load-bearing distinction: productive ideological conflict vs. destructive interpersonal conflict.**

- **Productive ideological conflict** — disagreement, debate, challenge around *ideas and decisions*. Can be emotional, sometimes heated. About the work, not the person.
- **Destructive interpersonal conflict** — attacks on character, factions, grudges, personal shots.

**Artificial harmony is the tell.** A team whose meetings are pleasant, drama-free, and consistently agreed-on is almost certainly stuck at Fear of Conflict. This looks like health and is the opposite. From Lencioni: *"If you don't have healthy conflict, you won't have real commitment."*

**Why the trust layer matters here:** without vulnerability-based trust, disagreement feels dangerous — someone might use it against you, remember it in the review, hold it as a grudge. Vulnerable teams can fight about ideas because they trust the fight won't become personal.

**Lencioni's own words:**
> "When there is trust, conflict becomes nothing but the pursuit of truth, an attempt to find the best possible answer."
> — *The Advantage*

### 3. Lack of Commitment

> "The lack of clarity and/or buy-in prevents team members from making decisions they stick to."

**The load-bearing distinction: buy-in vs. consensus.**

- **Consensus** — everyone agrees. Not required. Waiting for consensus is one of the ways teams stall.
- **Buy-in** — everyone gets heard, a decision is made, everyone leaves committed to executing it — even the people who lost the argument. The posture is **"disagree and commit."**

**Why the conflict layer matters here:** commitment requires that people were heard. If they never got to voice disagreement (Fear of Conflict), they can't commit — they leave the room half-checked-out, re-litigate in the hallway, and quietly execute their own preference.

**Ambiguity is the other killer.** Decisions that leave the room unclear ("did we decide? what did we decide?") produce fake commitment. The fix is a specific summary at the end of every meeting: what was decided, who owns what, by when.

**Lencioni's own words:**
> "Consensus is horrible. Waiting until everyone agrees is a formula for mediocrity."
> — recurring on *At The Table* podcast

### 4. Avoidance of Accountability

> "The need to avoid interpersonal discomfort prevents team members from holding each other accountable."

**The load-bearing distinction: peer-to-peer accountability vs. top-down enforcement.**

- **Top-down enforcement** — the manager is the only person who holds people accountable. This is the default, and it's a tell that the team is stuck at Avoidance of Accountability.
- **Peer-to-peer accountability** — teammates hold each other accountable to team commitments. The peer whose deliverable depends on yours can name your slip in a way the manager can't.

**Why the commitment layer matters here:** you can't hold a peer accountable to a plan they never committed to. Real Commitment (the layer below) is prerequisite. When people say "I never agreed to that" you're either at Lack of Commitment or the accountability move you're attempting is unearned.

**Why this is uncomfortable:** peer accountability requires the willingness to make a peer uncomfortable, which requires trust that the peer won't take it personally, which requires the trust layer to be built. Every layer below matters.

### 5. Inattention to Results *(top)*

> "The pursuit of individual goals and personal status erodes the team's focus on collective success."

**The load-bearing distinction: collective results vs. individual status.**

- **Individual status** — the enemy. Career advancement, ego, personal recognition, department metrics. Not bad in themselves; corrosive when they take priority over the team's collective outcome.
- **Collective results** — the team wins together, loses together. Individual heroics that make the team look good don't count if the team's shared commitments miss.

**Why every layer below matters here:** a team stuck at Absence of Trust cannot hold this. A team at Fear of Conflict can't have real Commitment, so can't be held accountable, so can't focus on collective results. The pyramid stacks *up* — and it also stacks *down*: fix the top by fixing the bottom.

**Lencioni's own words:**
> "Not finance. Not strategy. Not technology. It is teamwork that remains the ultimate competitive advantage, both because it is so powerful and so rare."
> — *The Five Dysfunctions of a Team*, opening

## CRITICAL: The pyramid is sequential, not parallel

**The single most important rule of the framework**, and the one violated most often by base-model summaries:

- The five dysfunctions are **not** five parallel problems a team might have.
- They are **one problem in five stacked layers**, where each layer *causes* the next.
- Fix the lowest broken layer first. Higher layers cannot be fixed while lower layers are broken.

**Why this matters:** teams that jump to accountability techniques (peer-review software, scorecards, PIPs) while sitting at Absence of Trust are doing performative accountability. Teams that write OKRs while stuck at Lack of Commitment write documents that get filed and forgotten. **Diagnose the lowest broken layer. Fix that first. Then the next.**

## CRITICAL: The pyramid diagnoses team dispositions, not personalities

- The pyramid measures **team behaviors** and **team dispositions**. "Our team is stuck at Fear of Conflict."
- The pyramid does NOT measure people. There is no such thing as "an Absence of Trust person."
- Same person, different team, can behave differently. The team's disposition is what the framework is diagnosing.

**Why this matters:** the moment someone labels a colleague ("she's just a Ruinous Empathy manager", "he's an Absence of Trust type"), the framework stops being a lever for change and becomes a diagnostic prison. It also violates Lencioni's actual model — he's naming what teams do together, not what individuals are.

## First team — Lencioni's counterintuitive rule

Most leaders default to treating their **direct reports** as their primary team. Lencioni insists on the reversal:

> Your **first team** is your peers — the other leaders you report alongside.

For a VP of Sales, the first team is the executive team (peers), not the sales organization (reports). Prioritizing sales-team wins over executive-team cohesion breaks the executive team's ability to hold the pyramid.

**Why:** collective results at the executive level are what set the strategy the whole company executes. If the executive team is stuck at any layer, the company is bounded by that dysfunction — no amount of downstream excellence compensates.

**Test:** in a genuine trade-off between what's good for your report team and what's good for your peer team, which do you choose? If the answer is "always my reports," you have the first-team relationship inverted.

## Vulnerability-based trust — how to build it (and how to fake it)

**How trust actually gets built** (from *Overcoming the Five Dysfunctions* field guide):

1. **The leader goes first.** Vulnerability cascades downward. If the CEO won't admit a mistake, nobody else will. Every trust-building intervention starts with the leader modeling.
2. **Personal histories exercise.** Each team member shares their childhood, family background, first job, and biggest challenge. Not a therapy session — a shared context that humanizes.
3. **Team effectiveness exercise.** Each member names the single most important contribution of every other team member, and the single behavior they most need to improve or eliminate. Direct, in-room, unfiltered.
4. **Ongoing practice** — the trust that gets built in a workshop must compound in the next real work situation. If it doesn't show up in Monday's meeting, it didn't stick.

**How trust gets faked (the trust-theater failure mode):**

- Ropes-course off-site + emotional bonding + tears + "we're a family now" + zero behavior change on Monday.
- Personal histories exercise treated as a one-time content deliverable rather than the start of a practice.
- Leader who runs the exercises but stays personally invulnerable in day-to-day work ("do as I say").
- Any trust intervention where the next meeting looks exactly like the meeting before it.

**The test of vulnerability-based trust is what happens in the next real work situation, not the off-site.** If the next disagreement gets suppressed, the trust wasn't built.

## Mining for conflict — the leader's active move

If your team never disagrees, don't celebrate — dig. The leader's job at Fear of Conflict is to **actively surface disagreement that people won't voluntarily bring up.**

- Direct question in the meeting: *"Sarah, you don't look convinced. What's the objection?"*
- Assign a devil's advocate role rotating through the team.
- Explicitly permission-grant: *"I want us to disagree here. If we all agree, we're not thinking hard enough."*
- After a decision, name the dissenters: *"Alex disagreed. Alex, tell us again what you think we're missing so we go in with our eyes open."*

**Why:** the natural pull of every team is toward artificial harmony. Without the leader actively mining for conflict, disagreement stays underground and shows up as passive resistance, hallway re-litigation, or quiet non-execution.

## End every meeting with a specific summary

The commitment layer's most operational move:

- **What was decided** — explicit list.
- **Who owns what** — one name per action.
- **By when** — a specific date, not "soon" or "this quarter."
- **Any deliberate non-decisions** — what we chose not to decide today, and when we'll come back to it.

**Why:** ambiguity kills commitment. If people leave the room unclear, commitment is gone regardless of how good the discussion was. The summary is the artifact that makes the commitment stick.

## Peer-to-peer accountability rituals

**How to install accountability without becoming the manager-enforcer:**

- **Team scorecard** — a small set of shared metrics the whole team is jointly responsible for. Reviewed together at the standing meeting. Peer commentary expected on any slippage.
- **Team commitments cascade** — each meeting ends with commitments (see above); the next meeting starts by naming what shipped and what didn't. Peers speak first.
- **Public retrospectives** — after each project, the team debriefs together, in-room, on what worked and what didn't. Attributed, not anonymous.
- **The leader's role** — sets the norm that peer accountability is expected, models it, doesn't rescue peers from uncomfortable conversations with each other.

**Warning:** peer-to-peer accountability without vulnerability-based trust and real commitment turns into public shaming or team backstabbing. The layers below have to be solid first.

## Integration — the pyramid and the rest of Lencioni's system

Lencioni's method isn't the pyramid alone. It's the pyramid inside a larger operating system he's built out over 24 years:

- **The pyramid** tells you what a functional *team* does.
- **The Advantage's Four Disciplines** (2012) tell you what a functional *organization* does — with the pyramid as the first discipline (build a cohesive leadership team). See `references/post-book.md`.
- **The Ideal Team Player** (2016) tells you *who to put on the team* — humble + hungry + smart.
- **The Motive** (2020) tells you what the *leader* has to be for any of this to work — responsibility-centered, not reward-centered.
- **The 6 Types of Working Genius / WIDGET** (2022) tells you *what kind of work each team member is suited for* and where the team has genius gaps.

These are not five separate frameworks. They are one operating system. When the pyramid isn't enough, reach for the adjacent instrument — see `references/post-book.md` for how each extends the system.

## What this method is NOT

- **NOT a personality test.** The pyramid measures team dispositions, not people.
- **NOT a set of parallel problems.** Sequential. Fix the lowest broken layer first.
- **NOT trust theater.** Off-site exercises alone don't build trust; ongoing behavior does.
- **NOT the same as psychological safety** (Edmondson) — near-adjacent, and complementary. Vulnerability-based trust and psychological safety describe the same substrate; Lencioni names the downstream behaviors the substrate enables, Edmondson stays closer to the research. See `references/applications.md`.
- **NOT the same as harmony.** Health looks like productive disagreement, not agreement. The team that "never disagrees" is a red flag, not a success.
- **NOT applicable to any group of people who report to the same manager.** Lencioni's definition of team requires shared collective responsibility. A group is not a team; the pyramid doesn't diagnose the same way.
- **NOT a diagnostic to apply to a single person.** Never label someone as "an Absence of Trust person." Diagnose the team's disposition and the specific behaviors, always.
