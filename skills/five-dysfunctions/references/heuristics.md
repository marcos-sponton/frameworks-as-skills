# Five Dysfunctions — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Lencioni's operational rules — that separate applying Five Dysfunctions well from the caricatured version (five items on a slide, no sequential logic, an off-site as the fix). The pyramid is sequential; vulnerability-based trust is load-bearing; the trust-theater guard runs through this whole file.

## Do's

### Diagnose the lowest broken layer of the pyramid first

The pyramid is sequential. Fixing higher layers when lower ones are broken produces performative outcomes:

- Peer-review software on a team stuck at Absence of Trust = surveillance, not accountability.
- OKRs on a team stuck at Lack of Commitment = documents that get filed and forgotten.
- "Focus on results" pep talk on a team stuck at Inattention to Results = the wrong intervention entirely — the fix is downstairs.

**How to apply:** before prescribing any team intervention, ask what happens in the team's meetings. What does disagreement look like? Do decisions stick? Does anyone hold anyone else accountable other than the manager? The answers locate the broken layer.

### Test for vulnerability-based trust, not predictive trust

Two questions to distinguish them:

- *"When was the last time someone on your team said 'I don't know' or 'I need help' out loud, in a meeting?"* — if the answer is *"I can't remember,"* the team has predictive trust at best.
- *"When was the last time someone (including the leader) admitted a mistake publicly?"* — same test.

**Predictive trust looks like a well-behaved, on-time team. Vulnerability-based trust looks like a team where people say hard things about themselves.** Only the second unlocks the pyramid.

### Mine for conflict — actively

If your team never disagrees, don't celebrate. Dig. The leader's job at Fear of Conflict is to surface disagreement that people won't voluntarily bring up:

- Direct question in the meeting: *"Alex, you don't look convinced. What's the objection?"*
- Assign devil's advocate — rotating, named, expected to argue against the emerging consensus.
- Explicitly permission-grant: *"I want us to disagree here. If we all agree, we're not thinking hard enough."*
- After a decision, name the dissenters: *"Sarah disagreed. Sarah, tell us again what you think we're missing so we go in with our eyes open."*

**Why:** the natural pull of every team is toward artificial harmony. Absent an active leader move, disagreement stays underground and shows up as hallway re-litigation.

### End every meeting with a specific summary

The commitment layer's most operational move. Every meeting closes with:

- **What was decided** — explicit list.
- **Who owns what** — one name per action.
- **By when** — a specific date, not "soon" or "this quarter."
- **Any deliberate non-decisions** — what we chose not to decide today, when we'll come back to it.

Not "great meeting everyone." A written or verbal summary that leaves the room with no ambiguity. Ambiguity kills commitment.

### Use the team assessment — and the discussion is the work

The Table Group's team assessment (in the book's appendix and at [tablegroup.com](https://www.tablegroup.com/product/online-team-assessment/)) is a Likert-scale instrument. Each team member scores the team on statements mapped to each dysfunction. The output plots the team on the pyramid.

**Rule:** the assessment is diagnostic, not prescriptive. The discussion of the results — as a team, in a room, with the leader modeling vulnerability first — is where the work starts. Distributing the assessment and reading the report doesn't build anything.

### Hire for humble + hungry + smart

From *The Ideal Team Player* (2016). All three virtues, at meaningful level:

- **Humble** — quick to point out others' contributions, slow to seek attention.
- **Hungry** — self-motivated, rarely has to be pushed.
- **Smart** — interpersonally smart. Common sense about people. Not IQ.

**Missing Humble is disqualifying.** The Hungry + Smart + not Humble profile is the Skillful Politician — the most dangerous type on a team, hardest to spot at hiring, hardest to remove later.

### Treat your peers as your first team

For any leader with a peer team above their report team:

- **First team = peers** — the people you report alongside. Executive team, if you're a VP.
- **Second team = reports** — the people you manage.

Trade-offs between the two default in most leaders' minds toward the reports. Lencioni: reverse it. The first team is where collective results are set; the second team executes them.

**Test:** in a genuine trade-off between what's good for your report team and what's good for your peer team, which do you choose? If the answer is always your reports, the relationship is inverted.

### Diagnose the leader before the team

From *The Motive* (2020). A **reward-centered** leader (leadership as the payoff) systematically avoids the five duties that fix the pyramid:
1. Developing the leadership team.
2. Managing subordinates (real 1:1s, not status updates).
3. Having difficult conversations.
4. Running great meetings.
5. Communicating constantly and repetitively.

If the leader is reward-centered, the team's pyramid can't be fixed without fixing that first. **Check the leader's motive before prescribing team interventions.**

### Answer the Six Critical Questions as a leadership team

From *The Advantage* (2012). Before overcommunicating clarity, the leadership team has to have clarity. Six questions, answered together, out of shared discussion, not delegated:

1. Why do we exist?
2. How do we behave?
3. What do we do?
4. How will we succeed?
5. What is most important, right now?
6. Who must do what?

**Rule:** if the executive team can't answer these in one voice, the organization can't be aligned around them. This is where the Four Disciplines start.

### Overcommunicate clarity — assume you're just starting when you think you're done

Also from *The Advantage*. Leaders systematically underestimate how much repetition is needed for a message to land across an organization. Lencioni's rule: any leader who thinks they're overcommunicating is probably just starting.

**How to apply:** repeat the answers to the Six Critical Questions relentlessly — in every all-hands, every leadership meeting, every 1:1, every hiring pitch. When you're bored of saying it, the org is starting to hear it.

### Reach for Working Genius when the team fails at specific *kinds* of work

From *The 6 Types of Working Genius* (2022). If a team keeps failing at a specific stage of work (ideas die, decisions never activate, projects don't finish), it may not be a Five Dysfunctions problem — it may be a genius gap.

- **Ideation stage** (Wonder + Invention) — do you have anyone who asks "what if?" or generates novel ideas?
- **Activation stage** (Discernment + Galvanizing) — do you have anyone who assesses merit and mobilizes people?
- **Implementation stage** (Enablement + Tenacity) — do you have anyone who supports and pushes through obstacles?

**The two frameworks are complementary, not competing.** Five Dysfunctions diagnoses how the team works together; Working Genius diagnoses what kinds of work the team can execute.

## Don'ts

### Don't present the five dysfunctions as a parallel list

The single most common misapplication. A slide with "5 problems your team might have" flattens the framework and destroys its power. Always present as a stacked pyramid with each layer enabling the next.

### Don't celebrate a team where nobody disagrees

**Artificial harmony is a tell, not a marker.** A leadership team whose meetings are pleasant and drama-free is almost certainly stuck at Fear of Conflict. Name it. Do not congratulate the team on how "aligned" they are.

**Red flag phrases to catch:**
- *"We're really tight, no drama."*
- *"We hardly ever disagree."*
- *"Everybody gets along."*
- *"Our meetings are efficient — no long debates."*

Each of these describes a team stuck at Fear of Conflict. Diagnose accordingly.

### Don't wait for consensus

Consensus-seeking is one of the most common ways teams stall at Lack of Commitment. The bar is **buy-in, not consensus.** Everyone gets heard. A decision gets made. Everyone commits — including the people who lost the argument. **"Disagree and commit"** is the posture.

**How to apply:** when the team is at 80% agreement, decide. Waiting for 100% is a failure mode.

### Don't be the only one holding people accountable

Manager-only accountability is a tell that the team is stuck at Avoidance of Accountability. When the manager is the only enforcer:

- The manager becomes the bottleneck for every issue.
- Peers learn accountability is not their job.
- Underperformance gets addressed slowly (only when it reaches the manager) instead of fast (when the peer whose deliverable depends on it notices).

**The fix is peer-to-peer accountability** — and it can only exist when the Commitment layer is real. If commitment is fake, don't try to install accountability yet.

### Don't do trust theater

**Off-site vulnerability exercise + no ongoing behavior change on Monday = performative.** The ropes course, the crying together, the personal-histories exercise — all fine as *starts*, disastrous as *deliverables*.

**The test:** what happens in the next real work situation? If the next disagreement gets suppressed, the trust wasn't built.

**Signal to catch:** the user asks for another trust-building exercise without describing what changed after the last one. Do not prescribe more exercises without diagnosing why the last one didn't stick. The exercise is not the intervention. Ongoing practice is.

### Don't force the pyramid onto a group that isn't a team

Lencioni's strict definition: a team is a group that shares collective responsibility for outcomes. A set of independent contributors reporting to the same manager is a **group**, not a team. The dysfunctions don't apply the same way.

**Test:** do the members need each other to succeed? If not, it's a group. Don't diagnose Absence of Trust on a group — the diagnosis is meaningless.

### Don't label individuals with dysfunction names

Never: *"She's an Absence of Trust person."* Always: *"The team's disposition is stuck at Absence of Trust, and here are the specific behaviors we can name and change."*

Personalizing the framework turns it into a diagnostic prison and undermines the actual method, which is about team dispositions and collective behaviors.

### Don't skip the field guide

*Overcoming the Five Dysfunctions of a Team* (2005) exists because too many people read the fable and asked "now what?" The field guide has the exercises, scripts, and worked examples that the fable alone doesn't provide. Use it.

### Don't confuse The Ideal Team Player with "team player" as a HR platitude

*"Team player"* as a job description bullet is meaningless. Lencioni's Ideal Team Player is a specific diagnostic: humble + hungry + smart, all three at meaningful level. Missing one — especially Humble — is disqualifying. Use the framework as a hiring rubric, not as a slogan.

### Don't promote the Skillful Politician

Hungry + Smart + not Humble = the Skillful Politician. High individual performance masks toxicity to peers. Every promotion of a Skillful Politician tells the org that Humble is optional and blows up the Trust layer for everyone else on the team.

**Same category as Kim Scott's "don't promote bullies" rule** — different vocabulary, same insistence.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### Treating the pyramid as a checklist

Managers read the book, run a workshop on each layer, tick each one off, declare victory. The pyramid is not a checklist. It's a set of team dispositions that require ongoing practice. A team that's "done" with the pyramid is a team that will slip back to its previous dysfunction within months if the practices aren't sustained.

### Predictive trust mistaken for vulnerability-based trust

A team where everyone hits deadlines, doesn't leak secrets, and shows up on time can still be stuck at Absence of Trust. The tell is that nobody says *"I don't know"* or *"I need help"* out loud. Civility is not vulnerability.

### Debate mistaken for productive conflict

Some teams have loud arguments and still don't have productive ideological conflict — because the arguments are personal (factions, egos, grudges) rather than about ideas. The tell: after the argument, do people go execute together, or do they retreat to their corners and re-litigate later? Productive conflict resolves in shared commitment; unproductive conflict resolves in factions.

### Commitment mistaken for compliance

The team leaves the room saying "yes, we're aligned" and then executes their own preferences quietly. Compliance without commitment. The tell: hallway re-litigation, slow execution, back-channel objections after the meeting. Fix: make sure the Conflict layer was real (people got heard) before declaring commitment.

### Team pride mistaken for team results

*"We're a great team, everyone likes each other, we ship on time."* Not the same as *"the team achieves the collective results it committed to."* Pride without results is the top-of-pyramid failure — Inattention to Results dressed up as team health. Ask: what were the collective commitments, and did the team hit them?

### The off-site as a one-time fix

Every trust-building intervention is the *start* of a practice, not the delivery of a deliverable. The off-site produces a shared context. The ongoing work is what compounds. Teams that treat the off-site as the whole intervention regress within weeks.

### Working Genius applied as a personality test

The 6 Types of Working Genius (WIDGET) is about *what work energizes each person* and *what genius the team is missing at a stage*. Applied as "I'm a Wonder, you're a Tenacity, we can't work together" it becomes another useless personality label. Same rule as the pyramid: diagnose the team's genius gaps and the person's energy patterns, not their identity.

### Reward-centered leader running the pyramid interventions

A leader who avoids the five duties (from *The Motive*) but runs the personal-histories exercise is doing trust theater from a place of personal invulnerability. The team notices. The trust doesn't compound. Fix the leader's motive first.

### Cross-functional or matrix "teams" that aren't teams

Matrixed groups, cross-functional working groups, project cohorts — often lack the shared collective responsibility that makes them a Lencioni-team. The pyramid can inform how they work, but they're groups by Lencioni's definition. Don't force the diagnosis.

## Pro tips

### The "personal histories" opening for any new leadership team

Every member shares (in a room, together, ~15 minutes each):
- Where they grew up.
- Number and order of siblings.
- Most interesting or challenging childhood experience.
- First job.
- Most important first job lesson.

Not therapy. Shared context that humanizes. The leader goes first.

### The "team effectiveness" exercise for existing teams

Each member names, for every other team member:
- The single most important contribution they make to the team.
- The single behavior they most need to improve or eliminate for the good of the team.

Delivered in the room, unfiltered, direct. Extremely uncomfortable the first time. Compounds fast if the trust layer holds.

### The 15-minute "what did we decide" audit

After any leadership meeting, spend 15 minutes writing:
1. What we decided.
2. Who owns what.
3. By when.
4. What did I hear each dissenter say, and how did we address it?

If you can't answer #4, the Commitment layer is at risk — someone didn't get heard, and they'll re-litigate later.

### The "would I be uncomfortable if a peer held me to this?" test

For any commitment you make in a team meeting: would you be uncomfortable if a peer publicly held you to it in a follow-up meeting? If yes, that's the commitment worth surfacing now, before you leave the room. If no, it's probably not a real commitment.

### After a decision, ask "who lost this argument?"

The dissenters. Name them. Then ask each: *"What are we missing that you think we should know going in?"* Two functions: gives them one last chance to raise the objection, and makes their commitment explicit even though they lost.

### The "first team" audit

Ask any leader: *"When you have a trade-off between what's best for the leadership team and what's best for the team you manage, which do you pick?"* If the answer is always the report team, they're inverting the first-team rule. This is a Five Dysfunctions issue at the executive-team level — no matter how well their department runs.

## Anti-patterns (what "bad team-building" looks like in Lencioni's terms)

### The Absence-of-Trust team masquerading as civility

**What it looks like:** meetings are polite, deadlines are met, nobody visibly conflicts. Everyone is professional. Nothing important is ever said out loud.
**Why it fails:** predictive trust masks the missing vulnerability-based trust. Real disagreement stays underground; underperformance goes unaddressed; the team executes its individual jobs and never achieves anything collective.
**How to redirect:** name the pattern (*"You have predictive trust; you don't have vulnerability-based trust. When was the last time someone on your team said 'I don't know'?"*). Start the trust practice with the leader modeling.

### The Fear-of-Conflict team celebrated for "alignment"

**What it looks like:** everyone agrees on every decision. Meetings are fast and pleasant. Leadership describes the team as "aligned" and "collaborative."
**Why it fails:** artificial harmony. Decisions aren't stress-tested; dissent doesn't happen in the room, only in the hallway; commitment is compliance, not buy-in.
**How to redirect:** mine for conflict actively. Assign a devil's advocate. Name the pattern: *"If we agree this fast, we're not thinking hard enough."*

### The Lack-of-Commitment team with beautiful documents

**What it looks like:** the team writes OKRs, roadmaps, strategy docs. Everyone signs off. Six months later, the documents describe a different company than the one that got built.
**Why it fails:** compliance without commitment. People agreed to make the meeting end; they never committed to executing. Often driven by Fear of Conflict below.
**How to redirect:** fix the layer below first. Then run the specific-summary discipline at the end of every meeting.

### The Avoidance-of-Accountability team where the manager is the enforcer

**What it looks like:** the manager holds everyone accountable, one at a time, in 1:1s. Peers don't hold each other accountable. Underperformance gets addressed slowly.
**Why it fails:** manager becomes the bottleneck. Peers learn accountability isn't their job. The team can only scale to what the manager can personally enforce.
**How to redirect:** fix Commitment first (real, not compliance). Then install peer accountability rituals — team scorecard, public retros, structured commitments cascade.

### The Inattention-to-Results team where everyone hits their individual numbers

**What it looks like:** each department shows green metrics. Individual bonuses paid. Collective outcome (revenue, product, mission) misses. The team is "high performing" on paper.
**Why it fails:** individual status prioritized over collective results. Each layer below is broken and the top of the pyramid is where it shows.
**How to redirect:** diagnose down the pyramid. The results problem is almost never a results problem — it's a symptom of what's broken below. Fix the lowest broken layer.

### The Skillful Politician on the team

**What it looks like:** high performer, well-liked by leadership, resented by peers. Takes credit, deflects blame, always positioned. Individual metrics are top of team.
**Why it fails:** Hungry + Smart + not Humble. Poisons the Trust layer for everyone. Every promotion of a Skillful Politician confirms to the team that Humble is optional.
**How to redirect:** name the pattern with the leader. If the leader can't remove or reform the Skillful Politician, the team's Trust layer will not stabilize.

### The reward-centered leader running "team-building"

**What it looks like:** the CEO commissions a workshop, hires the consultant, delegates the trust-building — and stays personally invulnerable in day-to-day work.
**Why it fails:** vulnerability cascades downward from the leader. If the leader won't go first, the rest of the team can't. The workshop is theater.
**How to redirect:** the intervention is the leader's motive first (*The Motive*), then the team.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts Lencioni has made explicit across his work:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Five problems your team has | The pyramid: each dysfunction enables the next | The framework is sequential, not parallel |
| Trust | Vulnerability-based trust (vs. predictive trust) | Predictive trust is not what the framework means |
| Alignment | Buy-in | Alignment often masks compliance without commitment |
| Consensus | Disagree and commit | Consensus-seeking stalls; buy-in doesn't require agreement |
| Conflict | Productive ideological conflict | Distinguishes healthy debate from personal attack |
| Accountability (manager-driven) | Peer-to-peer accountability | Manager-only enforcement is a tell of dysfunction |
| Individual performance | Collective results | The framework's north star; individual heroics don't count |
| Team-building off-site | Trust practice | The off-site is a start; ongoing behavior is the work |
| "We're really aligned" | "How often do people disagree in our meetings?" | Alignment claims are frequently artificial harmony |
| "She's an Absence of Trust person" | "The team's disposition is stuck at Absence of Trust" | Framework diagnoses team behavior, not personalities |
| Team player (HR platitude) | Humble + hungry + smart (all three) | The Ideal Team Player is a specific diagnostic, not a slogan |
| High potential | Genius + competency + frustration (WIDGET) | Working Genius disaggregates energy from ability |
| First team = my reports | First team = my peers | The counterintuitive Lencioni rule; peer team is where collective results are set |
| Overcommunicating | Just starting to communicate | Leaders systematically underestimate the repetition needed |
| Being an inspiring leader | Being a responsibility-centered leader | *The Motive*'s reframe; leadership is duty, not reward |

## The trust-theater guard (this file's non-negotiable)

If a user comes to this skill asking for a trust-building exercise:

- Do NOT prescribe a new exercise before diagnosing what happened after the last one.
- Ask: *"What was the last trust-building activity you did? What changed in day-to-day team behavior afterward?"*
- If the answer is *"nothing changed"* — that's the diagnosis. The team's problem isn't lack of exercises; it's that trust practice isn't compounding day-to-day. Prescribing another exercise makes it worse (teaches the team that these events don't matter).
- If the leader is not personally modeling vulnerability day-to-day, name it. Vulnerability cascades downward. The leader goes first.
- The exercise is not the intervention. Ongoing practice — starting with the leader's day-to-day vulnerability — is the intervention.
- **Never let an off-site ropes course substitute for the team's next real hard conversation.** The test of trust is what happens in the next real work situation, not the off-site.
