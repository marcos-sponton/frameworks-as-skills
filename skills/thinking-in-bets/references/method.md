# Thinking in Bets — Method

> The three-book system as one integrated loop. Fidelity matters — Duke's method is opinionated, and softening any of it collapses into generic "make better decisions" advice.

## The one-line definition

Every decision is a **bet on an uncertain future**. Two forces determine outcomes: **decision quality (DQ) + luck**. The failure mode is **"resulting"** — collapsing DQ into OQ.

Duke's operating principle:

> "You can't know if you made a good decision until you know the things you couldn't know at the time you made it."
> — Duke, *How to Decide* (paraphrased via grahammann.net book notes; **quote confidence: moderate** — verify against print edition before citing verbatim)

## The three books as one system

Duke's three books are not a series of unrelated books — they cohere as one loop.

| Book | Year | Role in the system |
|---|---|---|
| **Thinking in Bets** | 2018 | **The frame.** Decisions are bets under uncertainty. Separate DQ from OQ. Vet your beliefs. Form a truthseeking pod. Do mental time travel. |
| **How to Decide** | 2020 | **The toolkit.** 6-step decision framework. Speed-triage tests. Decision journal. Structured process for teachable decision quality. |
| **Quit** | 2022 | **The counter-force.** Once you're committed, kill criteria + monkeys-first + quitting coach protect against sunk cost, endowment, identity, status quo. |

**One integrated loop:**

1. **Name what you're betting.** Reframe the choice as a bet on an uncertain future.
2. **Structure the decision.** Use the 6-step or the speed-triage tools.
3. **Write it down.** Decision journal — what you decided, why, alternatives considered, confidence, what would change your mind.
4. **Set kill criteria in advance.** State + date. Before you're emotionally locked in.
5. **Revisit dispassionately.** Fight hindsight bias with the journal. Update calibration.

## Book 1: Thinking in Bets (2018) — the frame

### Thesis

Decisions are bets on uncertain futures under incomplete information. Two forces determine outcomes: **decision quality + luck**. The failure mode is **"resulting"** — judging decision quality by outcome quality.

### Core moves

**Reframe every choice as a bet.** Duke's mental prompt: *"Wanna bet?"* When you say "I'm sure X will happen," ask yourself: at what odds would I bet on it? Forces calibration.

**Belief formation problem.** Humans hear → believe → rarely vet:

> "We form beliefs without vetting most of them, and maintain them even after receiving clear, corrective information."
> — Duke, *Thinking in Bets*, cited via calvinrosser.com notes (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**Motivated reasoning + numeracy inversion.** The better you are with numbers, the better you are at spinning numbers to conform to your priors. Numeracy alone does not save you.

**Long-game framing.**

> "Life, like poker, is one long game, and there are going to be a lot of losses, even after making the best possible bets."
> — Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**Truthseeking pod.** Form a small group ("buddies" in poker) that will punish sloppy *thinking*, not sloppy *outcomes*. Cultivate members who will say "I don't understand why you made that choice" — not "great call, you won."

**Mental time travel.** Duke introduces the 10-10-10 device (how will I feel in 10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years). Expanded in *How to Decide*.

## Book 2: How to Decide (2020) — the toolkit

### Thesis

Decision quality is teachable via explicit process.

### The 6-step framework

| Step | Action |
|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the reasonable set of possible outcomes. |
| 2 | Identify your preferences over outcomes (payoffs). |
| 3 | Estimate the likelihood of each outcome. |
| 4 | Assess the relative likelihood of liked vs. disliked outcomes for a given option. |
| 5 | Repeat steps 1–4 for other options. |
| 6 | Compare options. |

(Source: summarized via wisdomfromexperts.com; a decision tree with probabilities is the artifact.)

### Speed-triage tests (decision hygiene for everyday decisions)

Not every decision needs the full 6-step. Duke gives fast tests:

- **The happiness test.** *"Will my happiness, a week from now, depend on this decision?"* If no, decide fast; don't burn cycles. (Paraphrased from Duke, *How to Decide*.)
- **The 10-10-10 rule.** 10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years. How will you feel about it at each horizon? Reveals whether the emotional weight matches the actual weight.
- **The only-option test.** If this were the only option available, would you take it? If yes to multiple options → flip a coin, both are fine.
- **The two-way door test.** Bezos-style. Reversible? Decide fast, ship, learn. Irreversible? Slow down.
- **Coin-flip principle.** *"When a decision is really hard, it means it's easy… you should just be flipping a coin."* (Duke via Behavioral Scientist Q&A — she means: when options are close, the cost of getting it "wrong" is low; don't overspend deliberation.)

### Resulting — defined precisely

The book gives the clean definition:

> "Resulting is when we use the quality of an outcome to judge the quality of a decision."
> — Duke, *How to Decide* (via grahammann.net notes; **quote confidence: moderate**)

Corollary — the reason resulting is so hard to fight:

> "You can't know if you made a good decision until you know the things you couldn't know at the time you made it."

### Decision journal

Write down: what you decided, why, what alternatives you considered, your confidence (percentage!), what you'd need to see to change your mind. Purpose: fight hindsight bias, which Duke calls *"the great enemy of learning from experience."*

## Book 3: Quit (2022) — the counter-force

### Thesis

Grit is oversold. Quitting on time is a decision skill. And quitting on time almost always **feels** like quitting too early.

### Core moves

**Kill criteria = state + date.** The load-bearing tool:

> "If I'm not in [state] by [date], I quit."
> — Duke, *Quit* (via wisdomfromexperts.com; **quote confidence: moderate**)

The specific format matters. Not "we'll revisit next quarter." A state (something observable) and a date (something binding).

**Monkeys and pedestals** (borrowed from Astro Teller, X). Tackle the **monkey** (the hard, unknowable problem) first. Pedestal-building is fake progress.

> "There is no point building the pedestal if you can't train the monkey. In other words, you ought to tackle the hardest part of the problem first."
> — Duke, cited via behavioralscientist.org (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**Quitting on time feels like quitting too early.**

> "Quitting on time usually feels like quitting too early."
> — Duke, *Quit* (via grahammann.net; **quote confidence: moderate**)

This is the emotional signature of a well-timed quit — and the reason people don't quit until they're forced to.

**Sunk cost as accumulation.**

> "The sunk cost effect turns your life into a Katamari: the more you roll forward, the more debris you accumulate, and the harder it is to stop."
> — Duke, *Quit* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**Identity is the hardest quit.**

> "When it comes to quitting, the hardest thing to quit is who you are."
> — Duke, *Quit* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

Sasha Cohen kept skating post-Olympics because "skater" was her self-concept. Sears held onto retail while abandoning thriving financial services because Sears' *identity* was retail.

**Ownership goes mental.**

> "We own what we've bought and what we've thought."

The endowment effect applies to positions we've publicly staked out, not just objects we own.

**Asymmetric error.**

> "When we're in the gains, we tend to quit too early. When we're in the losses, we tend to stick too long."

**Grit reframe.**

> "Success does not lie in sticking to things; it lies in picking the right thing to stick to and quitting the rest."

Not anti-grit. Grit is good *conditional on the goal being worth it*. Unconditional grit produces Siobhan O'Keeffe finishing the London Marathon on a broken fibula.

**Waste is prospective, not retrospective.**

> "Waste is a forward-looking problem: the real waste is spending more time and money on something that's no longer worth it."

The money and time already spent are gone. The waste is what you'll spend *from here*.

## Sequence of application (the integrated loop)

Duke does not prescribe a strict order, but in practice a session using her toolkit goes:

1. **Frame it as a bet.** Name the uncertainty. What are you betting on? What are the possible outcomes?
2. **Structure the choice** with the 6-step OR the appropriate speed-triage test.
3. **Force calibration.** No 100%s. Give a percentage. Give upper/lower bounds. Most people are overconfident on the width of their confidence intervals.
4. **Run a premortem.** Imagine you failed. Why? (Klein's device, adopted by Duke.)
5. **Pair with precommitment.** For each failure signal, what would you do when you see it? Kill criteria go here.
6. **Write it in the decision journal.** Include confidence and the signals that would change your mind.
7. **After the outcome, evaluate DQ separately from OQ.** Was the process good regardless of the result?

## What this method is NOT

Duke is emphatic about the negative definition. This method is NOT:

- **A way to eliminate uncertainty.** *"Certainty is an illusion. You're always deciding under conditions of uncertainty."* The method structures uncertainty, doesn't dissolve it.
- **A rejection of intuition.** Duke defends disciplined intuition (Klein's recognition-primed decision school). System 1 is correct for Nadal's forehand and the deer-in-headlights driver. The trap is treating strategic business/life decisions like Nadal's forehand.
- **A guarantee of good outcomes.** DQ ≠ OQ. Good decisions lose sometimes. Bad decisions win sometimes. Play the long game.
- **A rejection of grit.** *"Quitting and grit are two sides of the same decision."* (Duke, *Quit*.)
- **Only for professional poker players or VCs.** Duke's most-viewed material is on personal decisions — career, relationships, whether to run a marathon.
