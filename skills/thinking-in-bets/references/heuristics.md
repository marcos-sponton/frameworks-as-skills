# Thinking in Bets — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices Duke reaches for constantly, with attribution. Concepts are high confidence; specific verbatim quotes are moderate confidence (dossier note — verify against print before quoting).

## Do's

### Separate decision quality from outcome quality — every single time

The load-bearing move. Duke's whole method sits on it.

**Canonical case: Pete Carroll, Super Bowl XLIX (2015).** Seahawks on the 1-yard line, 26 seconds left, down by 4. Pass call, intercepted by Butler. Media savaged Carroll: "worst play call in Super Bowl history." Duke defends the call. Interception rate in that spot is ~1–2%. The outcome was terrible; the decision was defensible.

> "Just because the result was a bad outcome did not make the decision itself bad."
> — Duke (paraphrased from *Thinking in Bets*, opening chapter; via thepastorsworkshop.com)

**How to apply:** when the user brings you a past outcome to evaluate, before you say anything else, un-collapse DQ from OQ. Ask: "given only what was knowable at the time, was the process good? Would we make the same choice again?"

### Force calibration — never let a belief sit at 100%

Duke's rule: whenever someone claims certainty, force a percentage.

> "Certainty is an illusion. You're always deciding under conditions of uncertainty."
> — Duke, *How to Decide* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**How to apply:**
1. When the user says "I'm sure X will happen," push back: "How sure? Give me a percentage."
2. Ask for upper and lower bounds, not point estimates. Most people are overconfident on width.
3. Track calibration over time. Are your 70% claims coming true ~70% of the time? If they're coming true 50% of the time, you're miscalibrated — recalibrate future estimates.

**Vocabulary:** she prefers *"how promising do you think it is?"* for lay users who freeze on point probabilities. Both are calibration; the register is different.

### Run a premortem — pair it with precommitment

Premortem is Klein's tool, adopted and popularized by Duke:

> "A pre-mortem is imagining that you've failed and asking why."
> — via grahammann.net notes on *How to Decide*

**Duke's twist:** pair the premortem with **precommitment**. For each failure mode you identify, write down the *signal* you'd see and the *action* you'd take. Without precommitment, the premortem is journaling. With precommitment, it's a kill criterion in embryo.

**How to apply:**
1. Imagine it's 12 months from now and this initiative failed. Why?
2. For each cause of failure, name the earliest observable signal.
3. For each signal, pre-commit the action ("if we see X by date Y, we do Z").

### Write kill criteria as state + date

The single most tactical tool from *Quit*.

> "If I am (or am not) in a particular state at a particular date, then I have to quit."
> — Duke, *Quit* (via wisdomfromexperts.com; **quote confidence: moderate**)

**Canonical case: Everest 1996.** Standard turnaround time on Everest is ~1–2 pm. Rob Hall and Scott Fischer summited late with Doug Hansen. Descending in a storm, both Hall and Hansen died. Ed Viesturs, watching from Camp 4, saw the mistake as it happened. The kill criterion existed (turnaround time). It was violated because summit was so close, and identity ("we are summiters") overrode the pre-committed rule.

**How to apply:**
1. State: what observable condition triggers the quit? (Not "if things aren't going well" — that's not observable. "If active users drop below 500 for two consecutive weeks" is observable.)
2. Date: when do you check? Bind it to a calendar day, not "next quarter."
3. Write it down *before* you're emotionally locked in. After commitment, motivated reasoning erodes the criterion.
4. Share it with someone who will hold you to it (see: quitting coach).

### Backcast — imagine success, trace back the path

Complement to the premortem.

**How to apply:**
1. Imagine it's 12 months from now and this initiative succeeded wildly. What did the path look like?
2. What had to happen at month 3, month 6, month 9? Which of those are you actually set up to make happen?
3. Pair with premortem: backcasting motivates the goal; premortem stress-tests the path.

### Keep a decision journal

Purpose: **fight hindsight bias**.

> "Hindsight bias is the great enemy of learning from experience."
> — via grahammann.net notes on *How to Decide* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**What to record per decision:**
- What you decided.
- Why (the reasoning, not the outcome).
- What alternatives you considered.
- Your confidence (percentage).
- What you'd need to see to change your mind.

**Later:** when the outcome lands, re-read the entry *before* letting your memory reconstruct it. Hindsight is not 20/20 — hindsight is a lying reconstruction.

### Run group decisions with hygiene — the 3Ds / nominal group technique

Duke's most-cited group process, from Lenny 2024:

1. **Discover.** Collect opinions independently *in writing* before any discussion. Prevents anchoring on the first speaker or the loudest.
2. **Discuss.** Surface disagreements. Use "nevertheless" to hold both positions — a way to disagree while validating.
3. **Decide.** Re-collect independently after discussion. Averages of independent judgments beat conforming group votes.

This is the classical **nominal group technique**, and it's what **First Round Capital's investment protocol** does explicitly.

### Shorten your feedback loops

Long feedback loops let bad decisions compound before you learn from them.

> "Long feedback loops are a choice."
> — Duke, Lenny's Podcast 2024 (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**How to apply:** find correlated intermediate signals. In VC, don't wait for exit — track time-to-Series-A. In products, don't wait for retention — track activation. Wherever a long loop feels unavoidable, ask: what earlier proxy correlates?

### Find a quitting coach

Kahneman's line, which Duke uses constantly:

> "What everybody needs is a friend who really loves them but doesn't care much about hurt feelings in the moment."
> — Kahneman, via Duke, *Quit* (**quote confidence: moderate on wording**)

**Founder-world equivalent:** Ron Conway. Model: pretend to agree the founder can turn it around, set very short kill criteria ("let's define success in one week"), hold them to it.

## Don'ts

### Don't "trust your gut" unqualified

Duke doesn't reject intuition — she insists it must be **disciplined**. System 1 is correct for Nadal's forehand. It's not correct for whether to raise a Series B. The trap is treating strategic decisions like a reflex.

### Don't celebrate an outcome without inspecting the process

Poker version: bad-beat stories where a player played a hand badly and won. Business version: startup succeeded because founder happened to be in the right place at the right time — and now the founder claims the playbook works. Duke: separate the two. Winning does not vindicate the process.

### Don't use "we couldn't have known" as an excuse

Duke pushes back on this phrase hard. It's how post-hoc narratives absolve bad process. Often you *could* have known — you just didn't ask.

### Don't say "hindsight is 20/20"

*"Hindsight is a lying reconstruction."* The past looks knowable now because you're back-filling it with what happened. That's why you need the decision journal — written *before* the outcome.

### Don't use grit as unconditional virtue

Grit is Angela Duckworth's contribution and Duke respects it. But *"grit is good conditional on the goal being worth it."* Unconditional grit produces Siobhan O'Keeffe running the London Marathon on a broken fibula, mile 6.

### Don't treat sunk cost as a reason to continue

*"We've invested too much to stop"* is the sunk cost fallacy stated as an excuse. **The money and time are gone either way.** The only question is: given today, is the next dollar worth spending?

### Don't use "quitter" as a pejorative

Duke is actively rehabilitating the word. Quitting on time is a skill, not a moral failure. The stigma is exactly what keeps people locked into losing paths.

### Don't say "I'm not ready to decide yet"

> "For now, I choose the status quo."
> — Duke, *Quit* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

Deferring a decision *is* a decision. Own it as such.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### Pedestal-building looks like progress

Astro Teller's metaphor, adopted by Duke: to train a monkey to recite Shakespeare from a pedestal, you must (a) train the monkey and (b) build the pedestal. The pedestal is easy. The monkey is impossible. Teams instinctively build pedestals — visible progress on the tractable part — while never testing whether the monkey can be trained.

**Canonical case: California high-speed rail.** Engineers built easy flatland track for years before addressing the two mountain ranges that were the actual engineering challenge. The flatland track was a pedestal.

**How to apply:** ask "what's the monkey here? Have we made any progress on the monkey specifically?" If the answer is "no, but we've made great progress on X" — X is the pedestal.

### Goal-induced blindness

Once a goal is set, people barrel through changed conditions. **Canonical case: Siobhan O'Keeffe, 2019 London Marathon.** Broke her fibula at mile 6. Kept running. Finished the marathon. The goal ("finish this marathon") had overtaken the underlying purpose ("run a marathon in good health as an accomplishment").

**How to apply:** every kill criterion should be pre-committed *before* the goal-induced blindness sets in.

### "Not ready to decide yet" = default to status quo

See "don'ts" above. The most common way people defer is by re-labeling deferral as "gathering more information." Beyond a point, more information is a stall.

### Identity-driven inability to quit

The hardest quit is who you are. Sears was retail; abandoning retail meant not being Sears. Sasha Cohen was a competitive figure skater; not skating meant not being Sasha Cohen. Watch for identity-language in the resistance to quitting ("we're a hardware company", "I'm an entrepreneur, not an employee").

### Value proposition mistaken for competitive advantage (borrowed observation, applies here too)

A pretty pitch that any competitor can copy tomorrow is not a moat. Same failure mode as celebrating a hand you won by luck — mistaking a nice-looking artifact for evidence of underlying quality.

## Cognitive biases Duke hammers

Duke's active roster of biases she names and coaches around:

- **Sunk cost fallacy.** Spending more because you've already spent.
- **Loss aversion.** *"Losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good."*
- **Endowment effect.** Overvaluing what you own (goods AND positions publicly staked).
- **Status quo bias.** Defaulting to current state because deciding is effortful.
- **Identity threat.** Refusing information that contradicts who you think you are.
- **Confirmation bias.** Seeking data that supports what you already believe.
- **Motivated reasoning.** Reasoning to the conclusion you want; numeracy amplifies rather than fixes this.
- **Hindsight bias.** Rewriting your past prediction to match the outcome.
- **Resulting.** Duke's coined term for the confusion of DQ and OQ (subsumes several of the above).

## Pro tips (accelerators — small devices with high leverage)

### The "wanna bet?" prompt

When someone (or you) claims certainty, ask "wanna bet?" It's rhetorical. The point is to notice that the claim is really a bet, and force calibration.

### 10-10-10

10 minutes / 10 months / 10 years. How will you feel at each horizon? Reveals whether emotional weight matches actual weight. Introduced in *Thinking in Bets*, expanded in *How to Decide*.

### Happiness test

*"Will my happiness, a week from now, depend on this decision?"* If no, decide fast; don't burn cycles on it.

### Only-option test

If this were the only option available, would you take it? If yes to multiple options, flip a coin. Both are fine. Save deliberation for decisions where the options actually differ.

### Two-way door test

Bezos-style. Reversible? Move fast. Irreversible? Slow down. Most decisions are two-way doors mislabeled as one-way.

### "Unlesses" — pre-commit to reassessment triggers

Kill-criteria-adjacent. When committing to a course, name the conditions that would trigger a reassessment. *"We'll go with X unless [signal], in which case we stop and re-evaluate."*

### "Nevertheless" — Duke's disagreement move

From Lenny 2024. Instead of "you're wrong," say "nevertheless" — hold both positions. Lets you disagree while validating the other person's reasoning. Softer entry into productive conflict.

### "I don't understand" instead of "you're wrong"

Duke recommends this explicitly (Lenny 2024). Reframes the disagreement as a question about your own comprehension, invites the other person to elaborate, and often surfaces the gap in their reasoning without requiring you to attack.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| "Trust your gut" | "Disciplined intuition" or "run the 10-10-10" | Intuition is trainable, not sacred |
| "Good call, you won" | "Good decision — how confident were you?" | Separates DQ from OQ |
| "We couldn't have known" | "What could we have asked that we didn't?" | Fights the post-hoc absolution |
| "Hindsight is 20/20" | "Let me check the decision journal" | Hindsight is a lying reconstruction |
| "We've invested too much to stop" | "The next dollar — is it worth it?" | Sunk cost is gone; waste is forward |
| "Quitter" | "Someone who quit on time" | Rehabilitating the word |
| "I'm not ready to decide" | "For now, I'm choosing status quo" | Owns the deferral as decision |
| "Trust the process" (without evidence) | "What does the calibration record say?" | Process claims need evidence |
| "Point probability" (for lay audiences) | "How promising do you think it is?" | Same calibration, different register |
| "You're wrong" | "I don't understand" / "Nevertheless" | Duke's disagreement moves |
