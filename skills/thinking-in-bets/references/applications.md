# Thinking in Bets — Applications

> When Duke's toolkit fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where Thinking in Bets fits well

- **Evaluating a past decision fairly.** Post-mortems and retros are Duke's home turf. The DQ/OQ separation is the single most useful move you can make on a completed outcome — good or bad.
- **Making a decision under uncertainty and wanting structure instead of a gut call.** The 6-step framework, the speed-triage tests, and the decision journal are exactly for this.
- **VC / founder / operator decision-making.** Long feedback loops, high stakes, high noise. Duke's own home domain — she is Special Partner at First Round Capital, and they built their protocol on her method.
- **Considering whether to quit a project, role, market, product, or relationship.** *Quit* (2022) is the entire book on this. Kill criteria, monkeys and pedestals, the quitting coach.
- **Setting up a new initiative and wanting kill criteria before you're emotionally locked in.** Duke's most tactical use: pre-commit the state + date before commitment.
- **Running a group decision with hygiene.** 3Ds / nominal group technique / First Round's protocol. Independent judgment before discussion, discussion, independent again.
- **Fighting hindsight bias in a team.** Decision journals are the antidote.
- **Career / life decisions.** Duke's most-viewed material (*How to Decide*, the 10-10-10, the happiness test) applies here directly.
- **Teaching decision-making to a team or to kids.** The Alliance for Decision Education material is designed for exactly this.
- **Recovering from a big loss.** Un-collapsing DQ from OQ is emotionally protective — it prevents the loss from being read as evidence of your process being broken (when it may or may not have been).

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Real-time reflex decisions where System 1 is correct.** Duke defends disciplined intuition. Nadal's forehand doesn't need the 6-step framework. The deer-in-headlights driver *over*-thinks and dies. The trap is treating strategic decisions like reflexes, not the reverse.
- **Routine operational decisions.** "What should we order for lunch?" doesn't need a decision journal. The happiness test screens these out fast: if it won't matter in a week, don't burn cycles.
- **Diagnosis-first strategy questions.** "What's actually broken in our business?" — that's a Rumelt question. Use his kernel (diagnosis / guiding policy / coherent actions) first to find the crux, then bring Duke's toolkit to the choices that fall out.
- **Choice architecture at scale.** If the question is "how do we structure the environment to nudge millions of people," reach for Sunstein / Thaler *Nudge* or behavioral-economics-in-policy frameworks. Duke's tools are for individual and small-group decisions.
- **Pure information-gathering.** If the user genuinely doesn't know enough to have any position, no toolkit helps. Learn first, decide later — but note that "learn first" beyond a point becomes the status-quo default (which is a decision).
- **When the user just wants a summary of one of the books.** Give them the book link. Don't run the method at them.

## How Duke's toolkit composes with other frameworks

Duke has commented on many of these in podcasts and essays.

### Kahneman — she is his popularizer

Duke accepts System 1 / System 2 wholesale. Her extension: **System 2 isn't always superior.** The deer-in-headlights driver *over*-thinks and dies. Nadal's forehand is System 1, and he'd be worse if he ran it through System 2 in real time. The skill is knowing when each is right — not defaulting to System 2 as always-correct.

She also uses Kahneman's **outside view** heavily. When you're evaluating your own situation, treat it as a reference class ("what happens to businesses like ours in situations like this?") rather than as unique.

### Klein — source of the premortem

Duke didn't invent the premortem — Gary Klein did. Duke's contribution is pairing it with **precommitment**. Without precommitment, the premortem is journaling. With precommitment, it's kill-criteria-in-embryo.

Klein's naturalistic-decision-making school is also the reason Duke defends disciplined intuition rather than rejecting it — Klein's RPD (recognition-primed decision) is the frame for when System 1 is not just tolerable but correct.

### Tetlock — calibration and forecasting

Duke's Alliance runs **Student Forecasting Tournaments** modeled directly on Tetlock's Good Judgment Project. She interviewed Tetlock on the Alliance podcast. The state+date format of kill criteria has the same DNA as Tetlock's forecasting-with-explicit-timeframes.

### Rumelt — methodological cousin

No direct citation surfaced between Duke and Rumelt, but they are methodological cousins. Both attack post-hoc narrative substitutes for real analysis:
- **Rumelt's diagnosis-first** = attack the illusion that you already know what's wrong.
- **Duke's DQ/OQ separation** = attack the illusion that outcomes tell you about process.

If a user is doing strategy work with hard choices under uncertainty, the natural sequence is: **Rumelt first (find the crux) → Duke on the choices that fall out (calibrate, set kill criteria, run 3Ds if it's a group call).**

### Duckworth (Grit) — counter-force, not rejection

Duke positions *Quit* as the missing counter-force to Duckworth's *Grit*. Not a hostile relationship. Her framing:

> "Quitting and grit are two sides of the same decision."
> — Duke, *Quit* (**quote confidence: moderate**)

Grit is good *conditional on the goal being worth it*. Unconditional grit produces Siobhan O'Keeffe on a broken fibula. If a user is reading Duckworth in isolation, point them at Duke to complete the picture. If they're reading Duke in isolation, point them at Duckworth for the same reason.

### Munger / Farnam Street / Shane Parrish — natural allies

Mental-models culture is Duke's adjacent tribe. Parrish has hosted her twice (Ep #37 and a follow-up). If the user is deep in the Farnam Street world, this skill and the Duke material sit naturally alongside their reading.

### Astro Teller / X — she's a *user* of his framework

Duke consulted at X and popularized **monkeys and pedestals** beyond it. Teller is the source of the metaphor. X is also cited by Duke as the institutional model for good quitting practices — X pays people bonuses for killing their own projects.

### Reid Hoffman / Masters of Scale — shared case library

Butterfield/Slack, Andrew Wilkinson/Flow, and similar founder-facing cases appear in both Duke's material and Hoffman's podcast. Adjacent audience.

### Sunstein / Nudge — Alliance overlap

Duke's decision hygiene (3Ds, decision journal, calibration) is nudge-adjacent: she is structuring the choice architecture of your own thinking. Alliance for Decision Education work overlaps with behavioral-econ-in-schools territory.

## Frameworks Duke has explicitly critiqued (not "killed" — she's more diplomatic than that)

Duke doesn't have Martin's "toss it into the ashbin" register. Her critiques are gentler, but pointed:

- **"Trust your gut" as unqualified advice.** Not wrong for Nadal's forehand; wrong for a Series B.
- **Unconditional grit.** Duckworth's *Grit* is fine in itself; the popular reading of "just keep going" is the problem.
- **"We couldn't have known" as post-hoc absolution.** Usually you could have — you just didn't ask.
- **"Hindsight is 20/20"** — no, hindsight is a lying reconstruction.
- **Sunk cost invoked as reason to continue.** The money is gone.
- **Deferring a decision as if it's not a decision.** For now, you're choosing status quo.
- **Celebrating outcomes without inspecting process.** Winning a coin flip does not vindicate your process.

If a user invokes any of these expecting them to work, redirect gently but clearly — in Duke's register, not Martin's.

## The composability principle

Duke's tools are unusually **composable**. They sit inside any strategic framework:

- **Inside Rumelt:** once you've diagnosed the crux, Duke helps you calibrate the guiding policy and set kill criteria for the coherent actions.
- **Inside Playing to Win:** once you've chosen WTP/HTW, Duke helps you evaluate whether the choice was good (regardless of outcome) and set state+date kill criteria if WWHTBT breaks.
- **Inside Lean Startup:** the build-measure-learn loop *is* small bets with feedback. Duke's kill criteria formalize what "learn" should trigger.
- **Inside OKRs:** OKRs are goal-setting; Duke's tools are the decision-making that gets you to the objectives. Not competing; different layers.

This is why the skill is worth having alongside strategic frameworks rather than instead of them. Duke gives you the *decision-making instrumentation* that goes inside any strategic frame.
