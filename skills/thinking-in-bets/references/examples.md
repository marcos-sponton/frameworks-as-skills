## Pete Carroll — Super Bowl XLIX (2015) — the canonical resulting case

**Where Duke uses it:** *Thinking in Bets* (opening chapter, 2018), every subsequent talk and podcast, TEDxGeorgetown.

**The situation:** Seattle Seahawks vs. New England Patriots. 26 seconds left. Seahawks on the Patriots' 1-yard line, down by 4. Instead of handing off to Marshawn Lynch (the obvious "correct" call), Coach Pete Carroll called a pass play. Malcolm Butler intercepted. Seahawks lost. Media savaged Carroll — "worst play call in Super Bowl history."

**Duke's read:** the *outcome* was disastrous. The *decision* was defensible. Interception rate on a pass play from that spot is ~1–2%. Given the clock, the number of downs remaining, and the defensive alignment, a pass was a reasonable call.

**Duke's line:**

> "Just because the result was a bad outcome did not make the decision itself bad."
> — Duke, paraphrased from *Thinking in Bets* opening chapter (via thepastorsworkshop.com; **quote confidence: moderate on wording, high on argument**)

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the cleanest illustration of resulting. Everyone can feel the pull to say "he made a terrible call" — the outcome was terrible. But that pull IS the bias. Duke uses this case to teach the reflex to separate DQ from OQ before saying anything else about a past outcome.

**How to use it:** whenever the user is evaluating a past outcome — theirs or someone else's — bring up Pete Carroll. It's the single most memorable anchor for the DQ/OQ separation.

## Mount Everest 1996 — Rob Hall, Doug Hansen, Scott Fischer — kill criteria violated

**Where Duke uses it:** *Quit* (2022), Alliance for Decision Education podcast Ep. 13 (with Dr. Ken Kamler, Everest ER doctor), multiple podcast appearances.

**The situation:** Standard turnaround time on Everest is 1–2 pm. If you're not on the summit by then, you turn around, no matter how close. Rob Hall and Scott Fischer, both experienced expedition leaders, summited late with client Doug Hansen. Descending in a storm, Hall and Hansen both died. Fischer died separately. Ed Viesturs, watching from Camp 4, saw the mistake as it happened.

**Duke's read:** the kill criterion existed. It was violated. Why? Because summit was *right there* and identity ("we're summiters") overrode the pre-committed rule. The state (past turnaround time) and the date (that specific day) were both met — but the emotional pull of being close overrode the rule.

**Why the case is instructive:** it demonstrates that kill criteria only work if you commit to them **before** you're emotionally locked in. Once you're 100 vertical feet from the summit, no amount of pre-commitment survives if you've allowed yourself to reconsider. The rule must be *unlike* what you'll want to do in the moment.

**How to use it:** when the user is setting up a new initiative, cite Everest 1996 to make the case for pre-commitment BEFORE emotional commitment sets in. Explicit format: state + date. Written down. Shared with someone who will hold them to it.

## Sasha Cohen — identity-driven inability to quit

**Where Duke uses it:** *Quit* (2022), various podcasts.

**The situation:** Sasha Cohen, 2006 Turin Olympic silver medalist in figure skating. In the years after her Olympic performance, she kept attempting comebacks. Duke uses her arc to illustrate identity-driven inability to quit — "skater" was self-concept, and quitting meant not being Sasha Cohen.

**Duke's read:** the hardest quit is *who you are*. When your identity is anchored to a role, activity, or achievement, quitting feels like self-erasure. The endowment effect applies not just to objects owned but to positions publicly staked.

**Why the case is instructive:** it moves the identity discussion out of the abstract and into a specific person with visible costs. Cohen wasn't foolish — she was doing what identity capture makes people do.

**How to use it:** when the user is resisting quitting something and the language they're using is *identity language* ("I'm an entrepreneur, not a corporate employee", "we're a hardware company"), reach for Cohen. Then ask: what would you be if you weren't the identity you're currently holding?

## Stewart Butterfield / Slack — successful quitting

**Where Duke uses it:** *Quit* (2022), First Round Review Podcast.

**The situation:** Butterfield had spent years and ~$17M building **Glitch**, a multiplayer online game. It wasn't working. Buried inside the Glitch team, they'd built an internal chat tool they were using themselves. Butterfield killed Glitch and pivoted to that chat tool — which became **Slack**, later acquired by Salesforce for $27.7B.

**Duke's read:** poster child for successful quitting. Butterfield quit on time — and it *felt* like quitting too early (he'd spent years, raised significant capital, had a working game). But the underlying signals said the game wasn't going to reach a big enough market. He respected the signals and pivoted.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows that quitting is the *enabling* move for the next bet. If Butterfield had gritted through Glitch, Slack wouldn't exist. Also demonstrates the "quitting on time feels like quitting too early" principle in a case where the outcome is celebrated.

**How to use it:** when the user is considering a pivot and is worried it's "too early," cite Butterfield. The feeling of "too early" is the *right* feeling for a well-timed quit.

## Andrew Wilkinson / Flow — failure to quit

**Where Duke uses it:** *Quit* (2022), podcast appearances (paired with Butterfield as counter-example).

**The situation:** Wilkinson spent years and ~$10M building **Flow**, an Asana competitor. He has publicly said he wishes he'd quit sooner. The market signals were there earlier; sunk cost + endowment + founder identity kept him going.

**Duke's read:** the counter-example to Butterfield. Same class of decision (should we keep going or pivot?), opposite call, worse outcome. What Butterfield did well is exactly what Wilkinson didn't — he respected the sunk cost as sunk, and asked "the next dollar — is it worth it?" and answered honestly.

**Why the case is instructive:** it pairs with Butterfield. Duke often uses them together — same decision shape, opposite calls, opposite outcomes. Prevents the reader from concluding "quit whenever it's hard" (which is not the lesson) — the lesson is calibrate the signal, respect kill criteria, and be willing to make the call before you're forced to.

**How to use it:** when the user is convincing themselves that "just a little more time" will turn it around, cite Wilkinson. Ask: what signals do you already have that you're rationalizing away?

## Sears (retail vs. financial services) — organizational identity capture

**Where Duke uses it:** Deloitte interview ("art of quitting"), *Quit* (2022), podcast appearances.

**The situation:** Sears had a thriving portfolio of financial services businesses in the 1980s–1990s: **Dean Witter** (brokerage), **Discover Card**, **Allstate Insurance**. All were growing and profitable. Sears' retail business was declining and structurally troubled. Instead of pivoting into financial services, Sears divested those businesses to "focus on the core" — the identity-defining retail business. The retail business continued to decline. The divested businesses (Discover, Allstate) went on to be worth many multiples of what remained of Sears.

**Duke's read:** organizational-level identity capture. Sears' *identity* was retail. Being "the store where America shops" was foundational. Divesting the growing businesses to save the declining identity-defining one was the emotional decision — not the strategic one.

**Why the case is instructive:** it scales the identity-capture insight from individuals (Sasha Cohen) to organizations. Same mechanism, larger stakes. Also demonstrates that "focus on the core" is often not strategy — it's identity defense in strategy language.

**How to use it:** when a company is doubling down on a declining line while divesting a growing one, and the language is "focus," cite Sears. Ask: is the "core" strategic or identity?

## California high-speed rail — pedestal-building

**Where Duke uses it:** First Round Review Podcast, *Quit* (2022).

**The situation:** California high-speed rail project. The two mountain ranges that the line has to cross are the actual engineering challenge — tunneling through hard rock, at scale, in an earthquake-prone region. Engineers built years of easy flatland track first. The flatland track looks like progress but doesn't answer the question: can we actually cross the mountains?

**Duke's read:** classic pedestal-building. The monkey is "can we cross the mountains?" The pedestal is "can we build flatland track?" Everyone built the pedestal, no one trained the monkey. Years and billions later, the project is famously troubled — because the actual hard question was never addressed early enough.

**Why the case is instructive:** it takes an abstract concept (monkeys and pedestals) and grounds it in a specific, visible, expensive failure. Also demonstrates the *institutional* incentives that produce pedestal-building — visible progress reports look better than "we don't know if the hard part is possible."

**How to use it:** when a team is racking up visible wins on the tractable parts of a project without addressing the hard question, ask: what's the monkey? Have you made any progress on the monkey specifically?

## Astro Teller / X (Alphabet's moonshot factory) — institutional quitting discipline

**Where Duke uses it:** *Quit* (2022), the "Monkeys and Pedestals" YouTube clip, behavioralscientist.org.

**The situation:** X (formerly Google X) is Alphabet's moonshot factory. Its official method includes **monkeys-first** as institutional practice: attack the hardest, most uncertain part of a project first. X **kills projects fast**. X *pays people bonuses for killing their own projects*. Duke consulted at X.

**Duke's read:** X is the institutional model for what quitting-as-discipline looks like. Most orgs punish killing projects (implicit or explicit — you get resource-starved next time you propose something). X rewards it. This changes the incentive structure so people surface bad news early instead of hiding it.

**Teller's line that Duke cites:**

> "This is exactly why X produces such outsized returns. Not because we're perfect at what we aspire to, but because we're so relentlessly aspiring to it."
> — Astro Teller, via Duke, behavioralscientist.org (**quote confidence: moderate**)

**Why the case is instructive:** it demonstrates that quitting-as-discipline is possible at scale — but requires institutional design (incentives, culture, process) that most orgs don't have. Also the source of the "monkeys and pedestals" metaphor.

**How to use it:** when the user is discussing how to build a healthier decision culture in their org, cite X. Ask: does your org reward or punish killing a project?

## Ron Conway — quitting coach in founder-world

**Where Duke uses it:** First Round Review Podcast, *Quit* (2022).

**The situation:** Ron Conway is a legendary angel investor (SV Angel). Duke uses him as the model of a **quitting coach** in the founder world. His method (as Duke describes it): pretend to agree the founder can turn it around, then set very short kill criteria ("let's define success in one week"), then hold them to it. If the founder can't hit the one-week target, the conversation about winding down starts from a place the founder has already partially conceded.

**Duke's read:** Conway operationalizes Kahneman's "friend who really loves you but doesn't care much about hurt feelings in the moment." He doesn't confront the founder head-on — he sets a short, unambiguous test, then holds the line.

**Why the case is instructive:** it shows that a quitting coach doesn't have to be adversarial. The most effective form is warm, aligned in stated intent, and unyielding on the pre-committed test. This is how Duke recommends founders and executives find a quitting coach — not "someone who tells you to quit," but "someone who holds you to the kill criteria you set."

**How to use it:** when the user is asking who should hold them accountable, describe Conway's method. Then ask: who in your life has that combination of warmth and willingness to hold you to a hard rule?

## Siobhan O'Keeffe / 2019 London Marathon — goal-induced blindness

**Where Duke uses it:** First Round Review Podcast, *Quit* (2022).

**The situation:** Siobhan O'Keeffe was running the 2019 London Marathon. She broke her fibula at mile 6. **She ran the remaining 20 miles.** She finished the marathon.

**Duke's read:** the canonical goal-induced blindness case. The goal ("finish this marathon") had overtaken the underlying purpose ("run a marathon in good health as an accomplishment"). Once a goal is set, changed conditions get ignored — the running-on-a-broken-leg version of "we've invested too much to stop."

**Why the case is instructive:** it's visceral. Nobody hears this story and thinks "she made the right call." But the mechanism that produced her decision is the same mechanism that produces founders continuing on a project that isn't working, executives staying in roles they should have left, and organizations doubling down on declining lines.

**How to use it:** when the user has a goal and the conditions around them have changed (market shift, personal circumstances, new information) but they haven't updated the goal, cite O'Keeffe. Ask: what changed since you set this goal? Have you updated?

## First Round Capital investment process — instrumented decision protocol at scale

**Where Duke uses it:** Lenny's Podcast 2024, various interviews.

**The situation:** Duke is a Special Partner at First Round Capital. She helped design their explicit decision-recording system for investment decisions.

**What the protocol includes:**
- **1–7 scale on multiple dimensions per deal.** Not a single yes/no.
- **Decision journal per partner.** Each partner writes their reasoning *before* the collective discussion.
- **Calibration tracked over time.** Partners see whether their 5s are outperforming their 3s over years.
- **Individual partner strengths mapped.** Some partners are better on certain dimensions; the protocol makes this legible over time.

**Duke's read:** this is what her toolkit looks like when instrumented at institutional scale. It's the nominal group technique + decision journals + calibration training, applied to a live VC portfolio.

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the most fully implemented real-world example of Duke's method. When a user asks "how would this actually work at scale?" — First Round is the answer.

**How to use it:** when the user is asking how to build a healthier decision culture in a team or organization, describe First Round's protocol. Ask: which piece could you start with? Usually the decision journal is the lowest-friction entry.
