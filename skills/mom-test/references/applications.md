# The Mom Test — Applications & Adjacent Frameworks

> Where The Mom Test fits, where it doesn't, and how it composes with other frameworks. This file exists to prevent two failure modes: (1) applying The Mom Test where a different tool is needed, and (2) confusing The Mom Test for a complete product-development methodology when it's actually the conversational layer within one.

## Where The Mom Test fits

### Early-stage problem validation
You have an idea. You think people have a problem. You need to find out if they actually do, how painful it is, and what they're currently doing about it. **This is The Mom Test's home turf.** The method tells you exactly how to have those conversations without corrupting the data.

### Pre-product customer interviews
Before you've built anything. You're talking to potential customers to understand their world. The Mom Test gives you the rules for keeping those conversations honest.

### Feature validation
You have a product, and customers are requesting features. The Mom Test helps you dig beneath the request to find the actual need. (The analytics/export example from the book: they didn't want analytics — they wanted pretty charts for presentations.)

### Discovery calls for early sales
You're doing sales conversations where you also need to learn. The Mom Test helps you separate the learning mode from the selling mode — and warns you that mixing them corrupts both.

### Evaluating customer feedback quality
You've been doing interviews. You have notes. The Mom Test gives you tools to classify the data: is it facts (gold), compliments (fool's gold), or fluff (noise)?

### Internal stakeholder conversations
The Mom Test's principles apply beyond customers. Interviewing internal stakeholders (executives, ops teams, support reps) about their problems follows the same rules: talk about their work, not your proposal. Ask about specifics in the past. Listen more than you talk.

## Where The Mom Test does NOT fit

### Quantitative research
Surveys, A/B tests, analytics, cohort analysis, statistical significance — The Mom Test is purely qualitative. It tells you how to have conversations. It doesn't replace quantitative methods.

### Post-PMF customer research
NPS surveys, CSAT measurement, churn analysis, support-ticket mining — once you have product-market fit and a substantial user base, the research methods change. The Mom Test is for the search phase.

### Corporate strategy
"What markets should we be in?" "How do we win against competitors?" These are strategy questions, not conversational-method questions. Reach for Playing to Win (Martin), Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Rumelt), or 7 Powers (Helmer).

### Formal UX research
If you need sampling plans, coding frameworks, thematic analysis, statistical power calculations — you need formal research methodology, not The Mom Test. The Mom Test is founder-pragmatic, not research-rigorous.

### Sales execution
If the goal is to close a deal, not to learn, The Mom Test's rules don't apply. Fitzpatrick explicitly separates learning conversations from sales conversations. Trying to "Mom Test" a closing call will confuse your prospect and lose the deal.

## Adjacent Frameworks and How They Compose

### Customer Development (Steve Blank)

**Relationship:** The Mom Test is the *conversational layer* within Customer Development. Blank's method (The Four Steps to the Epiphany, 2005) defines the strategic process: Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, Company Building. The Mom Test answers: "OK, I'm doing Customer Discovery — but HOW do I actually talk to customers without getting lied to?"

**Composition:** Use Blank's Four Steps for the macro process. Use The Mom Test for the micro — how to run each conversation within Customer Discovery and Customer Validation.

**Fitzpatrick's acknowledgment:** He credits Steve Blank as the intellectual lineage. The Mom Test was born because "none of the books about sales or Customer Development seemed to help me figure out how to actually do it properly."

### The Lean Startup (Eric Ries)

**Relationship:** The Lean Startup provides the strategic framework (Build-Measure-Learn, MVP, pivot-or-persevere). The Mom Test provides one specific tool within the "Learn" phase — how to learn from customer conversations. They're complementary, not competing.

**Composition:** Use Lean Startup for the overall learning loop. When the "Learn" step involves talking to humans, apply The Mom Test. When it involves metrics, A/B tests, or analytics, use Lean Startup's Innovation Accounting devices.

**Key difference:** Lean Startup is a complete methodology for building under uncertainty. The Mom Test is a conversational tool. The Mom Test doesn't prescribe what to build, when to pivot, or how to measure — it only tells you how to talk to people honestly.

### Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres)

**Relationship:** Torres's method (2021) provides the *cadence and structure* for ongoing customer research: weekly customer touchpoints, Opportunity Solution Trees, assumption mapping, experiment design. The Mom Test provides the *conversational rules* for the customer touchpoints themselves.

**Composition:** Use Torres for the system — how often to talk to customers, how to organize what you learn, how to connect insights to product decisions. Use The Mom Test for the conversations — how to ask questions that produce facts, not opinions.

**Key difference:** Torres's method is broader (it includes assumption testing, experiment design, and product-decision frameworks). The Mom Test is narrower (just the conversation). Torres assumes you know how to run a good conversation; The Mom Test teaches you how.

### Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) — Christensen / Moesta / Kalbach

**Relationship:** JTBD provides a *framework for understanding why customers make choices* — the job they're hiring a product to do, the forces of progress, the switching dynamics. The Mom Test provides *rules for the conversations* in which you uncover those jobs.

**Composition:** Use JTBD to frame what you're trying to learn (what job is the customer hiring for? what forces are at play?). Use The Mom Test to design the actual questions and guard against bad data during the JTBD interview.

**Key difference:** JTBD is an analytical framework. The Mom Test is a conversational method. You can do JTBD interviews badly (by asking "would you hire this product?" — a hypothetical future question that violates Rule 2). Applying The Mom Test to JTBD interviews makes them better.

### The Challenger Sale / JOLT Effect (Matt Dixon)

**Relationship:** Challenger Sale is a *selling* methodology — how to teach, tailor, and take control in B2B sales. The Mom Test is a *learning* methodology. Fitzpatrick explicitly separates learning mode from selling mode.

**Where they touch:** Discovery calls. In enterprise sales, early conversations need to be both learning and selling. Fitzpatrick would say: separate them. Learn first (Mom Test rules), sell second (Challenger moves). Don't mix, because mixing corrupts the learning.

**When to use which:** If you're pre-product and trying to understand the problem: Mom Test. If you have a product and you're trying to win a deal: Challenger. If you're doing both simultaneously: split the conversation into two phases, or split into two separate meetings.

### Obviously Awesome (April Dunford)

**Relationship:** Dunford's positioning method asks "who are you for, what are you the best at, and what category do you compete in?" The Mom Test helps you gather the raw material for positioning — by understanding how customers actually describe their problem, what alternatives they consider, and what they value.

**Composition:** Use The Mom Test to gather the customer-language raw material. Use Obviously Awesome to synthesize it into positioning. The Mom Test tells you what customers actually say; Dunford tells you what to do with it.

### Shape Up (Ryan Singer)

**Relationship:** Shape Up's "shaping" phase involves understanding the problem before committing to a solution. The Mom Test provides rules for the conversations that inform shaping.

**Composition:** Use Shape Up for project scoping and the build cadence. When the "shaping" work involves talking to customers or internal stakeholders about their problems, apply Mom Test rules to those conversations.

## The Honest Disagreement: Learning vs. Commitment Altitude

The Mom Test assumes you're in a learning environment where getting it wrong is cheap — early-stage, pre-product, or pre-PMF. When the stakes are high (Amazon-scale launches, regulated industries, public-company reputation), the bias shifts from "learn fast, get it wrong cheaply" to "get it right before you commit." This is the same tension described in `lean-startup/references/applications.md` between Lean Startup and Working Backwards.

The Mom Test doesn't address this directly — Fitzpatrick assumes you're in the learning phase. But be aware: if a user is doing customer conversations at commitment altitude (enterprise sales, regulated products, high-reputation-risk situations), the conversational rules still apply, but the "move fast and iterate" assumption behind them may not.
