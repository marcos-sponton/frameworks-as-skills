# The Mom Test — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> **This is the load-bearing file of this skill.** Most people who "do customer interviews" are actually pitching and collecting compliments. The heuristics below exist because Fitzpatrick has spent a decade teaching people to notice what they're doing wrong. When the user shows up with "everyone says they'd buy it" or "I asked 20 people and they all loved the idea" — this file is what the skill needs to load first.

## Meta-heuristic

**Fitzpatrick's core diagnostic:** If you're getting consistently positive feedback from customer conversations, you're probably asking the wrong questions. Real customer conversations produce messy, contradictory, sometimes discouraging data. Clean, positive results are a symptom of bad questions, not good product-market fit.

> "You shouldn't ask anyone whether your business is a good idea. It's a bad question and everyone will lie to you at least a little." — *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

## The Bad Questions Family

### "Would you use a product that does X?"

**Why it's bad:** Hypothetical question about future behavior. People say yes to be supportive. Their "yes" costs them nothing and commits them to nothing.

**Better:** "How do you currently deal with X?" / "When's the last time X happened? What did you do?" / "What have you tried so far?"

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### "How much would you pay for X?"

**Why it's bad:** Hypothetical money for a hypothetical product. People are generous with imaginary budgets.

**Better:** "How much does this problem cost you now?" / "What are you currently spending on [current solution]?" / "What budget did you allocate for this last year?"

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### "What would your dream product do?"

**Why it's bad:** Invites fantasy, not facts. You'll get a wish list of features disconnected from their actual workflow and priorities.

**Better:** "Talk me through the last time you dealt with this." / "What's the hardest part about [process they already do]?" / "What takes the most time?"

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### "Do you think it's a good idea?"

**Why it's bad:** This is the original Mom Test failure. You're asking for an opinion about your idea. Opinions are cheap, supportive, and useless.

**Better:** Don't mention your idea at all. Ask about their life. *"I'm trying to understand how teams handle [problem]. Can you walk me through how your team does it?"*

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### "Would you buy this?"

**Why it's bad:** Hypothetical future purchase. The answer is almost always yes. The question is designed to produce false positives.

**Better:** "Can I get your pre-order right now?" / "Would you be willing to sign a letter of intent?" / "Can I schedule a demo with your team next week?" (These are commitment requests — the answer tells you something real.)

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 5 (Commitment and Advancement).

## DO's — How Fitzpatrick says to run conversations

### Ask about their life, not your idea

Enter the conversation with no agenda to pitch. Your goal is to understand their world. What are they working on? What frustrates them? What takes too long? What have they tried to fix?

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, Rule 1.

### Anchor fluff in the past

When someone says "I usually..." or "I would definitely..." — pull them back to a specific recent event. *"You mentioned you usually do X — when's the last time? Walk me through what happened."*

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 2.

### Dig beneath feature requests

When someone says "it should have analytics" — don't write down "add analytics." Ask: *"Why do you want that? What would you do with the analytics? Walk me through the last time you needed data like that."* The feature is a symptom; the job is underneath.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 2 (analytics/export example).

### Deflect compliments

When someone says "that's so cool!" or "I love it!" — don't write it down as validation. Instead: *"Thanks — but help me understand: when's the last time you actually dealt with this problem?"*

> "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless." — *The Mom Test*, ch. 2.

### Push for commitment at the end

Don't end the meeting with "thanks for your time, that was really helpful." End with an ask: *"Would you be willing to introduce me to your head of ops?" / "Can I send you a prototype next week and get 15 minutes of your feedback?" / "Would you be open to a paid pilot?"*

The answer tells you whether they actually care.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 5.

### Know your three questions

Enter every conversation knowing the three most important things you need to learn. One of them should be a "scary" question — something that could invalidate your entire business if the answer is wrong. If you're not asking questions that could kill your idea, you're not learning.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 6 (Preparation).

### Look for the graveyard

Ask what solutions they've abandoned and why. *"Have you tried anything else to solve this? What happened?"* The graveyard of abandoned solutions tells you what didn't work and why — which is often more valuable than knowing what they're currently doing.

**Source:** Brian Rhea's "Bright & Early" podcast with Fitzpatrick, 2026.

## DON'Ts — The anti-patterns Fitzpatrick names

### The pitch disguised as a learning conversation

You set up a "customer interview" but actually spend 80% of the time explaining your product and asking "what do you think?" This is the most common failure mode. You get compliments, not learning. You feel validated. Your product still fails.

**How to detect it:** Record the conversation (or have a teammate observe). If you're talking more than 20% of the time, you're pitching. If you mentioned your product in the first five minutes, you're pitching.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1 (The Pathos Problem).

### "Everyone says they'd buy it"

The most dangerous sentence in customer development. If everyone says they'd buy it, you almost certainly asked leading questions. Real demand shows up as commitments, not as promises.

**Fitzpatrick's test:** Replace "everyone says they'd buy it" with "everyone I pitched to said nice things." Is that still compelling? No? Then you don't have validation.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 5.

### Asking about the future instead of the past

Future-tense questions produce future-tense answers — which are essentially fictional. People genuinely believe future-them will behave differently than present-them. They won't.

> "People truly believe future them is going to help, but future me doesn't even want to see friends on Saturday that I agreed to on Friday." — Fitzpatrick, Brian Rhea podcast, 2026.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, Rule 2; Brian Rhea "Bright & Early" podcast, 2026.

### Treating opinions as data

"I think that would be really useful" is not data. "I spent $2,000 last year trying to solve this" is data. The first is an opinion; the second is a fact about past behavior. Only facts about past behavior survive the Mom Test.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### The fishing-for-compliments question

"Don't you think this is a problem?" or "Wouldn't it be great if you could...?" These questions telegraph the answer you want. The customer will agree because disagreeing is socially costly.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### Running conversations without segmentation

If you're talking to VPs, freelancers, and mid-level managers and treating their feedback as one dataset, you'll get contradictory signals that cancel out. The VP wants enterprise features. The freelancer wants simplicity. The mid-level manager wants team collaboration. Mixing their feedback is worse than talking to nobody — it gives you false confidence in a non-existent average customer.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 7.

### Seeking validation instead of information

The most insidious anti-pattern. You're not consciously asking bad questions — you just unconsciously steer conversations toward answers that confirm your hypothesis. You ask about the problem more energetically when they seem to have it. You probe deeper on features they like. You skim past the parts where they describe workarounds that make your product unnecessary.

**How to detect it:** After the conversation, ask yourself: *"Did I learn anything that surprised me or that I didn't want to hear?"* If every conversation confirms your hypothesis, you're probably seeking validation, not information.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1; Fitzpatrick, Indie Hackers AMA.

## Gotchas — Things that trip people up

### "If they haven't looked for solutions, they're not going to buy yours"

A customer who says "yeah, that's a problem" but has never spent time, money, or effort trying to solve it... doesn't actually have a painful problem. Pain is measured by action, not acknowledgment.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### The customer is not always right about the solution

They know their problem. They don't know your product design space. Feature requests from customers are inputs, not instructions. The insight is always underneath the request.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 2 (analytics/export example).

### Warm introductions corrupt data

When someone introduces you as "my friend who's building this amazing product," the conversation is poisoned from the start. The introduced person will be supportive because of the social pressure from the mutual connection.

**Mitigation:** Ask for intros, but ask the introducer to frame it as "someone trying to understand [problem]," not "someone building a product."

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 8.

### Note-taking as interpretation

If only one person attends the meeting and summarizes it from memory later, the notes will reflect their optimism bias, not the conversation. Key quotes get paraphrased into gentler versions. Concerns get downplayed. Enthusiasm gets amplified.

**Fix:** Two people in the meeting. One talks, one takes notes with exact quotes. Debrief with the full team immediately.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 9.

### Premature closure

You had five conversations. All five said the problem exists. You declare the problem validated and start building. But you never asked: how painful is it? What are they currently doing about it? How much are they spending? Would they switch? The problem exists — but it might be a paper cut, not a bleeding wound.

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### The "idea nihilism" trap

The opposite of seeking validation: becoming so skeptical that you dismiss every positive signal and never commit to building anything. Fitzpatrick himself fell into this after his early startup failures.

> "I'd adopted 'idea nihilism' unnecessarily — I later recognized that many factors ARE predictable about ideas." — Fitzpatrick, Indie Hackers AMA.

The Mom Test is a tool for learning, not for proving that every idea is bad.

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, Indie Hackers AMA.
