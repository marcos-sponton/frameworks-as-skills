# The Mom Test — Worked Examples

> Real cases Fitzpatrick has used publicly to illustrate the method's principles. Fictional composites from the book are also included where they illustrate a specific principle that no public case covers.

## 1. The Mom Conversation (the namesake example)

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

The book's opening scenario. You tell your mom about your new app idea — a recipe app, say. You ask her: "Would you use an app that helps you find new recipes?"

She says: "Oh, that sounds lovely! Yes, I'd definitely use that!"

You leave feeling validated. But Mom said yes because she loves you and doesn't want to discourage you — not because she'd actually use a recipe app. She's never searched for a recipe app. She doesn't currently have a problem finding recipes. She answered a hypothetical question about future behavior with a supportive answer.

**The fix:** Instead of pitching the idea and asking for opinions, ask about her life: "When's the last time you tried a new recipe? Where did you find it? How did that go?" If she says she makes the same five meals every week and hasn't tried a new recipe in years, you just learned that she doesn't have the problem your app solves — which is vastly more useful than her compliment.

**Principle illustrated:** Rule 1 (talk about their life, not your idea) + Rule 2 (specifics in the past, not generics about the future).

## 2. The Analytics/Export Feature Request

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 2.

A customer asks for "analytics and export capabilities" in the product. The team writes it down as a feature request and starts spec'ing an analytics dashboard with CSV export.

But when someone digs deeper — "What would you do with the analytics? Walk me through the last time you needed data like that" — it turns out the customer just needs pretty charts they can paste into a presentation for their boss. They don't need analytics. They don't need CSV export. They need a "make my boss happy" button.

The feature request (analytics + export) and the actual need (impressive-looking charts for a presentation) lead to very different products. The analytics dashboard is expensive to build, complex to maintain, and solves the wrong problem. A "generate pretty report" button is cheap, simple, and solves the actual problem.

**Principle illustrated:** Dig beneath feature requests. The feature is not the insight — the motivation underneath is.

## 3. Fitzpatrick's YC Startup — Shifted to Fit Investors

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, Indie Hackers AMA; The Knowledge podcast with David Elikwu.

Fitzpatrick went through YCombinator in Summer 2007. He and his cofounders were "enamored by the idea of the investors" and shifted their idea to fit what investors wanted: "We pulled it pretty far away from what we wanted and we ended up feeling stuck for the next three or four years."

The result: a company built on extrinsic motivations (investor approval, perceived success) rather than intrinsic ones (caring about the problem). When the extrinsic rewards dried up, they had nothing left.

> "We'd chosen things we didn't really care about... All we've got left is extrinsic motivations, which is like success... when that stuff dried up we were like, why are we doing this?" — Fitzpatrick, The Knowledge podcast.

**Principle illustrated:** The Mom Test applied to investor conversations. When you pitch to investors and shape your idea around their feedback, you're violating Rule 1 (talking about their investment thesis, not your understanding of the customer). Investors can validate that your idea is investable — they can't validate that customers have the problem.

## 4. The "Idea Nihilism" Trap

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, Indie Hackers AMA.

After his early startup failures, Fitzpatrick swung to the opposite extreme. Instead of blindly validating ideas, he started dismissing every idea. He'd adopted "idea nihilism" — the belief that you can't predict anything about an idea before testing it.

He later realized this was wrong: "Many factors ARE predictable about ideas." Idea selection matters. Not every idea deserves the effort of customer development.

**Principle illustrated:** The Mom Test is a tool for learning, not for proving that every idea is bad. It's possible to over-apply skepticism to the point where you never commit to anything. The method should produce actionable learning, not permanent paralysis.

## 5. The Product Built for Sony and MTV

**Source:** momtestbook.com; Indie Hackers AMA.

Fitzpatrick's earlier companies built products used by Sony and MTV. These are mentioned as credentials but also as cautionary context — having big-name customers doesn't mean you have product-market fit. It means you have customers. The distinction matters.

**Principle illustrated:** Social proof (big logos) is a form of compliment at the company level. The question isn't "who uses it?" but "why do they use it, what problem does it solve for them, and would they pay more / switch if you disappeared?"

## 6. The 300,000-Copy Book with No Email List

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, The Knowledge podcast; Indie Hackers AMA.

The Mom Test has sold over 300,000 copies. Fitzpatrick did this without an email list, without a marketing budget, without fancy launch campaigns. The book grew entirely through word of mouth.

> "I have maybe 10,000 Twitter followers now, but I've sold way more books than I have Twitter followers... there's something wrong there." — Fitzpatrick, The Knowledge podcast.

**Principle illustrated:** This is The Mom Test's own principles applied to its own distribution. The book solves a sharp problem for a specific person at a specific moment in their journey. People who have the problem recommend it to others who have the problem. No pitch required — the product does the work.

## 7. The Graveyard of Abandoned Solutions

**Source:** Brian Rhea's "Bright & Early" podcast, 2026.

Fitzpatrick recommends asking customers about solutions they've tried and abandoned. What did they try? Why did they stop? What was missing?

The "graveyard" reveals what didn't work and why — which is often more informative than knowing what they're currently using. If they tried three tools and abandoned all three, you know the problem is real (they keep trying to solve it) AND you know three approaches that failed (so you can avoid repeating them).

**Principle illustrated:** Past behavior over future intentions, extended. Not just "what are you doing now?" but "what did you try and stop doing?" The graveyard is a richer data source than the current state.

## 8. The Bowling Lane Approach

**Source:** Brian Rhea's "Bright & Early" podcast, 2026.

Fitzpatrick's metaphor for structuring conversations:

> "Put the bumpers on to keep them out of the gutters — nudge them back on track when they wander, then dig deeper into specifics about their past."

The conversation should be natural, not scripted. But it shouldn't be completely unstructured either. You have your three key questions (the lane). When the conversation wanders into irrelevant territory (the gutters), you gently redirect. When it touches something relevant, you dig deep.

**Principle illustrated:** The Mom Test is not "just have a chat." It's a structured conversation with specific goals, gentle guardrails, and the discipline to dig when you find something interesting.

## 9. The Beta-Reading-as-Customer-Development Example

**Source:** Fitzpatrick, The Knowledge podcast; Write Useful Books.

When writing Write Useful Books, Fitzpatrick applied The Mom Test's principles to book development. He put the book through beta reading — watching real readers interact with the manuscript and learning from their behavior, not their opinions.

> "The dream of customer development is to get to watch people doing their work with no bias... the quality of learning is so good." — Fitzpatrick, The Knowledge podcast.

He even quit books during beta reading when the signals were wrong: "I have given up on other books... during beta reading you start to get signals about whether it's used by your readers."

**Principle illustrated:** The Mom Test's principles are fractal. They apply to any domain where you're trying to learn from humans — not just customer interviews for startups. Watch behavior, not opinions. Look for commitment signals (did they finish the chapter? did they recommend it?), not compliments ("it's really well-written!").

## 10. The Five-Element Meeting Frame in Practice

**Source:** *The Mom Test*, ch. 8.

Fitzpatrick's cold-outreach framework in action. Instead of emailing "I'm building a product and I'd love to pick your brain," you structure the ask:

1. **Vision:** "We're trying to help ops teams reduce their monthly reporting burden."
2. **Framing:** "We're early — no product yet, just trying to understand the problem."
3. **Weakness:** "We're not sure which reporting tasks are the most painful."
4. **Pedestal:** "You've been running ops at [company] for 8 years, so you'd know better than anyone."
5. **Ask:** "Could I take 15 minutes to ask you about your reporting workflow?"

The structure works because it gives people a specific way to help (not "pick your brain"), makes them feel valued for their expertise (not targeted for their wallet), and removes sales pressure (no product to pitch).

**Principle illustrated:** The meeting framing structure. Getting the conversation in the first place is half the battle — and how you frame the request determines whether they show up as an expert helping a learner (good) or a prospect being sold to (bad).
