# The Mom Test — Method

> The canonical description of Rob Fitzpatrick's method in his own terms. Fidelity is the point — softening these rules collapses the method into generic "ask open-ended questions" advice. Load `heuristics.md` alongside this file: the rules below produce signal only when the anti-patterns are actively guarded against.

## The definition that anchors everything

The Mom Test is named for a simple litmus test:

> "The Mom Test: Talk about their life instead of your idea. Ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future. Talk less and listen more." — *The Mom Test*, Rob Fitzpatrick, 2013.

The test is: could you ask this question to your mom and get a useful, unbiased answer? If yes, it's a good question. If the only way you'd get a useful answer is from someone who'd buy your product, you're pitching, not learning.

The underlying insight: **people lie.** Not maliciously — they lie to be supportive, to avoid conflict, to seem helpful, to protect your feelings. Your mom would tell you your idea is great because she loves you. Your potential customers do something similar, just with less affection and more social awkwardness. The method exists to neutralize this bias by asking questions that can't be lied to.

## The Three Rules

### Rule 1: Talk about their life, not your idea

The moment you mention your idea, you've corrupted the conversation. People will respond to the idea — with compliments, with polite encouragement, with "that's interesting" — instead of telling you about their actual life and problems.

**What this means in practice:**
- Don't pitch. Don't describe your product. Don't explain what you're building.
- Ask about the problem space, not the solution space.
- Learn how they currently deal with the problem (or whether they even have the problem).
- Understand their workflow, their constraints, their priorities.

**The pathos problem:** When you expose your ego by sharing your idea, people instinctively protect it. They become supportive, not honest. Fitzpatrick calls this the "pathos problem" — the emotional dynamic where revealing vulnerability (your idea, your startup, your dream) triggers protective responses that feel like validation but aren't.

> "The more you talk about your idea, the more people will tell you what you want to hear." — *The Mom Test*, ch. 1.

### Rule 2: Ask about specifics in the past, not generics about the future

People are terrible at predicting their own future behavior. They're reasonably good at describing what they've already done.

**What this means in practice:**
- "Would you use X?" is a worthless question. "When's the last time you dealt with [problem]?" is a useful one.
- "How much would you pay?" is hypothetical money for a hypothetical product. "What are you paying now for [current solution]?" is a real number.
- "What would your dream product do?" invites fantasy. "What did you do the last time this happened?" invites facts.
- Anchor every conversation in concrete past events. Names, dates, actions, outcomes.

**The fluff test:** Fitzpatrick defines three types of fluff that corrupt conversations:
1. **Generic claims:** "I usually..." "I always..." "I never..."
2. **Future-tense promises:** "I would definitely..." "I will..." "I'd pay..."
3. **Hypothetical maybes:** "I might..." "I could see myself..."

All three are noise. When you hear them, anchor: *"When's the last time that actually happened?"* or *"Can you walk me through the most recent time?"*

### Rule 3: Talk less, listen more

If you're talking more than they are, you're pitching. The purpose of the conversation is to learn, and you learn by listening, not by explaining.

**What this means in practice:**
- Your job is to ask a question and then shut up.
- Resist the urge to fill silences — the customer will often fill them with exactly the information you need.
- Don't correct them when they say something "wrong." Don't explain why your approach is different. Just listen and take notes.
- The ideal ratio: they talk 80%+, you talk 20% or less.

## Bad Data: The Three Types

Fitzpatrick categorizes the data you get from conversations into useful and useless. Three types of bad data corrupt your learning:

### 1. Compliments

> "Compliments are the fool's gold of customer learning: shiny, distracting, and worthless." — *The Mom Test*, ch. 2.

"That's so cool!" "I love that idea!" "Definitely send me the link when it launches!" These are not data. They're social niceties. People give compliments when they want to be supportive (or when they want the meeting to end). The cure: **deflect compliments and get back to facts.**

When someone compliments your idea, don't thank them and write it down as validation. Instead, redirect: *"Thanks — but let me ask: when's the last time you actually dealt with this problem?"*

### 2. Fluff (generics, hypotheticals, the future)

Fluff is any statement about what people "would" do, "usually" do, or "might" do — as opposed to what they actually did. Three flavors:

- **Generic claims:** "I always check my email first thing." (Do you? Every day? Walk me through this morning.)
- **Future promises:** "I would definitely buy that." (But you haven't. What are you doing instead right now?)
- **Hypothetical maybes:** "I could see that being useful for our team." (Has your team ever looked for something like this? What happened?)

**The anchoring move:** When you hear fluff, pull the conversation back to earth: *"You mentioned you always do X — when's the last time? Can you walk me through it?"*

### 3. Ideas (feature requests without motivation)

When customers suggest features, the feature itself is usually not the insight — the motivation behind it is. Fitzpatrick's canonical example: a customer asks for "analytics and export capabilities." Dig deeper: they actually just need pretty charts they can paste into a presentation for their boss. The feature request (analytics + export) and the actual need (impress the boss) lead to very different products.

**The dig-beneath move:** When someone suggests a feature, ask: *"Why do you want that? What would you do with it? Talk me through the last time you needed something like that."*

## Commitment and Advancement

This is Fitzpatrick's mechanism for telling whether a conversation produced real signal or just pleasant noise.

### The core principle

A meeting went well only if it ends with a **commitment** — the customer gives up something they value. Compliments cost nothing. Commitments cost something.

> "It's not a real sales lead until you've given them a concrete chance to reject you." — *The Mom Test*, ch. 5.

### Three currencies of commitment

1. **Time commitment:** A scheduled follow-up with clear goals. Agreeing to review a prototype. Testing a trial product. Attending a demo with their team. (Not: "let me know when you launch.")
2. **Reputation commitment:** Introducing you to their boss. Connecting you with a peer who has the problem. Giving a public testimonial. (Not: "I know someone who might be interested — I'll think about it.")
3. **Financial commitment:** A letter of intent. A pre-order. A deposit. Paying for a pilot. (Not: "I'd definitely pay for that" — hypothetical money is not money.)

### Advancement vs. spinning

**Advancement** means the customer moved to the next step in your real-world funnel — closer to actually using or buying your product. **Spinning** means the meeting was pleasant but nothing moved forward. Most "validation" conversations are spinning.

**Test:** After the meeting, can you name the specific next step AND did the customer commit to it? If not, the meeting spun.

## The Five Elements of Meeting Framing

Fitzpatrick's structure for opening a customer conversation — especially when you're approaching someone cold or semi-cold:

1. **Vision:** You're an entrepreneur trying to solve a problem (establishes why you're there, without pitching the solution).
2. **Framing:** Where you are in the process — "we're early, we don't have a product yet, we're trying to understand the problem" (sets expectations; removes sales pressure).
3. **Weakness:** The specific thing you need help with — "we're not sure who actually has this problem most acutely" (gives them a concrete way to contribute).
4. **Pedestal:** Why THEY specifically are the right person — "you've been running ops teams for 10 years, so you'd know" (makes them feel valued for their expertise, not their wallet).
5. **Ask:** The specific request — "could I take 15 minutes to ask you about how you handle [problem]?" (clear, bounded, low-risk).

This structure gets people to help you without triggering sales resistance. They're being asked for their expertise, not their credit card.

## Segmentation

### The problem with unsegmented conversations

If you're talking to three different types of people (enterprise buyers, SMB owners, freelancers) and aggregating their feedback, you'll get contradictory signals that cancel each other out. Enterprise wants integrations; freelancers want simplicity; SMB wants price. Mixing their feedback is like averaging directions — you end up going nowhere.

### The rule

Before you have product-market fit, every customer conversation should be sliced by segment. When you notice contradictory feedback, the most common cause is that you're talking to multiple segments and treating them as one.

### How to segment

Fitzpatrick doesn't prescribe a segmentation method — he says you should look for patterns in *who has the problem most urgently*, *who is already spending money or time on it*, and *who was most excited* (backed by commitment, not just compliments). When a conversation produces a strong signal, ask: who is this person? What type are they? Who else is like them?

## Note-Taking Protocol

Fitzpatrick is specific about how to capture conversations:

- **Two people in the meeting:** One leads the conversation (asks questions, maintains rapport). The other takes notes (captures exact quotes, emotional reactions, key facts). Typing on a laptop during the meeting looks rude; use paper or notecards.
- **Capture exact quotes.** Don't paraphrase — exact quotes are evidence. Paraphrases are your interpretation of evidence, which is different.
- **Use symbols for fast classification:**
  - Exact quote (quotation marks)
  - Emotional reaction (capture the emotion, not just the content)
  - Pain point or problem (e.g., a lightning bolt symbol)
  - Goal or aspiration (e.g., a goal-post symbol)
  - Feature request (mark it, but remember: the feature is not the insight)
  - Follow-up task or commitment (what did they agree to do?)
- **Debrief with the full team immediately after.** Share exact quotes, not summaries. Discuss what was learned, what was surprising, what needs to change in the next conversation.
- **Meta-review:** After every few conversations, review the process itself. Which questions produced the best answers? Which ones fell flat? What patterns are emerging across conversations?

## When to Stop

Fitzpatrick's signal that you've learned enough from conversations:

- **The conversations are getting boring.** You're hearing the same answers to the same questions. No new information is emerging.
- **You can predict what they'll say.** Before they answer, you already know. That's pattern saturation.
- **Three to five conversations is often enough for a given question.** Not for the whole discovery process — for a specific question. When three people in a row tell you the same thing, you probably have the answer. Move to the next question.

The danger is not stopping too early; it's stopping too late — continuing to have conversations as a procrastination strategy to avoid building and testing.
