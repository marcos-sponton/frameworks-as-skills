# Demand-Side Sales — Applications

> When the demand-side lens fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where the demand-side lens fits well

- **You're trying to understand why customers buy, switch, or churn.** This is the core use case. The Forces diagram and timeline reveal causation, not just correlation.

- **Your conversion is broken and you don't know why.** Moesta's framing: you're probably obsessing over Pull (features, marketing) while Anxiety and Habit are winning unopposed. Map the Forces to find where the equation breaks.

- **You have a better product but customers won't switch.** Classic Habit + Anxiety problem. The product is fine; the forces against progress are stronger than the forces for it.

- **You're designing a sales process from scratch.** Build it around the buying timeline, not around your internal workflow. Different conversations at each stage.

- **You need to kill features.** Features that increase Anxiety instead of Pull should be cut. The demand-side lens gives you the diagnostic: does this feature reduce the customer's anxiety or add to it?

- **You're launching a new product and need to understand demand.** Interview users of the products yours would replace. "What would they fire if yours succeeded?"

- **You're redesigning onboarding.** First Use must feel like progress fast. The demand-side lens tells you what "progress" means to the customer — which is rarely what your team thinks.

- **You're doing customer research and getting shallow results.** Switch Interviews go deeper than satisfaction surveys, NPS, or "what features do you want?" questions. They reconstruct the causal chain.

- **Your marketing isn't working despite a good product.** Probably selling features (supply-side) instead of visualizing progress (demand-side). Casper's ZzzQuil ad worked because it addressed the customer's struggle, not the mattress specs.

- **You're trying to understand a career decision** (your own or someone else's). Job Moves applies the Forces to career switches. The Four Quests categorize why people leave.

- **You're building a hiring or retention strategy.** INDUSTRY 2025 extended the method: treat jobs as "products" employees hire. Retention is a Forces problem.

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **You want quantitative unmet-need scoring, job maps, or outcome statements.** That's Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation (ODI). Same JTBD axiom, different method entirely. Ulwick is quantitative (surveys, scoring); Moesta is qualitative (interviews, stories). Don't mix them without understanding the difference.

- **You want a Hypothesis Canvas or a structured Frame/Discovery process.** That's Jim Kalbach's JTBD Playbook. Kalbach synthesizes Ulwick and Moesta. His deliverable (Hypothesis Canvas) is his own artifact. Compatible with Moesta, but distinct.

- **You want supply-side strategic JTBD — "what business are we in?", disruption analysis.** That's Christensen's strategic-mindset variant. Moesta built the method; Christensen built the theory. For disruption analysis, read *Competing Against Luck* and *The Innovator's Dilemma*.

- **You want a technique-first sales method — objection handling, closing sequences, discovery call scripts.** That's Challenger Sale, SPIN, Sandler territory. Moesta insists on theory before technique. "Schools don't teach techniques; they teach theory."

- **You're doing market sizing or TAM analysis.** The demand-side lens is qualitative and causal. It tells you *why* people buy, not *how many will*. For quantification, use Ulwick's ODI or traditional market research — but use demand-side insights to define the segments.

- **You're doing pure UX research (usability, interaction patterns, information architecture).** The demand-side lens is about the buying decision and the forces around it. Once the product is in use, UX research tools (usability testing, analytics, task analysis) take over.

- **You're pre-product and need to choose a market.** The demand-side lens works best when there are buyers to interview. Pre-product, combine with lean startup search methods. Moesta's method kicks in once you have your first few switches to reconstruct.

## Adjacent frameworks — when to reach for a different one

| If the user's situation is... | Reach for... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative unmet-need scoring / job maps | **Ulwick's ODI** | Same JTBD axiom, different method — surveys, outcome statements, unmet-need scores |
| Hypothesis Canvas / Frame-Discovery-Design | **Kalbach's JTBD Playbook** | Synthesis of Ulwick + Moesta, with Kalbach's own artifacts |
| "What business are we in?" / disruption analysis | **Christensen's strategic JTBD** | The theory-level strategic lens. Read *Competing Against Luck*. |
| Sales technique (objection handling, closing) | **Challenger Sale** / SPIN / Sandler | Supply-side technique. Moesta gives theory; Challenger gives moves. They compose. |
| Weekly customer touchpoints / opportunity discovery | **Continuous Discovery Habits** (Torres) | Different unit (opportunities vs. progress+forces). CDH is operating cadence; Switch Interviews are periodic deep-dives. |
| Positioning the product in the market | **Obviously Awesome** (Dunford) | Downstream of Forces. Once you know the progress and competition, positioning becomes precise. |
| Designing the sales narrative | **Strategic Narrative** (Raskin) | Downstream of demand-side understanding. The narrative frames the "change in the world" that created the struggling moment. |
| Strategy-level diagnosis | **Good Strategy Bad Strategy** (Rumelt) | Different level. Rumelt diagnoses the organizational crux; Moesta diagnoses the customer's buying forces. Compose: diagnose (Rumelt) + understand demand (Moesta). |
| Market-choice cascade (where to play / how to win) | **Playing to Win** (Martin) | Different level. Martin makes strategic choices; Moesta understands customer demand. Demand-side insight feeds Martin's "where to play." |
| Growth loops and channel strategy | **Four Fits** (Balfour) | Different scope. Balfour designs the growth system; Moesta understands why each customer enters the system. |
| Network effects and cold start | **The Cold Start Problem** (Chen) | Different scope. Chen solves the chicken-and-egg; Moesta's Forces explain why early users switch (or don't). |

## How the demand-side lens composes with other frameworks

### With Challenger Sale — compose, don't replace

Challenger is supply-side: how to sell (teach, tailor, take-control). Moesta is demand-side: why people buy (Forces, timeline, struggling moments). They're not competitors — they're complementary.

**Composition:** use Moesta to understand the buying timeline and the Forces. Then use Challenger to design the sales interaction *within* each timeline stage. Teach during Passive Looking. Tailor during Active Looking. Take control during Deciding — but take control *of the trade-off framing*, not of the customer.

**Where they clash:** Challenger's "take control" move can feel supply-side if applied without demand-side understanding. Moesta would say: don't take control of the sale — take control of helping the customer see their own Forces clearly. The customer should feel progress, not pressure.

### With Continuous Discovery Habits (Torres) — different cadence, same commitment to customer evidence

Torres: weekly customer touchpoints, Opportunity Solution Trees, assumption testing. Moesta: periodic Switch Interview deep-dives (10-12 interviews per round), Forces diagrams, timelines.

**Composition:** run Torres's weekly cadence as the operating heartbeat. Run Moesta's Switch Interviews quarterly or when launching a new product, entering a new segment, or investigating churn. Torres gives you breadth (many touch points, continuous). Moesta gives you depth (causal understanding of specific switches).

**Key contrast:** Torres's unit is "opportunities" (unmet needs / pain points). Moesta's unit is "progress + forces." Torres builds Opportunity Solution Trees. Moesta builds Forces diagrams + timelines. Both are evidence-driven; they organize the evidence differently.

### With Obviously Awesome (Dunford) — demand-side feeds positioning

Dunford's 5-component positioning method requires you to know your competitive alternatives, your unique attributes, the value you deliver, your target customers, and the market category. The demand-side lens fills in most of these:

- **Competitive alternatives:** what the customer would hire if your product didn't exist (from the Switch Interview).
- **Unique value:** the progress your product enables that alternatives don't (from the Forces diagram).
- **Target customers:** people in the specific struggling moment your product addresses (from the Switch Interview recruiting).

**Composition:** do demand-side research first (Switch Interviews, Forces, timeline). Then run Dunford's positioning exercise with the demand-side data as input. The positioning becomes precise because it's grounded in causal customer data, not competitive speculation.

### With Christensen's JTBD — same axiom, different altitude

Christensen owned the theory ("people hire products to make progress"). Moesta owns the method (Switch Interview, Forces, timeline). They worked together for 27 years.

**Composition:** use Christensen for strategic-level thinking ("what business are we actually in?", "what disruption threatens us?") and Moesta for operational-level research ("why did this specific customer switch?", "what forces are blocking adoption?").

**Where they diverge:** Christensen's framing is philosophical and strategic; Moesta's is operational and causal. Christensen's deliverable is a strategic insight; Moesta's is a Forces diagram + timeline + verbatim quotes. Both are valid; they serve different purposes.

### With Ulwick's ODI — sibling, not competitor

Same JTBD axiom. Completely different method. Ulwick is quantitative: surveys, unmet-need scoring, job maps, structured outcome statements. Moesta is qualitative: Switch Interviews, Forces diagrams, timelines, verbatim language.

**Composition:** use Moesta to discover the Jobs (qualitative, causal). Use Ulwick to prioritize the outcomes within a known Job (quantitative, statistical). Moesta finds *which* Job to serve. Ulwick finds *which outcomes* within that Job are most underserved.

**Where they clash:** Moesta distrusts survey data for Job discovery ("never ask people what they wish existed"). Ulwick builds his method around survey data. They disagree on the research method, not the theory. A practitioner who uses both must be clear about when each is appropriate.
