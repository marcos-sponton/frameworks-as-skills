# Demand-Side Sales — Examples

> Cases Moesta uses publicly, with the demand-side lesson from each. These are not summaries for their own sake — each case demonstrates a specific mechanism of the method.

## Classic canon — the recurring cases

### The milkshake (McDonald's, with Christensen)

**The story:** McDonald's wanted to sell more milkshakes. They did focus groups, improved flavors, varied sizes — nothing moved the number. Moesta and Christensen observed the restaurant and interviewed actual buyers.

**What they found:** two entirely different Jobs, same product.

**Job 1 — morning commute.** Buyer profile: solo adults, before 8am, drive-through only. The milkshake was hired for a long, boring commute. Requirements: one hand on the wheel, lasts the whole drive, not messy, gives a small reward before a long day. Real competitors: bananas (boring, done in 2 minutes), bagels (dry, crumbs), donuts (guilty, sticky fingers), boredom (doing nothing).

**Job 2 — afternoon with kids.** Buyer profile: parents with children, after 3pm. The milkshake was hired to be a "good parent" — saying yes to something after saying no all day. Real competitors: other treats, but the emotional Job was about the parent's self-image, not the kid's preference.

**Demand-side lesson:** Same product, two completely different Jobs. You cannot optimize a product for a Job you haven't identified. The morning milkshake should be thicker (lasts longer) with bigger chunks (something to discover). The afternoon milkshake should be smaller (kid finishes it) and come faster (parent is tired). One product strategy for both Jobs guarantees mediocrity at both.

**Forces at work (Job 1):**
- **Push:** boredom, long commute, need something to do with one hand.
- **Pull:** something that lasts the whole drive, small reward, no mess.
- **Anxiety:** guilt about an unhealthy breakfast (low — it's a treat, not a meal).
- **Habit:** most people had a routine (coffee, radio) that the milkshake needed to beat.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 1-2; *Competing Against Luck* (2016); Lenny's Podcast #1.

### The mattress (jobstobedone.org Switch Interview)

**The story:** documented Switch Interview available on jobstobedone.org. A customer who hated their old mattress, moved houses, *dragged the old mattress to the new house*, dealt with spouse dynamics, and eventually bought a new one after physical pain became unbearable.

**Demand-side lesson:** This case demonstrates every force in vivid detail.

**Forces:**
- **Push:** back pain ("the spring dug into my back"), waking up tired, spouse complaints.
- **Pull:** imagining sleeping through the night, feeling rested, spouse being happy.
- **Anxiety:** "What if the new one isn't better?", cost of a good mattress, the hassle of delivery and disposal.
- **Habit:** *they dragged the old mattress to the new house.* This is the Habit force in its purest form — even when you hate the current solution, the comfort of the familiar pulls you back.

**Timeline insight:** the First Thought happened *years* before the purchase. The customer spent months in Passive Looking (reading reviews casually), weeks in Active Looking (visiting stores), and the tipping point was a specific night of pain. The timeline stretched much longer than anyone selling mattresses would assume.

**Source:** jobstobedone.org — [The Mattress Interview Part One](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/the-mattress-interview-part-one/); *Competing Against Luck*.

### Snickers vs. Milky Way

**The story:** same candy bar category, same basic ingredients (chocolate, nougat, caramel). Completely different Jobs.

**Snickers Job:** quick fuel between meetings. Hired for: density, sustenance, no mess (no chocolate on fingers because of the cookie layer), can eat on the move. Real competitor: protein bar, quick snack, skipping the snack entirely.

**Milky Way Job:** dessert, reward, indulgence. Hired for: taste experience, slow enjoyment, treat at the end of the day.

**Demand-side lesson:** category is not Job. Products that look identical in a category analysis serve completely different struggling moments. Competing on category terms ("our chocolate bar is better than their chocolate bar") misses the point. Snickers competes with protein bars and nothing; Milky Way competes with ice cream and cookies.

**Source:** DSS101; multiple podcast appearances.

### The dining room table (homebuilder, downsizers)

**The story:** A homebuilder was selling smaller homes to empty-nesters who were downsizing. Buyers said they didn't want a dining room — too much space, don't need it. The builder eliminated dining rooms from the floor plans.

Sales dropped. The builder couldn't figure out why.

Switch Interviews revealed the emotional/social Job: empty-nesters needed to know they could *host the family for holidays*. They didn't need a dining room they used daily — they needed the *possibility* of Thanksgiving dinner. The builder put a table-size space back into the plans (not a formal dining room — just enough room for a table). Sales increased 22%.

**Demand-side lesson:** stated preferences are not Jobs. What customers *say* they want (no dining room) and what they *need to feel* (I can still host my family) can be opposite. Only the Switch Interview — with its focus on emotional and social dimensions — reveals the gap.

**Forces:**
- **Push:** current house is too big, maintenance burden, kids are gone.
- **Pull:** simpler life, less maintenance, right-sized space.
- **Anxiety:** "Will I lose my identity as the family host?" "Will the holidays happen somewhere else?" "Am I giving up my role?"
- **Habit:** 30 years of Thanksgiving at this table. The table *is* the family identity.

The builder's mistake was addressing the functional Job (right-size the house) without the emotional/social Job (preserve the family host role). Adding the table space addressed the Anxiety without adding significant cost.

**Source:** DSS101; Lenny's Podcast #1; Intercom podcast.

### Basecamp calendar

**The story:** Basecamp customers loudly demanded a "calendar feature." The product team could have built a full calendar (9-month build). Instead, they ran Switch Interviews to understand the Job.

**What they found:** customers didn't need a calendar. They needed to see *available time slots* — "when can we meet?" The underlying Job was "coordinate a meeting time without email ping-pong."

**Solution:** a "dot view" showing available dates (6-week build instead of 9 months). Customers were delighted because it solved the actual Job, which a full calendar would have buried under complexity.

**Demand-side lesson:** feature requests are not Jobs. The stated request ("I need a calendar") masks the actual struggle ("I need to coordinate meeting times without hassle"). Building the literal request would have been slower, more complex, and *increased Anxiety* (a full calendar is intimidating and hard to learn). Building for the Job produced a simpler, faster solution.

**Forces:**
- **Push:** email ping-pong to find meeting times.
- **Pull:** instantly seeing when everyone is available.
- **Anxiety (of the full calendar):** "now I have to manage another calendar?", "will it sync with my other calendars?", "what if it's complicated?"
- **Anxiety (of the dot view):** almost none — it's simple by design.

**Source:** DSS101; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Casper vs. ZzzQuil

**The story:** Casper (mattress company) needed to understand their real competition. Category analysis would say Tempur-Pedic, Sleep Number, etc.

Switch Interviews revealed the real competitor: **ZzzQuil** (a sleep aid). Customers were hiring ZzzQuil to solve the same struggling moment — "I can't sleep well." ZzzQuil was the cheaper, easier, lower-anxiety alternative to buying a new mattress.

**Marketing insight:** Casper ran an ad: *"How many bottles of ZzzQuil before you realize you need a new mattress?"* Sales increased 37%.

**Demand-side lesson:** the real competition is what the customer would hire to make the same progress if your product didn't exist. This is almost never the obvious category competitor. Understanding the real competition changes everything — messaging, pricing, sales approach, onboarding.

**Source:** DSS101; Business of Software talk; multiple podcast appearances.

## Newer cases

### AutoBooks (fintech for SMB banking)

**The story:** AutoBooks provides accounting/invoicing tools for small businesses through their bank's platform. Sales cycle was long and conversion was low.

**Demand-side intervention:** Moesta helped them restructure around the buying timeline. Instead of one generic demo, they built three demos — one for each of the first three timeline stages:

1. **Passive Looking demo:** educational, shows what's possible, teaches language. "Here's what your business could look like if invoicing wasn't manual."
2. **Active Looking demo:** interactive, playground-style. "Play with it. See if this feels right."
3. **Deciding demo:** focused on trade-offs. Three options. "Here's the basic, here's the pro, here's the enterprise. The basic doesn't include X — is that important to you?"

**Result:** sales cycle cut ~50%. Conversion doubled. Valuation increased.

**Demand-side lesson:** "Same action (demo request) means completely different things at different stages." A Passive Looking demo request needs education. An Active Looking demo request needs exploration. A Deciding demo request needs trade-off clarity. One-size-fits-all demos serve none of them well.

**Source:** DSS101; Business of Software talk; Coleman McCormick notes.

### Facebook Marketplace (new product launch)

**The story:** Facebook was launching Marketplace — but they had no existing customers to interview. How do you run Switch Interviews without buyers?

**Moesta's approach:** interview users of the products that Marketplace would *replace* — Etsy, Craigslist, eBay. These are the products that customers would "fire" if Marketplace succeeded.

**Interview focus:** "Walk me through the last time you sold something on Craigslist. What was frustrating? What worked? What almost stopped you? What would you change?"

**Demand-side lesson:** when launching a new product, the Switch Interview still works — you just interview the *outgoing* side. The people who are struggling with the current solutions are the people who will hire your new solution if you address their Forces correctly.

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast #1.

### SNHU (Southern New Hampshire University)

**The story:** SNHU used JTBD (Moesta + Christensen, documented in *Choosing College*) to understand why adult learners enroll in online degree programs.

**What they found:** adult learners had Jobs completely different from traditional 18-year-olds:
- **Career pivot:** "I've been in this field 15 years and it's going nowhere. I need credentials to switch."
- **Kid respect:** "My kids are in school and I want them to see me learning too."
- **Personal proof:** "I never finished college and it's been a chip on my shoulder for 20 years."

These are emotional and social Jobs — not "I want to learn accounting." SNHU redesigned admissions, marketing, and onboarding around these Jobs. The result was massive enrollment growth and one of the most successful university transformations in US higher education.

**Demand-side lesson:** the struggling moment for adult learners is rarely academic. It's personal, emotional, and social. Selling "great curriculum" (supply-side) misses the Job entirely. Selling "your kids will see you learning" (demand-side) hits it.

**Source:** *Choosing College* (2019); Business of Software talk; SNHU case coverage.

### InVideo (anti-example)

**The story:** InVideo (online video editing tool) tried to serve two contradictory Jobs with one product for three years.

**Job 1 — Expert creators:** "Make my workflow faster." Want power tools, shortcuts, professional output.
**Job 2 — Beginner creators:** "Help me figure out what to do." Want templates, guidance, simple output.

**The result:** the expert features intimidated beginners (Anxiety). The beginner simplifications frustrated experts (Push). Three years of trying to serve both produced a product that frustrated everyone and grew slowly.

**Demand-side lesson:** you cannot serve contradictory Jobs with one product. The design decisions required for each Job are opposite — simplicity vs. power, guidance vs. speed, templates vs. flexibility. Diagnose which Job you're serving. Commit. Build the other as a separate product if you must.

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast #1; Re-Wired Group case work.

### Hudl (sports video analytics)

**The story:** Hudl is a client of The Re-Wired Group. They provide sports video analytics — coaches film games and practices, Hudl helps them analyze and share the footage.

**Demand-side insight:** the Job is not "analyze video" (functional). The Job is "look like a prepared, professional coach to my team, parents, and administration" (social) and "feel like I'm giving my players the best chance to improve" (emotional). The video is a tool in service of those Jobs.

**Source:** Re-Wired Group client work; Circuit Breaker podcast.

## Case pattern — what to notice

Across all cases, the same pattern repeats:

1. **The real Job is not the stated need.** Milkshake: not "I want breakfast" but "make this commute tolerable." Dining room: not "I don't need a dining room" but "I need to be able to host Thanksgiving." Basecamp: not "I need a calendar" but "I need to coordinate meeting times."

2. **The real competition is not the obvious competitor.** Casper: ZzzQuil. Milkshake: bananas, boredom. Snickers: protein bars, nothing.

3. **Features often increase Anxiety instead of Pull.** Basecamp: a full calendar would have been harder to learn. InVideo: expert features intimidated beginners.

4. **The timeline is longer than you think.** Mattress: years from First Thought to purchase. SNHU: the chip on the shoulder can be decades old.

5. **Emotional and social dimensions drive the decision.** Dining room: family identity. SNHU: kid respect. Hudl: coach reputation. The functional dimension is necessary but not sufficient.

6. **Habit is the hidden force.** The mattress customer dragged the old one to a new house. The dining room customer clung to the table space. Habit is not rational, and it's not addressed by better features.

**Source:** all cases above; DSS101; Lenny's Podcast #1.
