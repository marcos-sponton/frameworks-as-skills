# Demand-Side Sales — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> Moesta's practical devices — the operational moves that separate real demand-side work from JTBD theater. Attribution is precise: this comes from DSS101 (2020), this from Learning to Build (2022), this from Job Moves (2024), this from a specific podcast or talk.

## Do's

### Start from the customer's life, not your product

The first question is never about the product. It's about the customer's circumstance. "What's happening in your world?" before "What do you think of our product?"

**How to apply:** if the user opens with product features or competitive analysis, redirect: *"Before we talk about what you built — what's the struggling moment that makes someone start looking?"*

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 1-2; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Interview recent buyers, not prospects

The Switch Interview reconstructs a real past purchase. Prospects haven't bought yet — they can tell you what they *think* they'd do, which is speculation. Recent buyers can tell you what they *actually* did, which is causal data.

**Exception:** when launching a new product with no buyers, interview users of the products yours would replace — the products they'd fire if yours succeeded.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 7-9; jobstobedone.org.

### Ask "tell me more" and "give me an example" instead of "why?"

"Why?" drives people to rationalization. They'll give you the pablum layer — "it was easy", "it was a good price", "it had the features I needed." These are post-hoc rationalizations, not causes.

"Tell me more about that" and "give me an example" keep people in the story. Stories have context. Context has causation.

**Source:** DSS101; June.so interview method breakdown; SaaS Club #423.

### Play back incorrectly on purpose

Getting a "no" produces richer detail than a "yes." If you suspect the customer switched because of back pain, say "so it was really about the price?" They'll correct you passionately — and in the correction, they'll give you the real story with specificity and emotion.

**Source:** jobstobedone.org Switch Interview recordings; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Use the Columbo move

Ask the real question just as you're about to close the notebook. People relax when they think the interview is ending. The thing they've been holding back — the embarrassing reason, the emotional driver, the social pressure — comes out when the pressure is off.

**Source:** multiple podcast appearances; DSS101.

### Debrief for equal time

The interview is 50% of the work. The debrief with the cross-functional team is the other 50%. Synthesis, pattern-matching, and Force-mapping happen in the debrief, not during the interview. If you skip the debrief, you have anecdotes, not data.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 9; Intercom podcast.

### Prefer two rounds of 12 over one of 24

The first round of 10-12 interviews reveals patterns. Debrief, adjust your recruiting, sharpen your questions. The second round of 10-12 validates and deepens. One round of 24 without iteration is less valuable — you iterate too late.

**Source:** DSS101; Business of Software talk.

### Present exactly three options when the customer is Deciding

Never one, rarely two, never more than three. Design one "albatross" that's obviously wrong for the customer. They eliminate it first. Then they compare the remaining two — which gives them a choice architecture they can navigate and a story for why they chose.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 12-13.

### Help customers articulate why they chose

If the customer can't explain their choice to their boss, their spouse, or their team, the deal dies — either before the purchase (internal politics) or after (buyer's remorse). Give them the language. Frame the progress. Make them the hero of the story.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 14.

### Look for the real competition

The real competition is rarely the obvious category competitor. It's what the customer would hire to make the same progress if your product didn't exist.

- Casper's real competitor: ZzzQuil, not Tempur-Pedic.
- McDonald's milkshake's real competitor: bananas, bagels, boredom.
- Basecamp's real competitor: email chains and spreadsheets, not Asana.

**How to find it:** "What would you have done if this product didn't exist? What were you doing before?"

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 6; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Track all three dimensions of the Job

Every real Job has three dimensions active:
- **Functional** — the task to accomplish.
- **Emotional** — how the person wants to feel.
- **Social** — how the person wants to be perceived.

If you can only articulate the functional dimension, you're at the pablum layer. The emotional and social dimensions are where the real purchase energy lives.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 4; Learning to Build; jobstobedone.org.

## Don'ts

### Don't interview prospects for a Switch Interview

Prospects haven't made a decision. They can speculate about what they'd do, but speculation is not causation. Switch Interviews reconstruct *what actually happened*. Interview people who already bought (or who recently churned — they switched *away*).

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 7-8; repeated across all podcast appearances.

### Don't use a discussion guide

A discussion guide turns the interview into a structured Q&A. The Switch Interview is a story reconstruction. Follow the customer's narrative. Prompt with "tell me more" and "give me an example" when they reach the pablum layer. But don't direct the story.

**Source:** DSS101; jobstobedone.org.

### Don't ask "why?" repeatedly

"Why" triggers rationalization and post-hoc justification. "Why did you buy X?" gets you "because it was the best option" (pablum). "Walk me through the day you decided to look" gets you "it was a Tuesday, my back was killing me, and my wife said..." (causation).

**Source:** DSS101; Lenny's Podcast #1; June.so breakdown.

### Don't accept the first answer

The first answer is almost always a feature ("it had the best reviews") or a rationalization ("it was a good deal"). These are the *stated* reasons, not the *causal* reasons. Push past them: "Tell me more about that." "What was happening before that?" "What else were you considering?"

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 8.

### Don't skip the timeline

Jumping straight to the Forces without reconstructing the chronological sequence loses causation. The timeline reveals *when* each force became active, *what triggered* the transition between stages, and *how long* the customer spent in each stage. Without the timeline, you have a static diagram instead of a causal story.

**Source:** DSS101; Business of Software talk.

### Don't add features to reduce Anxiety

This is the most counterintuitive heuristic in the method. When customers express Anxiety ("what if it doesn't work?", "what if it's too complicated?"), the instinct is to add features that address the concern. But more features = more complexity = more to learn = *more* Anxiety.

Reduce Anxiety with guarantees, social proof, simplified onboarding, and "first win in 5 minutes" — not features.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 5; multiple podcast appearances.

### Don't confuse "bitchin'" with switching energy

> "Bitchin' ain't switchin'."

Complaints do not predict behavior. People complain about their current solution endlessly and never switch. Complaining is a release valve — it *reduces* pressure to change, not increases it. What predicts switching is a struggling moment that generates actual energy for progress.

**Source:** DSS101; Circuit Breaker podcast; LinkedIn.

### Don't build for contradictory Jobs simultaneously

The InVideo trap: serving experts and beginners with one product for three years. The expert Job ("make my workflow faster") and the beginner Job ("help me figure out what to do") require opposite design decisions. Trying to serve both produces a product that frustrates everyone.

Diagnose which Job you're serving. Commit. Build the other as a separate product if you must.

**Source:** Lenny's Podcast #1; Re-Wired Group case work.

### Don't try to close during Active Looking

Active Looking is playground time. The customer is exploring possibilities, wishing for features, imagining progress. They are NOT making trade-offs yet. Trying to close during Active Looking feels like pressure and pushes them backward (to Passive Looking or out entirely).

**Sales role in Active Looking:** expand possibilities. "Have you considered this?" "What would it look like if you could also...?" Don't close. Don't price. Don't push.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 11-12.

### Don't treat the Forces as a checklist

The Forces are in tension with each other. Push interacts with Habit. Pull interacts with Anxiety. Increasing Pull without addressing Anxiety often makes things worse. The skill is in understanding the *dynamics* between the forces, not filling in four boxes.

**Source:** DSS101; jobstobedone.org Forces diagram.

## Gotchas — common misapplications

### Persona substitution

Reframing personas as "jobs." "Millennials hire coffee to..." is wrong. Context is not persona. The same person has different Jobs in different contexts. The morning commute milkshake is a different Job from the Saturday afternoon milkshake, even though the same person buys both.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 2; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Survey-driven JTBD

Quantitative unmet-need scoring is Ulwick's ODI, not Moesta. They're sibling methods that share the core axiom but differ on everything else. A survey can validate patterns found through Switch Interviews — but it cannot *discover* them. Discovery is qualitative, causal, and story-based.

**Source:** GoPractice framework comparison; jobstobedone.org.

### Feature-driven JTBD

Writing "as a user I want X so that Y" and calling it a Job. This is a user story — a task statement. A Job is a progress statement in context. "When I'm stuck in traffic on a long boring commute, I need something to keep one hand busy and give me a small reward before a long day" is a Job. "As a user I want a thick drink" is not.

**Source:** DSS101; Lenny's Podcast #1.

### Job statements without Switch Interviews

Armchair Jobs — written at a whiteboard without talking to real buyers. They feel plausible but lack causation. If you can't point to a specific interview where a specific person told you the story, it's not a Job — it's a hypothesis.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapter 7; multiple podcast appearances.

### Collapsing Job into Task

The Job includes functional, emotional, and social dimensions. If your Job statement only captures the functional task ("get from A to B"), you've lost the emotional driver ("feel in control of my time") and the social dimension ("look competent to my team"). All three are active in every real Job.

**Source:** DSS101; Learning to Build; jobstobedone.org.

### Ignoring the timeline

Jumping to Forces without reconstructing the sequence loses causation. The *when* matters as much as the *what*. How long did the customer sit in Passive Looking before something triggered Active Looking? What was the tipping point from Active Looking to Deciding? The timeline is where the actionable insight lives.

**Source:** DSS101; Business of Software talk.

### Treating "bitchin'" as demand signal

Customer complaints are not buying signals. Complaints are a release valve. Demand requires a struggling moment that generates *energy for progress*, not just dissatisfaction with the status quo.

**Source:** DSS101; Circuit Breaker podcast.

## Interview quality checks

After a Switch Interview, test the output quality:

1. **Can you map all four Forces with specific quotes?** If you can't fill Push with a real quote from this interview, you didn't get deep enough on that force.
2. **Can you draw the timeline with dates?** If the timeline is vague ("sometime last year"), you didn't push for specificity.
3. **Do you have the First Thought moment?** "What was happening the day you decided to look?" — if you can't answer this with a specific scene, you missed the trigger.
4. **Do you know what they gave up?** Habit force. What were they comfortable with? What did switching cost them emotionally?
5. **Do you know what they were afraid of?** Anxiety force. What almost stopped them?
6. **Can you name the real competition?** Not the category competitor — the thing they would have hired instead.
7. **Would Moesta say you got below the pablum layer?** If your notes are full of "it was easy", "good price", "better features" — you're at pablum. Push deeper.

**Source:** DSS101, Chapters 8-9; jobstobedone.org.
