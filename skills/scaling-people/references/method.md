# Scaling People — Method

> The canonical description of Claire Hughes Johnson's method, structured for template-first use. Fidelity to Claire means naming the specific artifact — the doc, the checklist, the opener, the question — alongside each principle. She never gives a principle without a template; neither should the assistant.

## Definition

Claire's operating definition (compressed from Chapter 1 of *Scaling People*):

> "Scaling people is about scaling the most important resource a company has — its people. That requires operating principles you actually hold, founding documents that make the implicit explicit, and mechanisms — hiring, feedback, planning, review — that repeat reliably at 200, 2,000, and 7,000."
> — paraphrase of *Scaling People*, Chapter 1 (Stripe Press, 2023)

## The 4 Essential Operating Principles

The meta-frame that runs through everything else. Load these before diagnosing any specific management situation.

### 1. Build self-awareness to build mutual awareness

**The most important of the four.** Self-awareness has three components:
- **Underlying value system** — what you actually care about, tested against what you spend time on.
- **Innate preferences** — your work-style defaults, decision-making tendencies, how you communicate under stress, when you have energy.
- **Skills and capability gaps** — honest inventory of what you're excellent at, what you can do adequately, and what you can't do.

**Tools Claire recommends** (as starting points, not identity labels): Myers-Briggs, Enneagram, DISC, StrengthsFinder, Hogan.

**The energy-map exercise:** track good days and bad days on your calendar for a month. See which activities enhance or drain performance. Claire's own discovery: *"weeks when I had more than one work event that kept me from having dinner with my kids and getting them to bed were bad weeks."*

**The point of the assessments:** articulate your defaults into shared vocabulary so others can work with you. That translation is what the **personal operating manual** ("Working with X") is for — see below.

### 2. Say the thing you think you cannot say

Adapted from Fred Kofman's *"detoxify the left-hand column"* (in *Conscious Business*). In every meeting there is something people are thinking that no one is saying. Naming it early prevents a compounding tax of unsaid tension.

**The framing move:** separate the person from the "it" — the task, work, or issue.
- Instead of: *"You're being sloppy."*
- Say: *"I'm worried about the communication in this doc — from my seat, 10,000-to-20,000 feet away, here's what I'm noticing."*

**Claire's own line:** *"I'm not criticizing you, I'm criticizing the communication."*

**Claire took ~10 years to get comfortable doing this.** She treats it as a learnable skill, not a personality trait. Manager as it, not identity as it.

### 3. Distinguish between management and leadership

Two different jobs, both required. Most managers are asked to do both without noticing.

- **Management** = the knowable process from A to B. Stability, structure, execution, mechanisms that repeat. Marty Linsky's line (Claire endorses): *"Management is about creating stability."*
- **Leadership** = driving change. Vision, setting standards you don't know are achievable yet, pulling the organization forward. Linsky: *"Leadership is ultimately about driving change."*

**Diagnostic when stuck:**
- If the team can't execute the plan you have, that's a **management problem** (mechanism, clarity, accountability).
- If the team executes the plan but the plan is wrong for the moment, that's a **leadership problem** (vision, standard, direction).

Confusing them: managers who mistake themselves for leaders skip the execution work and produce chaotic vision decks. Leaders who mistake themselves for managers stop pulling the org forward and get consumed by the operating cadence.

### 4. Come back to the operating system

The company's operating system (mission, values, planning cadence, decision rights, review rhythms) is **not a one-time setup**. It's the thing you return to every quarter, every re-org, every crisis.

**The diagnostic move when something feels off:**
- *"Which part of the operating system broke?"*
- NOT: *"Who screwed up?"*

Individual failures are almost always OS failures in disguise: the review rhythm wasn't hitting, the decision rights were ambiguous, the goal wasn't specific, the interview loop let the wrong person in. Come back to the OS, fix the piece, and the individual failure stops recurring.

## Founding Documents ("Guiding Papers")

Chapter 2. The doc bundle every company needs by ~30 people, refreshed annually.

### The four components

1. **Mission** — a concise statement of why the company exists. Example: Stripe's *"Increase the GDP of the internet."*
   - **Warning:** don't force it in the first month. *"Listen for the crystallization of what you're trying to be"* — the honest mission emerges from work, not a whiteboard.
   - Test: could a new hire in month one state the mission back to you in a sentence?

2. **Long-term goals** — 3-to-5-year plan. Stripe called them "long-term goals" publicly (not "vision"), because the concrete framing forces specificity.
   - Include: concrete milestones, measurable outcomes, the specific bets.
   - Test: could you tell whether you hit or missed them?

3. **Values** — cultural markers, authentic (not aspirational-only).
   - Test: *would you fire someone for violating them?* If not, they're marketing copy, not values.
   - Values live in hiring loops (interview questions test for them), in review rubrics (behaviors that map to values), and in comp decisions (violating values costs you comp).

4. **Team charters** — team-level docs defining team mission, strategy, customers (internal + external), why the team exists, and what it will and won't do.
   - Kept short (1–3 pages).
   - Refreshed annually.
   - Owned by the team lead, reviewed by the org.

### Template shape for a team charter

```
Team: [name]
Mission: [one sentence — why this team exists]
Customers: [who this team serves — internal, external, or both]
Strategy (3–5 year): [the big bets and how they connect to the company mission]
Goals (this year / quarter): [FOCUS(S)-compliant — see below]
What we will do: [the scope]
What we will NOT do: [the anti-scope — as important as the scope]
Team norms: [meeting cadence, decision rights, comms defaults]
Owner: [name]
Last updated: [date]
```

**When routing a user here:** if they don't have a team charter, that's often the intervention. Most "unclear team" problems dissolve when the charter is written.

## The Operating System

Three interlocking subsystems (Chapter 2):

- **Strategy** — resource allocation decisions. Where you invest and what you say no to. Explicit trade-offs, not implicit ones.
- **Accountability** — clear responsibility assignment. Who decides, who executes, who is informed.
- **Communication** — candid, accessible information. Meeting notes transparent by default. Leadership email cadence. Source-of-truth docs.

**The failure mode:** any two without the third collapses. Great strategy + accountability with poor communication = paralysis. Great accountability + communication with no strategy = motion without progress. Great strategy + communication with no accountability = talk without shipping.

## Operating Cadence

Claire's recommended layers (approximate; adjust to org state):

| Layer | Cadence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Charters (founding docs)** | Annual refresh | 3–5 year vision framing; refreshed once a year with the leadership team |
| **OKRs / goal-setting** | Quarterly (Stripe shifted from 6-month to quarterly) | Framework matters less than *"clarity of the goals and the repetition in their communication"* |
| **Company metrics targets** | Annual | Strategic metrics that move over quarters/years (e.g., share of global payment volume) |
| **Business reviews** | Variable per team, driven by team state | Written memos first; narrative quality > slide aesthetics |
| **Performance reviews** | Semi-annual or annual | Multi-source (self + peers + manager); calibrated to reduce single-manager bias |
| **1:1s** | Weekly (typical) | Grove's territory; Claire builds on top with the personal operating manual + hypothesis-based coaching |

**Warning Claire gives:** *"You need planning structures earlier than you think."* But also: don't over-engineer. Add structure at the moment something breaks, not preemptively.

### FOCUS(S) — the goal-quality rubric

Claire's criteria for whether a goal is well-formed:

- **F**ocus — is it one of the most important things?
- **O**bjectively assessable — shared understanding of what success looks like.
- **C**hallenging but possible — credible and feasible.
- **U**ser-oriented — anchored in customer value.
- **S**tates, not activities — outcomes, not motion. *"We will have 1,000 paying customers in segment X"* not *"we will run 10 sales campaigns."*
- **S**ensitivity and specificity — clear success AND clear failure criteria.

Run it as a checklist on every draft goal before adopting it.

### Metrics categories to track separately

- **Input metrics** — leading indicators (activity that predicts outcomes).
- **Operational metrics** — current health of the system.
- **Strategic metrics** — numbers that move over quarters or years.

Don't mix them on one dashboard without labels. Different cadences, different owners, different interventions.

## The Personal Operating Manual ("Working with X")

Claire's most-cited artifact. Origin: she wrote *"Working with Claire"* at Google as a private doc for her direct reports, made it open, and it spread. At Stripe she brought it and the pattern went viral.

**Purpose:** *"You're actually completely cutting off all of that studying and reading between the lines and jumping right to work."*

### Template structure (from Fortune + Lenny podcast)

**Section 1 — Work preferences (operational)**
- Communication channels per message type. *("Ping me on Slack if it's [X]. Stick it in our 1:1 doc if it's [Y]. Email is fine for [Z].")*
- Response timeframes for docs, decisions, reviews.
- Contact-method norms (phone/personal-number thresholds).
- Preferred meeting formats (fewer/longer? shorter/more? asynchronous first?).

**Section 2 — Behavioral style**
- Decision-making approach — default to consensus? Fast call? Data-first?
- Behavior under stress — what to watch for; what helps; what makes it worse.
- Work patterns — morning vs. afternoon deep-work; when interruptions are cheap.
- Management style with new work vs. established work. Claire's own line: *"I am not a micromanager necessarily, I'm gonna give you space to do your work. But the first time that we're doing something, I will get really into the details."*

**Section 3 — Optional (for new hires, or as an extended version)**
- Important personal dates (birthdays, family milestones).
- Accomplishments the manager should know about.
- Personal / values context.

### How to use it

- **Managers write theirs first**, share with direct reports and cross-functional peers.
- **New hires write one in first 30 days.**
- **Review annually** — style changes with life stage and role.
- **Cross-functional partners should share theirs** with each other when they start working together.

**Common failure mode:** the manual gets written once and never referenced. It has to be pinned somewhere everyone can find it and cited when a work-style mismatch surfaces (*"remind me what your manual says about how you handle deadline surprises"*).

## Hiring (Chapter 3)

### Recruiting pipeline

- **Transparent job descriptions** — what will actually be evaluated, what year-one success looks like. Not marketing copy.
- **Employee referrals** as first-order signal.
- **Target the "forever user"** of the company brand and cluster the hiring brand where they cluster. (For Stripe: developers.)
- **Bar-raiser / Elevate programs** to maintain the bar as the company scales. The bar-raiser sits on the interview loop but is not the hiring manager — they gatekeep the standard, not the specific hire.

### Structured interviews with scorecards

Design the loop *before* the first candidate.

1. **Standardized interview rubric** — decide the dimensions you'll evaluate. Typically: role-specific skills, values alignment, "learning organism" attributes, work-style / collaboration signals.
2. **Assign dimensions to interviewers.** Each interviewer owns one or two, so the loop covers all dimensions without overlap.
3. **Scorecard per interviewer** — score against the rubric, with evidence. Not free-form vibes.
4. **Interview committee** submits scores; **hiring manager owns the final call.** Recruiters are critical but not solely responsible.
5. **Debrief with evidence**, not with impressions.

### Behavioral interview technique

- **Open with something off the résumé that isn't recent.** Reveals depth vs. rehearsed narrative.
- **Peel the onion** with follow-up "why" questions. Test introspection.
- **Ask about hardest assignments and their response.** Watch for ownership vs. blame-shifting. Bonus if they can name a subsequent situation where they applied the learning.
- **"What would you do differently?"** — test comfort with course-correction.

### Claire's signature questions

- **The self-awareness question:** *"If I interviewed Peter next, what would he say it's like working with you?"* Reveals balance, honesty, willingness to acknowledge growth areas.
- **The learning-orientation probe:** *"Tell me about a time you worked on something you knew you wouldn't enjoy."*
- **The failure probe:** *"A project that failed and what role you played in it."* Then: *"How do you recover from failure?"*
- **The ownership check:** *"What would you change in your current job?"* Victim vs. Player tell.

### The "learning organism" filter

*Claire treats this as the single most predictive attribute for scaling talent.*

**Definition:** *"Is the person a learning organism? Are they curious? Are they a learner?"* Different from "ambitious" — plenty of ambitious people hit a definition of success and stop learning.

**Traits to test in the loop:**
- Results orientation — track outcomes, not process.
- Curiosity — go deep, introspect.
- Resilience — respond under pressure, recover.
- Ownership — Fred Kofman's Victim vs. Player.

**Behavioral proof:** ask what they've changed their mind about recently, and why, and when.

### Executive hiring — the specific failure mode

- **Red flag:** an executive hired as a leader who **doesn't hire anyone good in their first 3–6 months**. Followership doesn't attach. The best signal for exec quality is who they attract.
- **Claire names her own weakness:** exec hiring at Stripe was her weaker area. Her diagnosis: *"I was trying to be liked"* and didn't push hard enough on diligence.
- **Assumption error:** *"If you got to be this successful in your career, you're gonna adapt."* You don't know that. Test it.
- **Ask executive candidates:** *"Tell me about your current team and why you chose the structure you have."*
  - **Red flag response:** *"Well, this is the team I got."*
  - **Green flag response:** specific, opinionated, has agency in describing the structure.
- **Test recruiting muscle:** some execs from scaled environments haven't actually recruited in years. If they can't articulate a recruiting playbook, teach them or don't hire them for a scaling role.

## The Reference-Check Drill

Do it yourself.

### Non-negotiable rule

> *"If you are delegating the references, you're making a mistake."*
> — Claire, First Round Review podcast

References are diligence, not box-checking. They're often the single highest-signal step in the loop and are almost always under-invested.

### The drill

1. **Ask candidate for permission before back-channel calls.** *"Would you be comfortable if I talked to them very confidentially?"* Usually back-load the back-channels — avoid rushing due to confirmation bias.
2. **Push for recent references.** *"Is there someone who left your company recently who could talk about you?"* Old references are usually curated; recent departures are honest.
3. **Prime the referee with role context.** Describe the current situation, year-one success indicators, the capabilities required. Then: *"Do you think they'll thrive in this?"* Don't assume the referee understands the role.
4. **The ranking question.** *"Are they top 50%, top 20%, top 10%?"* People reveal truth when asked for specifics. Follow-up: *"Does that mean they're top 5%?"* Pin it down.
5. **The coaching question.** *"What can I do as their manager to help them succeed?"* Immediately gets to performance, blind spots, development. Reveals what won't come up in the résumé.
6. **The followership question** (for exec references). *"When they moved, who followed them?"* Or: *"Would you work with them again?"* If yes: *"Would you follow them to a new company?"*

### Failure modes

- **Rushing** — confirmation bias amplified. Slow down.
- **Delegating to a recruiter** — the recruiter gets the box-check; you don't get the diligence.
- **Only calling references the candidate offers** — those are curated. Back-channel calls (with permission) are where the real signal is.

## Feedback and Performance (Chapter 5)

### Hypothesis-based coaching ("inquisitive coaching")

Three steps:

1. **Observe carefully** across situations. Different strengths and weaknesses emerge in different contexts.
2. **Identify persistent patterns.** One incident is a data point; a pattern is a signal.
3. **Share tactfully as a hypothesis**, framed as behavior not character.

### The opening move

- **Hypothesis, not verdict.** *"Here's what I'm noticing — does this match your experience?"*
- **Let them self-assess first.** Growth-mindset frame, not defense-mindset frame.
- **Neutral distance line:** *"From my seat, 10,000-to-20,000 feet away, I have a couple of things I'm worried about."*

### The "explorer, not lecturer" rule

> *"Your job is not to tell somebody what to do... your job is to explore what happened, why, and what they'll do differently."*
> — Claire, First Round Review

Ask questions. Guide to self-discovery. Celebrate the person arriving at the insight themselves.

Related: *"People do not learn by being told answers... you're going to lead them through learning with you and they are going to get to the answer and you are going to celebrate them doing that."*

### Separating the person from the "it"

Claire's operating principle #2 shows up here as a mechanic:

- *"I'm not criticizing you, I'm criticizing the communication."*
- *"I'm not worried about your commitment — I'm worried about how this project is being scoped."*

The "it" is the target, not the person.

### The four-phase low-performer escalation

When hypothesis-based coaching isn't enough:

| Phase | Duration | Move |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 0 — Pattern recognition** | Under 3 weeks | Notice the pattern. Don't act yet. Test whether it's context or trend. |
| **Phase 1 — Align on challenge** | 1–3 conversations over ~2 weeks | Name the pattern. Hear their perspective. Confirm shared understanding of the gap. |
| **Phase 2 — Agree on next steps** | 1–2 conversations over ~1 week | Concrete changes. Explicit timeline. Both sides sign on. |
| **Phase 3 — Create action plan** | 1–3 months | Formal improvement plan with milestones. Parallel-track backup planning (start considering successor, adjacent role, or exit). |

**The direct opening line for Phase 1 or later:**
> *"I am worried this is not working out for you."*

Clear, solution-oriented, doesn't hedge. Uses "I" (owned) + "worried" (concerned, not decided) + "you" (their side of the equation).

### Formal review process

- **Multi-source** — self + peers + manager.
- **Data-driven calibration** — cross-manager calibration to reduce bias, ideally with an HR partner in the room.
- **Explicit link to compensation** — with structural transparency (bands) so calibration means something and single-manager bias doesn't drive comp.

### Informal feedback culture

- **Frequency > formality.** Peer feedback matters more than manager feedback in most workflows.
- **Manager solicits feedback on self first**, publicly and repeatedly, then delivers up-the-stack. Modeling matters more than telling.
- **All feedback is valid, even if incorrect** — the fact that someone perceived it that way is data. Don't dismiss inaccurate feedback; investigate why the perception exists.

## Team Development (Chapter 4)

### Team structures

- **No such thing as an optimal org structure.** Every organizational decision has an externality — ask what you're optimizing and what price you pay.
- **Re-orgs every 3–6 months are normal in hypergrowth.** Frame as iteration and learning, not resistance.
- **Reversible where possible.** Ship the org chart as an evolving artifact, not a monument.

### Diagnosing team state — Tuckman

Forming → Storming → Norming → Performing. Different agenda for each stage. Claire's offsite toolkit is structured around this.

- **Forming** — high on introductions, low on debate. Establish operating norms.
- **Storming** — surface conflict productively. This is when "say the thing you think you cannot say" pays highest dividends.
- **Norming** — codify what's working. Team charter refresh.
- **Performing** — the team can run on its own. Manager shifts to coaching / removing obstacles.

### Skill/will matrix — delegating strategically

Two axes: **skill** (can they do it?) and **will** (do they want to?). Four quadrants:

|  | High skill | Low skill |
|---|---|---|
| **High will** | Delegate + get out of the way | Coach + provide scaffolding |
| **Low will** | Investigate the demotivation first | Consider role fit — or exit |

**Rule for what NOT to delegate:** high-consequence + irreversible tasks. The manager keeps or pairs on them.

### Offsites

Claire has a public toolkit ([Claire's Offsite Toolkit](https://docs.superhuman.com/@clairehughesjohnson/claires-offsite-toolkit)):

- **Planning Checklist** — ensures nothing is overlooked.
- **Set Objectives & Agenda** — define the goal before the schedule.
- **Facilitator Guide** — instructions for session leaders.
- **Icebreaker Ideas** — opening activities.
- **Offsite Welcome / Check-in / Session / Check-out templates** — day-of execution.

**Three legitimate offsite objectives:**
1. Transform work groups into cohesive teams.
2. Evaluate progress while planning future priorities.
3. Encourage long-term strategic thinking.

**Task vs. team focus balance** — every offsite has both. Setting metrics is task-focused; team-building activities are team-focused. Design the ratio deliberately.

**Warning:** assess the state of the team before booking an offsite. Offsites aren't universally appropriate. A Storming-stage team may need a different intervention.

## Decision-Making

Claire does NOT use RAPID or DACI as her primary vocabulary. Her framing:

### Be explicit about the decision type upfront

State one of:
- *"I'm ultimately making this decision — I want input."*
- *"Help me explore options — I haven't decided yet."*
- *"This is a group decision — I'll go with the consensus."*
- *"Group discussion only, no decision today."*

**The failure mode:** the group thinks they're the decision-maker, the leader has already decided, and the meeting produces performative debate + confused outcome. Stated upfront, the confusion evaporates.

### CEO / founder-owned decisions

Some cannot be delegated:
- Hiring senior leaders.
- Stopping projects.
- Retrenchment / layoff decisions.
- Values / mission changes.

### Compatible tools Claire endorses

- **SPADE** (Gokul Rajaram) — Setting, People, Alternatives, Decide, Explain. Good for structured decisions.
- **The Five Whys** — root cause analysis.
- **Left-hand column technique** (Kofman) — writing down what you think but don't say, then examining the gap.

## Managing Managers

- **Modeling > telling.** Managers set the terms for how their managers manage. Your defaults become their defaults within two levels.
- **Skip-levels.** Claire endorses the "crashing meetings" pattern — show up unannounced, listen, don't intervene. Preserves signal that would filter out by the time it reached your 1:1.
- **Build the manager's confidence in their own instinct.** *"80 percent of the way there. They're just not confident. My job is to build their confidence, then we brainstorm the last 20 percent."*

---

## Writing / Memo Culture

- **Written communication first.** Business reviews are written memos before they are meetings. Read the memo silently in the first 15 minutes of the meeting (Bezos style).
- **Meeting notes transparent by default.** The org compounds trust when the default is open.
- **Narrative quality > slide aesthetics.** If it can't be written in prose, it isn't thought through.
- **Team charters are living documents**, not one-time deliverables. Refresh annually.
- **"Say the thing you think you cannot say" is a written-culture principle too.** If you're avoiding writing something down, that's the signal to write it.

## Integration — the moves working together

*Scaling People* is a system, not a menu. The pieces reinforce:

- **The 4 principles** are the meta-frame — every specific move traces back to one of them.
- **Founding documents** operationalize principle #4 (the OS) at the company level.
- **The personal operating manual** operationalizes principle #1 (self-awareness) at the individual level.
- **Structured interviews + scorecards + the reference drill** operationalize the OS at the hiring layer.
- **Hypothesis-based coaching + the low-performer escalation** operationalize principle #2 (say the thing) at the feedback layer.
- **The operating cadence** (charters + OKRs + reviews) is the recurring return to the OS (principle #4).
- **The decision-type declaration** operationalizes principle #2 (making the implicit explicit) at the decision layer.

The system is what makes each move survive scale. A single Radical Candor conversation is a move; the operating system that produces many such conversations reliably is Claire's contribution.

## What this method is NOT

- **NOT a one-time setup.** The whole framing is about the operating system you *return to*. Any use of Claire's method that treats founding docs, personal operating manuals, or hiring loops as one-time deliverables misses the point.
- **NOT a substitute for judgment.** Templates route your judgment; they don't replace it.
- **NOT a personality assessment.** MBTI/DISC/Enneagram results are starting points for shared vocabulary, not identity.
- **NOT premature.** Claire warns explicitly: don't force a mission statement in the first month. Don't over-engineer the operating cadence before the org has broken enough times to need it.
- **NOT a solo invention.** Claire synthesizes Grove, Kofman, Kim Scott, Bezos, Linsky, Rajaram. Fidelity to Claire means naming those origins, not laundering them.
