# Scaling People — Worked Cases Claire Uses Publicly

> The recurring cases Claire Hughes Johnson returns to across her book, podcast circuit, and interviews. Unlike Kim Scott's canonical duo (Sheryl / Bob) which she uses relentlessly, Claire uses a *broader* set of shorter cases — she's more likely to pull a specific Stripe scaling moment or a cross-industry leader case (Dan Cohen, Dominique Crème) than to tell the same story three times. Reach for these when helping a user recognize their own situation on the operating-system lens.

## The through-line case

### 1. Stripe scaling from ~160 to 7,000+ (2014–2021)

**Source:** *Scaling People* (2023); every podcast appearance.

**The story:** Claire joined Stripe as COO in 2014 when the company had fewer than 200 employees. Over the next 7 years, the company scaled to over 7,000. The book is the operational memoir of that arc — what worked, what didn't, what she wishes she'd done differently.

**Sub-cases that come out of the arc:**
- **Founding documents adoption** — Stripe wrote the founding docs (mission "Increase the GDP of the internet", long-term goals, values, team charters) as the company scaled.
- **Shift from 6-month planning to quarterly OKRs.** Operational choice, not ideological — quarterly let them re-plan when the market moved.
- **The "Working with Claire" doc going viral inside Stripe.**
- **Multiple re-orgs every 3–6 months during hypergrowth.** Frame as iteration, not failure.
- **Bar-raiser hiring program to maintain the bar as the company scaled.**

**When to reach for this case:** anchor for "here's what a real scaling company actually did." Not to prescribe — every company differs — but to ground the templates in a real story.

## The personal operating manual cases

### 2. "Working with Claire" — the viral spread

**Source:** Fortune (Feb 2023); Lenny's Podcast (2023).

**The story:** Claire originally wrote "Working with Claire" at Google as a private doc for her closest reports. When she joined Stripe, she brought it with her. She made it open. People started asking *"Well, why don't we have more of these?"* Organic adoption took over — many people at Stripe wrote their own. The pattern became a company norm without a top-down mandate.

**The Claire line:** *"You're actually completely cutting off all of that studying and reading between the lines and jumping right to work."*

**The document sections** (from Fortune):
- Section 1: Work preferences (communication channels, response timeframes, contact norms)
- Section 2: Behavioral style (decisions, stress, work patterns, management style)
- Section 3 (optional): Personal dates, accomplishments

**Claire's own line about her own management style, from her manual:** *"I am not a micromanager necessarily, I'm gonna give you space to do your work. But the first time that we're doing something, I will get really into the details."*

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user write their first personal operating manual. Also: model of how a tool spreads bottom-up when it's genuinely useful, not top-down mandate.

## The hiring cases

### 3. The Collison brothers' hiring process — for Claire herself

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Patrick and John Collison spent months recruiting Claire. Their process was the model of exec-hiring done well:

- **Relentless research.** Patrick "relentlessly researched" and pursued exceptional talent. Asked Claire's references detailed questions, then: *"Who have you worked with better than this person?"* Maps talent networks systematically.
- **Genuine curiosity, not hard sell.** Patrick shared knowledge passionately (sent Claire a vintage copy of *Computer Lib*).
- **Multiple rounds + company-wide Q&A** — extensive diligence on both sides.
- **"Practiced working together"** — drove to office to write company goals together.
- **Work samples.** Sent her materials to assess her leadership style before hiring.
- **Portfolio assembly, not perfect fit.** Hired her as "Chief of Business Operations" (not COO) initially — to show respect to Billy Alvarado, the incumbent leader. Portfolio grew over years (added marketing, risk, workplace/real estate; gave up other functions).
- **Asked:** *"What do we need? What are your career aspirations? What might you bring we didn't know we needed?"*

**Patrick's specific line, showing he was listening:** *"I think people might think you're taking his job"* — protecting team dynamics as part of the diligence.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user run exec-hiring diligence. This is Claire's model of exec hiring done well — she uses it as the counter-example to the "trying to be liked" failure mode.

### 4. Claire's own executive-hiring blind spots at Stripe

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Claire names her own weaker area: executive hiring at Stripe. Her diagnosis:

- She was *"trying to be liked"* — didn't push hard enough on diligence.
- Made the assumption: *"If you got to be this successful in your career, you're gonna adapt."* Reality: adaptation is not guaranteed.
- Didn't probe deeply enough on how they'd work with others.
- Missed the followership signal — some execs from scaled environments never actually recruited themselves.

**The specific red flag she now tests for:** an executive hired as a leader who *doesn't hire anyone good in their first 3–6 months*. That's the pattern that predicts flameout.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user push through their own "trying to be liked" moment before making a senior hire. Also: model of Claire's brief-then-move-on failure register.

### 5. The Reid Hoffman "wouldn't hire you to manage a McDonald's" story

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Reid Hoffman hired a head of ops who, early on, told Reid: *"I wouldn't hire you to manage a McDonald's."* Reid's response wasn't defensive — his superpower is creating an active feedback culture where people aren't afraid to say that.

**The lesson Claire draws:** hold personal values (welcoming hard feedback) constant across company sizes. What made you receptive to feedback at 20 people has to survive scale to 2,000.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a leader who has started deflecting hard feedback as they've scaled.

## The cross-industry cases

Claire deliberately uses non-tech leaders to show that scaling-people principles are universal.

### 6. Dominique Crème — the Michelin-star chef

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Dominique Crème: *"I'm strict, but I'm also kind."* Never raises her voice but never holds back on expectations. High standards without abuse.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user who's oscillating between "nice but unclear" and "clear but abrasive." Crème is Claire's exemplar of the both-at-once move.

### 7. Dan Cohen — Met Museum President

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Dan Cohen, President of the Metropolitan Museum of Art, dedicates Saturday mornings to his own creative work (separate from his CEO role). Paraphrased: *"I would be terrible as a CEO if I didn't get to do my own work."*

**The lesson Claire draws:** feed your other needs so you can do the day job better. Leaders who wholly identify with the role burn out or lose the input that made them valuable in the first place.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a leader who has abandoned every creative/personal input in service of the role.

### 8. Zannya Mittenbottles — The Economist editor

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Zannya Mittenbottles practices skip-levels by "crashing meetings" — showing up unannounced, listening, influencing through presence.

**The lesson Claire draws:** skip-levels don't have to be scheduled formal 1:1s. Sometimes the highest-signal skip-level is showing up in the middle of the work.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user redesign their skip-level practice.

### 9. Sam Hagood — UCSF Chancellor

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The story:** Sam Hagood, Chancellor of UCSF, said something like: *"More and more all I can do is influence."*

**The lesson Claire draws:** senior leadership is influence, not power. The mistake is thinking you get more control as you go up; you get less. What grows is your reach.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a senior leader who's frustrated that they can't force outcomes anymore.

## Claire's personal origin cases

### 10. Her father's response to being robbed in Paris

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023); Tim Ferriss #724 (2024).

**The story:** Claire's family was robbed in Paris. Her father's response was: *"What's our action plan?"* Not *"why me?"*

**The lesson Claire draws:** player-orientation modeled early. This is where her comfort with the victim-vs-player frame comes from — she watched it modeled in a moment of real stress.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize a victim pattern in themselves. The father-in-Paris story is the reference point Claire uses for what player-orientation looks like in a moment where "why me?" would be understandable.

### 11. Her parents modeling versus telling

**Source:** First Round Review podcast (2023).

**The line:** *"Parents, it's like leaders. It's what they do more than what they say."*

**When to reach for this case:** helping a manager who is telling their team one thing while doing another. The team is watching the actions, not the words.

## Composite cases the assistant can construct

When a user brings a scenario that maps to Claire's canonical cases, use hers by analogy. When the scenario is different enough that Claire's canonical cases don't fit, construct a case in Claire's register:

- **Template-first** — start with the artifact (personal operating manual, team charter, structured-interview scorecard) that fits the situation.
- **Named failure mode** — name the specific pattern to watch for, briefly.
- **Specific opener or question** — give the verbatim opener/question Claire would use.
- **The player-move** — end with the ownership move, not the victim frame.

**Do not invent quotes from Claire.** If you're using a constructed case, don't attribute it to her. Say *"in Claire's method, this would be…"* rather than putting words in her mouth.

## When NOT to reach for these cases

- **When the user is pre-PMF with 5 people.** Most of the Stripe scaling arc doesn't apply yet. Point them at pre-PMF material. The personal operating manual and "say the thing" principles still work; the rest can wait.
- **When the user's situation is legally sensitive** (harassment, discrimination). Don't collapse it into a *Scaling People* case — direct the user to HR / legal counsel first. The framework informs; it doesn't replace them.
- **When the user is really asking a strategy question.** Don't tell them about Stripe's org design when the actual question is "should we even be in this market?"
- **When the user is fishing for permission to unload on a report.** Redirect using the "separate the person from the it" move. The construction-site / hypothesis-based coaching path is the right route.
