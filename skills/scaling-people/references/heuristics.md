# Scaling People — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Claire's operational rules — that separate applying *Scaling People* well from template-worship. Claire is opinionated: certain moves she treats as non-negotiable, certain failure modes she names by category. This file consolidates those.

## Do's

### Do the 4-principles diagnostic when anything feels stuck

Before jumping to a template, run the meta-check:

- *Did I do the self-awareness work? Do they know how I actually work?* (Principle #1)
- *Is there something I'm not saying? What's the thing I think I cannot say?* (Principle #2)
- *Am I confusing management and leadership? Which one is called for here?* (Principle #3)
- *Which piece of the operating system broke — and how do we return to it?* (Principle #4)

Most "stuck" management situations dissolve when one of these surfaces the actual gap.

### Do write your personal operating manual BEFORE you need it

- Ship a first version in month one of a new role.
- Share with direct reports, cross-functional peers, and skip-levels.
- Update annually.
- Pin it somewhere everyone can find it.
- **Test:** if a new team member had it on day one, would they need to spend less time "reading between the lines"?

### Do the references yourself

**Non-negotiable per Claire:** *"If you are delegating the references, you're making a mistake."*

- Ask the ranking question. *"Are they top 50%, top 20%, top 10%?"*
- Ask the coaching question. *"What can I do as their manager to help them succeed?"*
- Push for recent references — the ones who worked with them last year, not five years ago.
- Prime the referee with role context — don't assume they know what success looks like in *your* situation.
- Get candidate permission for back-channel calls.
- Back-load them — resist confirmation bias.

### Do state the decision type upfront

Before any meeting where a decision might happen, state:
- *"I'm making this — I want input"*, OR
- *"We're deciding together"*, OR
- *"We're exploring — no decision today."*

The confusion cost of leaving this implicit is enormous.

### Do write founding documents in order: mission → long-term goals → values → team charters

Refresh annually. Keep team charters short (1–3 pages). Refresh with the team, not to them.

### Do reward learning-organism traits over pedigree

Learning organisms compound across roles; pedigree does not. The behavioral test: what have they changed their mind about recently, and why, and when?

### Do delegate strategically using the skill/will matrix

- High skill + high will → delegate and get out of the way.
- High skill + low will → investigate the demotivation.
- Low skill + high will → coach + scaffold.
- Low skill + low will → role fit conversation.
- **Never delegate:** high-consequence + irreversible tasks.

### Do assume re-orgs every 3–6 months during hypergrowth

*"There's no such thing as an optimal org structure."* Frame changes as iteration and learning, not failure. Announce them with clarity about what's being optimized and what price is being paid.

### Do use hypothesis-based coaching

- Observe carefully across multiple situations.
- Wait for the pattern before intervening.
- Open with a hypothesis, not a verdict. *"Here's what I'm noticing — does this match your experience?"*
- Let the person self-assess first.

### Do use the neutral-distance line for hard feedback

*"From my seat, 10,000-to-20,000 feet away, I have a couple of things I'm worried about."*

Deliberate register shift. Reduces defensiveness without softening the message.

### Do separate the person from the "it"

*"I'm not criticizing you, I'm criticizing the communication."*

The "it" is the target. The person is the collaborator on fixing it.

### Do "come back to the operating system"

When individual failures repeat, the OS is broken. Fix the mechanism, not just the person. Interview loop is letting the wrong people in? Fix the loop. Review process is producing surprises? Fix the feedback rhythm. Decisions are getting relitigated? Fix the decision-type declaration.

### Do write everything down

Business reviews as written memos > slides. Meeting notes transparent by default. Team charters as living docs. If you're avoiding writing something, that's the signal to write it.

### Do run the FOCUS(S) rubric on every goal

Before adopting a goal, check: Focus, Objectively assessable, Challenging but possible, User-oriented, States not activities, Sensitivity and specificity. Kills fake goals early.

### Do build the "worried" muscle

Claire's opening for hard conversations: *"I am worried this is not working out for you."* Practice it. It's clearer than "I'm concerned" and more owned than "the team is concerned."

## Don'ts

### Don't force a mission statement in the first 30 days

*"Listen for the crystallization of what you're trying to be."* The honest mission emerges from work. A whiteboard mission written before you've built anything is aspirational marketing.

### Don't hire senior executives on pedigree

Ask them to describe their current team and why they chose that structure. Red flag: *"This is the team I got."* The answer diagnoses whether they develop people or accept the status quo.

### Don't assume "if they were successful there, they'll adapt here"

Claire names this as her own repeated executive-hiring error. Adaptation is not guaranteed. Test it: put them in real work-like sessions during the loop, not just conversations.

### Don't skip the followership test

If an executive hired as a leader doesn't hire (or attract) strong people in their first 3–6 months, that's the red flag. Every executive-flameout Claire has seen carried this signal.

### Don't try to be liked

Claire's own diagnosis of her weaker executive-hiring track record: *"I was trying to be liked and not pushing hard enough on diligence."* Being liked is not the goal; the goal is a great decision.

### Don't apply the Golden Rule to management

*"The Golden Rule is not a great rule for management."* Manage each person by what gets *their* best, not what would get yours. Different people need different management. Especially in diverse teams.

### Don't confuse "being nice" with being a leader

Kindness is not the same as clarity, and it's not the same as high standards. Dominique Crème (Michelin-star chef, Claire's recurring case): *"I'm strict, but I'm also kind."* Both, not one.

### Don't deliver hard feedback so bluntly it lands as attack

Directness matters, but *"feedback so direct that it feels like an attack"* makes people stop listening. Use the neutral-distance line and the hypothesis-first opener.

### Don't let unspoken tension compound

*"Say the thing you think you cannot say"* is a manager's tax-payment tool. Compounding tax will eat the team. The moment the thought crosses your mind that you should say something, that's the signal.

### Don't over-engineer the operating cadence early

Add structure at the moment something breaks, not preemptively. *"You need planning structures earlier than you think"* is Claire's line — but she equally warns against premature infrastructure. The trigger is *"we broke this way once,"* not *"we might break this way later."*

### Don't treat MBTI / DISC / Enneagram results as identity

Assessments are starting points for self-awareness, not permanent labels. Use them to explain your defaults so others can work with you. *"I'm an INTJ so I can't do warmth"* is hiding, not self-awareness.

### Don't skip the reference-check drill because you're excited

Confirmation bias is loudest right before a great candidate offer. Back-load the references, ask the ranking + coaching questions, and get the recent references.

### Don't run an offsite for a team that doesn't need one

Assess team state (Tuckman) first. A Storming-stage team may need a different intervention. Offsites aren't universally appropriate.

### Don't run career conversations without follow-through

If you have a growth conversation and don't deliver on the specific commitments, you've taught the person the conversation was theater.

### Don't confuse a strategy problem with a management problem

If the team can't execute the plan, it's a management problem (mechanism). If the team executes the plan but the plan is wrong, it's a strategy or leadership problem (direction). Different intervention.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### Template-worship

Filling out the personal operating manual, writing the team charter, doing the interview scorecard — without doing the underlying work of self-awareness, honest observation, or actual diligence — produces theatre. Claire's templates are scaffolding for judgment, not replacements for it.

### The personal operating manual as one-time deliverable

Wrote it, shared it, done. No. It needs to be pinned, cited when work-style mismatches surface, and updated annually (styles shift with life stage and role).

### The team charter that nobody reads

Written by the team lead, filed in a wiki, never referenced. The charter has to be the artifact you return to when scope drift or role confusion surfaces. If it's not, the charter isn't doing its job.

### The mission statement that was rushed

Written in month one, revised in month six, revised again in year one. Better: don't write it until the work has revealed the shape. *"Listen for the crystallization."*

### The reference call that becomes a validation call

The candidate is amazing, you're excited, you call the reference to confirm — and only ask the questions that confirm. The reference-check drill (ranking, coaching, primed referee) exists to counteract this.

### The "learning organism" filter used lazily

Every candidate says they're curious. Every résumé claims growth. The test is behavioral evidence — what did they change their mind about, when, and why. Without the behavioral evidence, "learning organism" becomes vibes.

### Over-relying on offsites

Offsites are expensive (time + money + travel). They should transform a work group into a cohesive team OR advance strategic thinking. If the goal is "team building," ask what specifically has to change and whether an offsite is the right dose.

### The victim who looks like a "player" in the interview

Interviewees have rehearsed ownership stories. Test with real work: workshops, joint sessions, sample docs. Claire's own hiring at Stripe (Collison brothers) used this — they drove to the office to write company goals together.

### The self-awareness gap in senior hires

Claire names this as one of the two hardest gaps to close in adults: **people who think they're the best in the world.** Interviews reward confidence; the gap surfaces post-hire. The reference-check drill is the primary defense.

### Confusing self-awareness with public vulnerability

Self-awareness is internal work translated into shared vocabulary (the personal operating manual). It is NOT "be vulnerable in every meeting." A leader who confuses the two produces performative confessionals that don't help the team.

### The all-hands as monologue

Once the company is >100, all-hands as one-way broadcast stops working. Claire's Stripe pattern: mix Q&A, panels, cross-functional voices. Broadcast-only signals to the org that leadership doesn't need input.

### The 1:1 as status update

Grove's territory: the 1:1 is the single highest-leverage manager time. Claire's extension: it's where hypothesis-based coaching lives, where the personal operating manual is referenced when needed, and where the career conversation surfaces. Reducing it to status is malpractice.

### Executive comp negotiation as signal quality

An executive who negotiates aggressively on comp isn't necessarily wrong — but the pattern of *what* they negotiate on tells you what they value. Comp-only negotiation with no interest in team, scope, or mandate is a signal about long-term fit.

## Pro tips

### The "if we were doing this again, what would we do differently?" retro

For every project of consequence, run the retro. Claire's principle #4 (come back to the OS) plus principle #2 (say the thing) meet here. Written retros with owner-assigned action items become durable OS improvements.

### The 15-minute pre-1:1 prep

Before a 1:1, spend 15 minutes writing (not thinking):
1. What did they do this week that I want to reinforce?
2. What's the pattern I'm noticing that I want to share as a hypothesis?
3. What career signal did I pick up that I should return to?
4. What am I asking them for feedback on this week?

Turns a status update into a real 1:1.

### The "what's the one thing?" question

Instead of asking "any feedback?", ask *"What's the one thing I could do differently that would make working with me easier?"* Kim Scott's move; Claire endorses in the podcast interviews. Forces specificity.

### The offsite check-out exercise

At the end of every offsite: each person says one thing they're taking away and one thing they're worried about. Compresses to 30 minutes and gives you the honest reception of the offsite in real time.

### The "what price are we paying?" question for org design

Every org design decision has an externality. Before adopting a structure, ask: *"What are we optimizing? What price are we paying?"* Names the trade-off; makes the decision reversible if the price gets too high.

### The reference call that becomes a coaching call

After you've done the ranking + coaching questions, the great referees often start offering unsolicited context (*"one thing to watch is…"*). That's the signal. Follow it.

### The energy map as a manager tool

Ask your report to do the same exercise you do — track good days and bad days for a month. Look at the patterns together in a 1:1. Reveals structural changes you can make to their week that will produce more good days.

### The team charter as a scope-drift diagnostic

When people start doing work outside the team's charter — either the charter needs updating or the work is misplaced. The charter surfaces the question early.

## The specific failure modes Claire names by category

Consolidated here so they're easy to pattern-match:

### The "trying to be liked" executive hirer

Under-diligences on senior hires because pushing hard feels adversarial. Claire's own diagnosis of her weaker exec-hiring track record.

### The mission-statement-rusher

Writes the mission in month one, then rewrites it three times before year two. Better: listen for the crystallization.

### The template-worshipper

Fills out the personal operating manual, the team charter, the interview scorecard — but doesn't do the underlying observation, self-awareness, or diligence work.

### The Ruinous-Empathy manager (Kim Scott's term, Claire endorses)

Withholds hard feedback to spare feelings. Compounds cost. Claire's version: doesn't run the "I am worried this is not working out for you" conversation until Phase 3 of the low-performer escalation, by which point the surprise is cruel.

### The "we don't have time for feedback" org

Kim Scott named this pattern; Claire mirrors it. The compounding cost of ungiven feedback dwarfs the time cost. 1:1s and reviews are the highest-leverage manager time.

### The victim (Kofman, Claire endorses)

Attributes outcomes to external factors. One of the two hardest gaps to close in adults. Detected in the interview loop by probing failure ownership.

### The self-awareness gap (Claire's term)

Believes they're the best in the world. The other hard-to-close gap. Detected in the interview loop by asking *"if I interviewed Peter next, what would he say it's like working with you?"* — and watching for evasion.

### The premature-infrastructure builder

Builds an operating cadence before the team has broken enough times to need it. Signal: multiple planning frameworks, no shipped work.

### The exec who doesn't build followership

Hired as a leader, three months in, no strong hires, no one lobbying to work with them. Highest-signal exec-flameout indicator Claire tracks.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| "You're being sloppy" | "I'm worried about the communication in this doc" | Separates person from the "it" |
| "Any feedback?" | "What's the one thing I could do differently to make working with me easier?" | Forces specificity |
| "We're a values-driven culture" | "These are the behaviors we'd fire someone for" | Values are only real if they're enforced |
| "I need you to figure this out" | "Here's what I'm noticing — does this match your experience?" | Hypothesis-based coaching opens the door; edict closes it |
| "That's a bad idea" | "Help me understand what problem you're solving" | Curiosity opens; verdict closes |
| Aspirational mission (rushed) | "Listen for the crystallization" | Honest mission emerges from work |
| Optimal org structure | "What are we optimizing? What price are we paying?" | There's no optimal — only trade-offs |
| Golden Rule ("treat them how you'd want to be treated") | Study each person; manage differently | Diverse teams break the Golden Rule |
| "Just be honest" | Neutral distance + hypothesis-first opener | Direct without attack |
| "High potential" | "Learning organism" | Trajectory + curiosity, not just ambition |

## The weaponization guard (adapted for this skill)

Claire's method is less susceptible to weaponization than Kim Scott's Radical Candor — but a specific misuse deserves calling out:

**Weaponized use:** *"Claire Hughes Johnson says say the thing you think you cannot say"* deployed as permission to unload on someone.

**What Claire actually means:** name unspoken tension in a shared work situation, framed as behavior not character, with the "it" separated from the person, using the neutral-distance line.

If a user is fishing for permission to unload:
- Redirect to the "separate the person from the it" move.
- Test the Care Personally axis (borrow from [[radical-candor]]) — the "say the thing" move works because the manager has invested in the relationship. Without that investment, it lands as attack.
- Do NOT help draft an unloading conversation. Draft a hypothesis-based coaching opener instead.
