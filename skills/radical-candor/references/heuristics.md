# Radical Candor — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Kim's operational rules — that separate applying Radical Candor well from doing the version she spends most of her airtime warning against. The Care Personally axis is load-bearing; the weaponization guard runs through this whole file.

## Do's

### Diagnose the specific piece of feedback, not the person

The 2×2 measures *specific moves*, not personality. Same person can be in all four quadrants in a single day. Before applying the framework, ask *"which quadrant was that piece of feedback in?"* — never *"what quadrant is this person?"*

**Why it matters:** the moment someone gets labeled ("she's just a Ruinous Empathy manager", "he's an Obnoxious Aggression type"), the framework stops being a lever for change and becomes a diagnostic prison. Also: this is how weaponization starts — someone treats their own bluntness as an identity ("I'm just a Radical Candor person") and stops examining specific moves.

### Care Personally BEFORE you Challenge Directly

The Care axis must be *established* — not just *intended* — before hard feedback lands as Radical Candor. In a new relationship, jumping straight to hard feedback registers as Obnoxious Aggression even if you care personally, because the recipient has no evidence of the Care yet.

**How to apply:** before delivering hard feedback, ask yourself:
- *"Does this person know I want them to succeed?"*
- *"What have I done in the last 30 days to demonstrate that?"*

If nothing — build the relationship first. Feedback deposited into an empty relationship account bounces.

### Solicit feedback on yourself first

Kim's most operational move for managers. Ask for feedback on yourself, publicly and repeatedly, *before* giving it. The script:

> "What could I do or stop doing that would make it easier to work with me?"

Rules:
- Do not accept silence. Ask again. Wait.
- When feedback comes, resist the urge to defend. Thank the person.
- Change something visibly so they see the feedback was heard.
- Repeat. If you only do it once, it's theater.

**Why it works:** manager-report power dynamics make it much harder for the report to initiate. Kim's rule: the person with more power has to go first, and has to keep going.

### Praise as specifically as you criticize

Common mistake: managers give vague praise ("great job!") and specific criticism ("your third slide had four data errors"). This asymmetry teaches direct reports that praise is noise and criticism is signal.

**Rule:** if you can be specific about what went wrong, be equally specific about what went right. Both praise and criticism should be so specific the person can repeat (or avoid) the exact behavior.

**Ratio:** Kim's bias is at least 3× as much specific impromptu praise as formal praise, and both more frequent than criticism.

### Right after a mistake, criticize privately. Right after a win, praise publicly.

The default from HHIPP. Not politeness — pedagogy. Public criticism triggers defensiveness that blocks learning. Public praise creates modeling for the rest of the team.

### Kind and clear is kind. Unkind and unclear is unkind.

Kim's rhythmic reframe to attack the Ruinous Empathy default. Being vague to spare feelings isn't kind — it's the opposite. Managers who withhold hard feedback to "protect" a direct report deprive them of the information they need to grow, and then fire them for the very behavior they were never warned about.

**Use this line** when a user justifies not giving hard feedback with "I don't want to hurt them."

### Run Career Conversations in your first 60-90 days, then every 12-18 months

Career Conversations (Russ Laraway's three: Life Story → Dreams → Career Action Plan) are the mechanism that lets you know whether someone is a Rock Star or Superstar this year. Without them, you're guessing. Dreams shift with life stage — repeat the ritual.

Attribute the framework to Russ Laraway when using it. Kim always does.

### Reward Rock Stars and Superstars differently

- **Rock stars:** recognition, scope-deepening, expert-track paths, stability. Do NOT keep pushing promotion on someone who's happy where they are — you'll drive them out.
- **Superstars:** new problems, promotion, scope-growth. Do NOT let them stagnate — they'll leave.

Treating "wants promotion" as the marker of value systematically undervalues rock stars. Kim considers this one of her biggest historical mistakes.

### Don't skip Persuade in the GSD wheel

The most common execution failure: a decision gets made, and the manager expects everyone to just do it. Without the Persuade step, the losing side of the debate executes reluctantly. The Persuade step is 10 minutes of *"here's why this is the call, here's what I heard from your position, here's why we're going this way"* — and it's the difference between committed and reluctant execution.

### Don't let loud voices win Debate by default

Build in structured Listen mechanisms (async writing, small groups, direct 1:1 solicit) *before* the loud debate starts. If the loudest person always wins, you're getting the loudest ideas, not the best ideas.

### Diagnose Bias vs. Prejudice vs. Bullying before responding

Using an "It statement" on unconscious bias will feel like an attack (they didn't mean it, you're bringing law/policy — mismatch). Using an "I statement" on a bully cedes the power dynamic they're exploiting. **Diagnosis before response.**

- Bias → I statement.
- Prejudice → It statement (decency / policy / law).
- Bullying → You statement.

## Don'ts

### Don't use "Radical Candor" as a preamble to hard feedback

**Red flag pattern:** *"I'm going to be radically candid with you now..."*

If someone announces Radical Candor before saying the hard thing, they are almost always about to be in Obnoxious Aggression. Radical Candor is diagnosed *after* the feedback by looking at whether the recipient felt cared for AND challenged. Announcing it as permission is a tell that the Care axis hasn't been built.

### Don't use "just being honest" as a defense

**Red flag pattern:** *"I'm just being honest / I'm being radically candid, they can't handle it."*

Radical Candor by definition includes the Care axis. If the recipient didn't feel cared for, the feedback was Obnoxious Aggression regardless of how the giver felt about it. The recipient's experience is the diagnostic, not the giver's intent.

### Don't tolerate "brilliant jerks"

Kim's post-Netflix line. A high performer who's an asshole to teammates isn't a net-positive — they poison the culture, drive out other high performers, and their output doesn't justify the cost.

Kim goes further than most: fire the brilliant jerk, even if it hurts short-term output, because keeping them signals to everyone else that behavior doesn't matter. A brilliant jerk retained is a public statement that Care Personally is optional.

### Don't confuse "high potential" with "high performer"

Rock stars are often the highest performers on the team. Treating "wants promotion" as the value marker systematically drives them out. See Do's above — reward differently, don't rank.

### Don't rely on the annual performance review as your primary feedback mechanism

Kim is emphatic: annual reviews are the wrong medium for anything except summary/documentation. If a direct report hears something at their annual review that they didn't hear in the moment, the manager failed. Feedback belongs in the moment, not in a spreadsheet quarterly.

### Don't pretend Radical Candor is the same as Radical Transparency

Ray Dalio's Bridgewater model (radical transparency, recorded meetings, public rating apps) is not Radical Candor. It tends to drop the Care Personally axis and curdle into Obnoxious Aggression at scale. Kim has publicly distinguished the two — don't blend them.

### Don't stop soliciting feedback because "no one has any"

Silence from your team is not the absence of feedback. It's evidence that you haven't made it safe to give feedback yet, or that you've responded badly the last time it came. Do not accept "everything's fine" as an answer. Ask again. Wait.

### Don't cascade criticism sideways ("Manager X asked me to tell you...")

Manipulative Insincerity failure mode at the org level. If Manager X has feedback for a report, Manager X gives it. Passing it through a third party removes the Care axis and encodes distrust.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### Treating the 2×2 as a personality test

The single most common misapplication. Managers read the book, do the "which quadrant am I in?" workshop, and label themselves and others. The 2×2 does not diagnose people. It diagnoses specific pieces of feedback. Same person, same day, all four quadrants possible.

**Test:** if the user is applying the framework to a person ("she's a Ruinous Empathy manager"), redirect them to a specific piece of feedback ("what's a specific piece of feedback she gave last week and which quadrant was THAT in?").

### Care Personally as "be nice"

Kim's "give a damn axis" is not about niceness. It's about actually caring what happens to the person. A manager can be extremely nice AND deeply not-caring (Manipulative Insincerity often looks like this — the fake-friendly manager who won't fight for their reports). Test: *"Would I fight for this person's career if it cost me something?"*

### Building the Care account and then never withdrawing

Some managers build strong relationships with their reports and then never give hard feedback because they don't want to spend the account. This is Ruinous Empathy with better preparation. The Care account exists *to be spent* on hard feedback — that's the point.

### Delivering Radical Candor to someone in crisis

Kim's implicit rule: HHIPP includes "immediate" but that doesn't override basic humanity. If the person just lost their parent, that's not the moment for the pointed feedback about the sales deck. The Immediate in HHIPP means "close to the event that generated the feedback," not "regardless of what else is happening."

### Confusing the Bob story with an excuse to fire quickly

Some readers take "don't be Ruinous Empathy" as "fire fast." Kim's actual point: give the hard feedback *early and often* so that firing is either unnecessary (the report improves) or so well-signaled that there's no surprise. The Bob failure was NOT firing too slowly. It was not giving feedback for 10 months.

### Career Conversations without follow-through

Running the three conversations and then not delivering on the 2-3 commitments you made is worse than not running them at all. It teaches your report that the strategic conversation is theater.

### Using the framework in cultures where the calibration differs

The axes (Care Personally, Challenge Directly) are universal. The calibration of what counts as "direct" is not — a Dutch team's Radical Candor might read as Obnoxious Aggression in a Japanese team, and a Japanese team's Radical Candor might read as Ruinous Empathy in a Dutch team. Kim addresses this in the podcast: adjust the calibration, don't drop the axes.

### The "Radical Candor" label used sideways to describe a company's culture

**Red flag:** "we're a Radical Candor culture" without demonstrated Care Personally practices (Career Conversations, 1:1s, feedback ratios, no-promotion-for-bullies policy). This is usually Obnoxious Aggression rebranded — a culture that celebrates bluntness without investing in the relational foundation.

## Pro tips

### The "would you tell them if there were no consequences?" test

For any hard feedback you're avoiding, ask: *"If there were no professional consequences, would I tell my closest friend this same thing?"* If yes — you're in Ruinous Empathy. Give the feedback.

### The 15-minute pre-feedback prep

Before a hard feedback conversation, spend 15 minutes writing (not saying) three things:
1. **The specific behavior** (not the character trait) you want to address.
2. **The impact** — what happened as a result, business or team.
3. **The Care evidence** — what have you done in the last 30 days that demonstrates you want this person to succeed? If you can't name it, delay the feedback and build the relationship first.

### Ask "what's the ONE thing" instead of "any feedback?"

The "any feedback?" question invites "no" because the mind can't process an open-ended request. *"What's the one thing I could do differently that would make working with me easier?"* forces a specific answer.

### Feedback in the moment beats feedback in the review, every time

If you catch yourself saving up feedback for a scheduled review, you've already failed HHIPP's Immediate. Give it now, in the meeting where it happened, in the hallway after. Save the review for summarizing what was already discussed.

### After hard feedback, ask "did I say that well?"

The recipient's answer diagnoses your quadrant. *"Did the way I said that land as caring, or did it feel harsh?"* If they say harsh, you were in Obnoxious Aggression regardless of intent. Adjust.

## Anti-patterns (the "bad management" Kim explicitly names)

### The Ruinous Empathy manager

**What it looks like:** vague performance reviews, "everything's fine," conflict-avoidance, hard conversations always postponed, people fired for behaviors they were never warned about.
**Why it fails:** kind at the moment, cruel over time. Deprives the report of the information they need to grow.
**How to redirect:** *Kind and clear is kind. Unkind and unclear is unkind.* Name the specific feedback that's being withheld and set a 48-hour delivery deadline.

### The Obnoxious Aggression manager

**What it looks like:** blunt to the point of dismissive; treats "no filter" as a virtue; often gets short-term compliance and long-term attrition; defends style as "just being honest."
**Why it fails:** compliance without commitment; the best people leave; institutional knowledge bleeds out.
**How to redirect:** Care Personally axis check. When was the last time you demonstrated to this person that you want them to succeed? If you can't answer, that's the work — not the delivery of more feedback.

### The Manipulative Insincerity manager

**What it looks like:** publicly agreeable, privately critical; runs feedback through other people; smiles in meetings, kills projects behind the scenes.
**Why it fails:** corrosive to trust across the org. Direct reports learn they can't tell what's real.
**How to redirect:** everything either party wants to say about performance has to be said directly to the person, in the moment. If it can't be said directly, it can't be said at all.

### The "we don't have time for feedback" org

**What it looks like:** *"We're too busy shipping to do 1:1s."* / *"We'll figure out career development after this quarter."*
**Why it fails:** the compounding cost of ungiven feedback dwarfs the time cost of giving it. Kim has never met a "we don't have time for feedback" org that wasn't burning talent silently.
**How to redirect:** 1:1s and Career Conversations are the highest-leverage manager time. If a manager can't defend that ratio, they're not managing.

### The "we're Radical Candor" as culture-marketing

**What it looks like:** company page has "Radical Candor" as a value; leaders quote the book; hard feedback is celebrated; Care Personally practices (Career Conversations, 1:1 rhythm, no-promotion-for-bullies) are absent.
**Why it fails:** Care Personally can't be a slogan. It has to be practiced. Without practice, the culture is Obnoxious Aggression cosplaying as Radical Candor.
**How to redirect:** audit the Care Personally practices. If they don't exist, name the gap — the culture is not Radical Candor yet, regardless of the value on the wall.

### Promoting a bully

Kim's non-negotiable. *"Do not promote bullies. It's that simple."* When a leader promotes someone known to bully, the signal to the whole org is: results matter more than behavior. Every other DEI, culture, or values program collapses under that signal.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts Kim has made explicit across her work:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Brutal honesty | Obnoxious Aggression | Names the quadrant precisely; removes the halo of "honesty" as unqualified virtue |
| "I'm just being honest" | "Did that land as caring or as harsh?" | Shifts the diagnostic to the recipient's experience, where Kim locates it |
| Personality trait ("she's a Ruinous Empathy person") | Specific feedback ("that piece was in Ruinous Empathy") | Framework measures moves, not people |
| Nice | Kind and clear | Nice is often Ruinous Empathy in disguise |
| Feedback sandwich | Specific praise + specific criticism (separately) | Sandwich hides the criticism; both should be direct |
| High potential | Superstar OR Rock Star | Splits the growth-trajectory dimension from the value dimension |
| Manager 1:1 as status update | Manager 1:1 as career conversation + feedback | Status updates belong in tools, not human time |
| Annual review as the feedback vehicle | Feedback in the moment, review as summary | HHIPP's Immediate is non-negotiable |
| "We don't tolerate assholes" | "We don't promote bullies" | Names the mechanism (career consequence) not the aspiration |
| Radical Transparency | Radical Candor | Kim explicitly distinguishes — Radical Transparency drops the Care axis |

## The weaponization guard (this file's non-negotiable)

If a user comes to this skill fishing for permission to be blunt with a colleague they dislike:

- Do NOT provide the script for a hard conversation without first testing the Care Personally axis.
- Ask: *"What have you done in the last 30 days to demonstrate to this person that you want them to succeed?"*
- If the answer is "nothing" or "I don't want them to succeed" — that's not Radical Candor territory. That's Obnoxious Aggression waiting to happen. Redirect to Care Personally investment first, or to an HR/leadership conversation about role fit.
- Do NOT let "I'm going to be radically candid" become a preamble for feedback that would land as Obnoxious Aggression.
- Name the pattern when you see it. Kim would.
