# Radical Candor — Worked Cases Kim Uses Publicly

> The recurring cases Kim Scott returns to across her book, podcast, essays, and talks. She uses them like a jazz musician uses standards — same tune, different context, the audience learns to hear the pattern. Reach for these when helping a user recognize their own situation on the matrix.

## The canonical duo

### 1. Sheryl Sandberg's "um" feedback — the canonical Radical Candor case

**Source:** *Radical Candor* (2017); First Round Review (2015); repeated in almost every talk and podcast.

**The story:**
- Kim, early in her Google career, gave a presentation to a large internal audience. She thought it went great.
- After the meeting, Sheryl Sandberg (Kim's boss) walked her out and started with something like *"You did a great job in there. But you're going to have to give up on the umms."*
- Kim brushed it off — small nervous tic, not important.
- Sheryl escalated: *"When you say um every third word, it makes you sound stupid."*
- Kim heard it — and acted on it. The blunt line worked.

**The Radical Candor diagnostic:** the same words from a stranger, or from a boss who hadn't invested in the relationship, would have landed as Obnoxious Aggression. Sheryl had built the Care Personally account over months (book-club invitations, family flexibility, active mentorship). The blunt line landed as Radical Candor because the Care account had a balance.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user understand why Care Personally is prerequisite to Challenge Directly landing well.

### 2. Bob at Google — the canonical Ruinous Empathy case

**Source:** *Radical Candor* (2017); First Round Review (2015); the story Kim tells most often.

**The story:**
- Kim managed Bob at Google. Warm engineer. Everyone liked him.
- Bob's work was subpar. Kim didn't tell him. She rationalized: *"He's going through a hard time. I don't want to be harsh. He'll figure it out."*
- Months passed. Bob's work stayed subpar. Kim's team started to suffer — the strongest engineers noticed, morale dropped.
- Around 10 months in, Kim had to fire Bob.
- Bob's response: *"Why didn't you tell me? Why didn't anyone tell me?"*

**Kim's diagnosis of herself:** she was in Ruinous Empathy. Her "kindness" — the withholding of hard feedback — harmed Bob more than any candor could have. He never got the chance to improve because he never knew there was a problem.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user who's soft-pedaling feedback, avoiding a hard conversation, or telling themselves they're being kind by not saying the hard thing. *Kind and clear is kind. Unkind and unclear is unkind.*

## Kim's own failures at Juice Software

### 3. The elevator morning

**Source:** [First Round Review — My Management Lessons from Three Failed Startups...](https://review.firstround.com/My-Management-Lessons-from-Three-Failed-Startups-Google-Apple-Dropbox-Twitter-and-Square); recounted in book and talks.

**The story:**
- Kim, CEO of Juice Software (early 2000s spreadsheet-collaboration startup).
- Blocked her whole morning to work on product pricing — a critical decision.
- The moment she stepped off the elevator, co-worker after co-worker needed to talk to her. She comforted and listened to each one in turn.
- End of the morning: no pricing done. Just a lot of comforting.

**Kim's diagnosis:** Care Personally without Challenge Directly. She was in Ruinous Empathy at the *organizational* level — prioritizing feelings over shipping.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user who confuses "being available" with "being a good manager." Care doesn't mean "always be interruptible for emotional support." It means "actually care what happens to this person's career, which means the product also has to ship."

### 4. "I thought assholes had the advantage"

**Source:** First Round Review; recounted in talks.

**The story:** Kim opens her origin story with the admission that early in her career, she thought jerks had a structural advantage — that being a hard-ass got results. The Juice Software period disabused her of this. She reversed her position and coined *"Good managers give a damn."*

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user who's romanticizing the "brilliant asshole" model. Kim's reversal is the direct counter — she believed it, tested it, and rejected it.

## The construction site analogy

### 5. Warning someone about to cut the beam wrong

**Source:** [Radical Candor Is Not Brutal Honesty (Medium)](https://kimmalonescott.medium.com/radical-candor-is-not-brutal-honesty-6cdeb04cac53).

**The story:** Imagine you're on a construction site and you see a worker about to cut a beam from the wrong end. The consequences are serious. Do you say *"Hey, moron, wrong end"?* No — you shout the specific instruction, urgently, with respect. Kim's point: *urgency is real, but urgency does not require disrespect.*

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user who thinks Challenge Directly requires bluntness or rudeness. It doesn't. Direct can be urgent AND respectful.

## The Rock Star / Superstar cases

### 6. Taylor Malone — the Rock Star mentor

**Source:** *Radical Candor* and podcast episodes on Rock Stars vs Superstars.

**The story:** Kim's mentor Taylor Malone was a world-class direct report — deeply skilled, deeply content in the role, and wanted stability (and fishing time). He didn't want promotion. Kim initially tried to push him upward, treating "wants promotion" as the marker of value. She stopped when she realized she was about to lose the Rock of Gibraltar of her team.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize a Rock Star on their team, and helping them stop pushing promotion on someone who doesn't want it. *Trajectory ≠ value.*

### 7. Joe Hyde — the Superstar empire builder

**Source:** *Radical Candor* and podcast; contrast pair to Taylor Malone.

**The story:** Joe Hyde was the counter-example. Steep growth trajectory, would go stir-crazy in the same job in 12 months, needed new problems and scope. Kim's move with Joe: give him the next stretch, next stretch, next stretch — and accept that eventually he'd outgrow her team.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize a Superstar on their team and stop stagnating them. Superstars leave for stretch opportunities elsewhere if you don't provide them internally.

## Career Conversations cases

### 8. Russ Laraway at Google — the origin of Career Conversations

**Source:** [Podcast Episode 5: Career Conversations](https://www.radicalcandor.com/podcast/s1-e5-career-conversations); Russ Laraway's book *When They Win, You Win*.

**The story:** Russ Laraway developed the 3-conversation ritual (Life Story → Dreams → Career Action Plan) as an ops manager at Google. Rolled it out to his team, found that direct reports became dramatically more engaged, and stayed longer. Kim adopted it, credits Russ every time.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user run their first Career Conversations. Also: model of how to credit a collaborator's contribution to a framework.

## Just Work / Radical Respect cases

Kim uses many real anecdotes in *Just Work* (2021) and *Radical Respect* (2024) — some her own, some others'. They tend to be specific and named-source; the pattern is the same as her Radical Candor cases:

- **Being mistaken for the intern** — factual correction with an I statement: *"I am the person you are negotiating with. This is Jack, my summer intern."*
- **Being talked over in meetings** — response tactics for the person being talked over, for allies, and for leaders designing meeting hygiene.
- **The Susan Fowler / Uber case** — anchor for how leadership failure at bullying compounds into systemic culture damage.
- **Being called a "girl" as a grown professional woman** — It statement, decency-based: *"It is disrespectful to call a grown woman a girl."*

## Composite cases the assistant can construct

When a user brings a scenario that maps to Kim's canonical cases, use hers by analogy. When the scenario is different enough that Kim's canonical cases don't fit, construct a case in Kim's register:

- Confession-first — describe a moment of Ruinous Empathy or Obnoxious Aggression.
- Specific — name the behavior, the setting, the words.
- Named quadrant — locate the move on the matrix explicitly.
- The reversal — what the person did differently, or wishes they had.

**Do not invent quotes from Kim.** If you're using a constructed case, don't attribute it to her. Say *"in Kim's method, this would be…"* rather than putting words in her mouth.

## When NOT to reach for these cases

- **When the user's situation is legally sensitive** (harassment, discrimination). Don't collapse it into a Radical Candor case study — direct the user to HR / legal counsel first. The framework informs; it doesn't replace them.
- **When the user is fishing for permission to be blunt** — don't reach for the Sheryl story as justification. Reach for the construction-site analogy or the Bob story to redirect toward Care Personally first.
- **When the user is deep in personal crisis at work** — the framework isn't the primary tool. Basic humanity is.
