# Obviously Awesome — Applications

> When Dunford's method fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where positioning fits well

- **B2B SaaS with a complex product** where sales conversations aren't landing consistently. This is the sweet spot Dunford's practice grew out of.
- **New prospects "don't get it" while existing customers love the product** — the mismatch is the tell.
- **Sales requires an extra call just to explain what you are.** Positioning debt.
- **Customers compare you to the wrong alternatives** ("Oh, you're like Salesforce" / "I could just do this in a spreadsheet"). Wrong market frame.
- **Different functions describe the product differently.** Founder says one thing, marketing says another, sales says a third. Cross-functional workshop territory.
- **Losing to "no decision" more than to competitors.** The Sales Pitch bolt-on is designed for exactly this.
- **Repositioning after a pivot, acquisition, new leadership, or market shift** (AI is the current forcing function).
- **Multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona complexity** where a single positioning is stretching thin.
- **Redesigning the sales pitch / deck / demo narrative.** The 8-step Sales Pitch is the operational bridge.
- **Category confusion** — 3+ years of "we don't know what to call ourselves". Postman-shaped problems.
- **Handling a competitor who lies about you in-deal** or whose "strength" is actually a trade-off you can flip.
- **Preparing a sales pitch that respects the champion + handles other stakeholders through objection handling.**
- **Annual planning where the marketing / GTM section keeps producing taglines instead of a strategic frame.**

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Pre-product/market fit.** Dunford's method assumes you have customers whose behavior you can observe — win/loss data, sales-call recordings, customer interviews. Pre-PMF startups don't have that evidence base yet. Positioning them prematurely locks in the wrong frame. Reach for: JTBD / continuous discovery / lean customer development to find PMF first, then position.

- **D2C consumer brand where branding dominates the customer experience.** Fashion, spirits, cosmetics, lifestyle brands, DTC apparel. Positioning still matters, but the branding-first altitude (Byron Sharp / Mark Ritson / Marty Neumeier / Wally Olins territory) owns decisions Dunford doesn't work in. Positioning becomes a section of the brand strategy, not the whole thing.

- **Enterprise strategy-level choice** (which market to enter, whether to fund a bet, corporate portfolio decisions). That's Playing to Win / Rumelt altitude. Positioning is downstream of strategy — Dunford tells you *how to compete in the market you chose*, not *which market to choose*.

- **Category creation as a bet-the-company move** where the founder is committed to years of educating the market. Dunford is skeptical of category creation as default (90% of tech IPOs won in existing categories), but if the user is *committed* to it, category-design frameworks (Play Bigger, Category Pirates) are the operational tool. Dunford's framework will still tell you what components you need — but the mode of execution is different.

- **When the user just wants a summary of the book.** Give them the book link. Don't run the exercise at them.

- **Pure copywriting or homepage rewrite** without a positioning problem underneath. Bring in a copywriter or a landing-page conversion specialist.

- **Personal branding / individual creator positioning.** The framework is B2B-product-shaped. It can be adapted, but a creator's positioning is usually about voice, POV, and audience segment more than "market category" — different craft.

## Adjacent frameworks — when to reach for a different one

| If the user's situation is... | Reach for... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-PMF startup search | Continuous Discovery (Teresa Torres), Lean Startup, JTBD | Search, not positioning |
| Which market to enter at all | Playing to Win (Roger Martin), Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Rumelt) | Strategic choice at company altitude, upstream of positioning |
| Diagnosis of what's broken before choosing | Rumelt's kernel (diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action) | Front-loads diagnosis; positioning presupposes you know your problem |
| Category creation as intentional bet | Play Bigger (Ramadan / Lochhead / Peterson / Maney), Category Pirates | Category-design is a specific play Dunford is explicitly skeptical of |
| Strategic narrative arc for a big talk / event / market moment | Andy Raskin's Strategic Narrative | Narrative arc, complementary to positioning — Raskin maps to Setup phase of Sales Pitch |
| B2C brand identity | Byron Sharp (How Brands Grow), Mark Ritson, Marty Neumeier | Brand-first altitude, different craft |
| Job the customer is hiring the product to do | JTBD (Christensen / Kalbach / Moesta / Ulwick) | Input to positioning — what job is being hired for |
| Adoption cycle / crossing the chasm | Geoffrey Moore | Segmentation and market-category thinking |
| Competitive strategy of the firm | Michael Porter (Competitive Strategy, Five Forces) | Upstream — Porter operates at firm altitude, Dunford at product-in-market |
| Messaging craft, tone, copy | Emma Stratton (Punchy), copywriting resources | Downstream of positioning |
| Sales methodology (discovery, MEDDPICC, Challenger) | JOLT Effect (Dixon), Challenger Sale (Dixon/Adamson), MEDDPICC | Complementary to Sales Pitch — Sales Pitch is the *narrative*, methodology is the *process* |
| Pricing strategy | Monetizing Innovation (Ramanujam), Value-Based Pricing | Downstream — positioning informs pricing, doesn't specify mechanics |
| Product marketing operating model | Product Marketing Alliance frameworks, Kevan Lee's work | Positioning is one artifact of PMM; PMM has broader scope |

## How Obviously Awesome composes with other frameworks

### Ries & Trout — *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981)

**Foundational.** Dunford is explicit: they defined the concept but "didn't give a how-to manual." She sees herself as executing on their idea, not replacing it. Her one active pushback is on the Mad-Libs positioning statement template inherited from that era — *"not only pointless but potentially dangerous"* — because it assumes one right answer per blank when most products can plausibly sit in multiple categories.

Use Ries & Trout for the *concept* of positioning as a battle for cognitive space; use Dunford for the *operational method* to win that battle.

### Geoffrey Moore — *Crossing the Chasm*

**Direct influence.** The technology adoption cycle informs Dunford's segmentation and market-category thinking. Explicitly named in the synthesis behind *Obviously Awesome*. If the user's positioning problem is "we win with early adopters but not mainstream", that's a Moore-shaped question — reach for both.

### Clayton Christensen — *Jobs to Be Done*

**Named as an input.** JTBD sensibility shows in Dunford's "what would customers do if you didn't exist?" question and her insistence that competitive alternatives include non-product options (status quo, DIY, "hire an intern"). Notably: her "competitive alternatives" question is essentially a JTBD move — buyers are hiring *something* to do the job; anything they might hire is an alternative. JTBD is upstream (what job?), positioning is downstream (against which alternatives for that job?).

### Michael Porter — *Competitive Strategy* / Five Forces

**Compatible, not competing.** Porter operates at strategy-of-the-firm altitude; Dunford is one altitude below (product positioning within a strategy). Porter's "differentiation strategy" is the *input*; her framework is the operational method for expressing it. Rarely cited by name in Dunford's work — the altitude difference is respected in both directions.

### Andy Raskin — Strategic Narrative

**Complementary, not overlapping.** Community shorthand: **Dunford is the gold standard for B2B positioning; Raskin is the gold standard for strategic narrative arc.** Positioning tells you *where you fit in the market*; strategic narrative tells you *why now, why this change matters*. Positioning comes before the narrative. Raskin's structure maps closest to Dunford's **Setup** phase in the Sales Pitch (especially Insight + Perfect World) — not to positioning itself.

If the user is building a keynote for a market moment (product launch event, customer conference, investor day), Raskin is the operative frame. If they're building the sales pitch for the actual buyer conversation, Dunford is.

### Roger Martin — Playing to Win

**Compatible, upstream.** Playing to Win asks "where to play / how to win" at the corporate level; Dunford's method is what "how to win" looks like at the product/market interface. She never cites Martin directly, but her insistence on trade-offs ("If you decide to go north, you cannot also go south.") is Martin-shaped. Use PtW to make the strategic choice; use Dunford to operationalize it into positioning + pitch.

### Richard Rumelt — Good Strategy Bad Strategy

**Compatible, upstream.** Rumelt's kernel (diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action) sits above positioning. Diagnose the real problem first (Rumelt); position within the market the diagnosis identifies (Dunford). Not overlapping.

### Category Design / Play Bigger

**Explicitly skeptical of "you must create your own category."** Her 90%-of-tech-IPOs-were-in-existing-categories datapoint is a direct counter to Play Bigger orthodoxy. She's not dismissive — she just resists it as the default move. Use Play Bigger only when you have a bet-the-company appetite and the marketing budget to educate for years. Otherwise, position within an existing category.

### Byron Sharp — *How Brands Grow*

**Downstream / different altitude.** Sharp's laws are useful for measuring in-market performance and marketing execution once positioning is set. They aren't positioning choice. Different craft.

### The JOLT Effect — Matt Dixon

**Direct complement to Sales Pitch.** Dixon's research on "no decision" mechanics (40–60% of B2B deals) is the empirical basis for Dunford's Sales Pitch design. Dunford has Dixon as a guest on The Positioning Show. If the user is deep on the "no decision" problem, read both.

### Challenger Sale — Adamson & Dixon

**Compatible.** Challenger's "commercial insight" maps to Dunford's Sales Pitch step 1 (Insight). The two frameworks share the DNA of "align on how the market is changing before you pitch the product". Use together.

## Frameworks Dunford has explicitly resisted (see `heuristics.md` for details)

- **Fill-in-the-blank positioning statements** (the Ries & Trout Mad-Libs template inheritance) — retired.
- **New category creation as default** — Play Bigger orthodoxy, resisted with the 90% datapoint.
- **Positioning as branding / messaging / copywriting / tagline** — the four confusions, pushed back on constantly.
- **Positioning as an internal opinion battle** — replaced with cross-functional evidence-based workshop.
- **Investor pitch = sales pitch** — different jobs, different audiences.

If the user invokes any of these expecting them to work, redirect gently but clearly.
