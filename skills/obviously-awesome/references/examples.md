## Janna Systems — the canonical "market category is the highest-leverage lever" story

**Where Dunford uses it:** *Obviously Awesome* (book, 2019), Lenny's Podcast (June 2022 + Oct 2023), most keynote talks, The Positioning Show (multiple episodes).

**The situation:** Late 1990s. Dunford was VP Marketing at Janna Systems, a generic "Enterprise CRM" competing against Siebel — a category leader with massive scale and sales muscle. Janna was small, kept losing, kept getting compared unfavorably to Siebel on features Siebel had that Janna didn't.

**The repositioning move:** rather than fight Siebel head-on in the generic Enterprise CRM category, Janna repositioned as **"CRM for Investment Banks"** — a specific vertical where their capabilities (relationship-heavy, deal-team-oriented, high-touch account management) mattered more than Siebel's breadth of features.

**Result:** grew from **<$2M to ~$80M ARR in 18 months**. Acquired for **$1.3B** (by Siebel, of all people).

**How the 5 components applied:**
- **Competitive alternatives** — no longer "Siebel + other generic CRMs"; now "manual investment-banking deal management + Excel + industry-specific point solutions".
- **Unique attributes** — deep support for the relationship-heavy, multi-deal-team, syndicate-oriented workflow that investment banks actually run.
- **Value themes** — win more deals; keep relationships warm across deal teams; forecast the pipeline in a way IB CFOs recognize.
- **Best-fit customers** — VPs at investment banks and their revenue-operations counterparts (specifically, not "enterprise buyers in general").
- **Market category** — "CRM for Investment Banks" (not "Enterprise CRM"). The lever that changed everything.

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the archetypal story of the market-category component being the single highest-leverage lever in the framework. Same product, same features — different frame — completely different competitive dynamics and win rates. Dunford returns to this case constantly because it makes the point that positioning is a *choice*, not a description.

**Quotable moment:**
> "We didn't change the product. We changed the market we were selling into." — paraphrased from her talks.

## Help Scout — insight-driven positioning

**Where Dunford uses it:** The Positioning Show — Nick Francis (Help Scout CEO) is a guest walking through Help Scout's positioning evolution. Referenced in Substack essays on Insight.

**The situation:** Help Scout is a customer support platform in a category dominated by Zendesk. Standard positioning: "customer support software" — puts them in Zendesk's shadow.

**The insight-driven reframe:** their point of view on the market — *"Customer service is a growth driver, not a cost center."* This insight anchors everything downstream. If a buyer accepts that insight, the value themes flip: customer support tooling isn't about ticket-deflection efficiency (Zendesk's frame); it's about relationship depth, retention, and expansion revenue (Help Scout's frame).

**Why the case is instructive:** shows how Insight (Sales Pitch step 1) reshapes the entire competitive frame. Same category ("customer support software"), different insight, different competitive dynamics — Zendesk isn't the direct alternative anymore because they're solving a different problem in the buyer's mental model.

## Postman — 3 years of category confusion

**Where Dunford uses it:** talks and Substack essays as the canonical "didn't know what to call themselves" story.

**The situation:** Postman started as a developer tool for testing APIs (originally a Chrome extension). For ~3 years they lived through category confusion — were they a testing tool? A collaboration platform for developers? A documentation tool? Each frame put them against different competitors with different value stories.

**The reposition:** landed on **"API platform"** — a broader category that housed API design, testing, documentation, collaboration, and monitoring as one integrated stack.

**Why the case is instructive:** market category isn't just a naming choice — it defines the shortlist of alternatives, the buyer persona, the value themes, and the price point the market will bear. Three years of category confusion cost Postman meaningful growth; naming "API platform" unlocked it.

**Also instructive:** category confusion is a *specific symptom* that triggers repositioning. If you can't name your category in a way that makes value obvious in 30 seconds, that's data. See *When To Revisit Your Positioning* in `author-live-sources.md`.

## Sampler — targeted digital sampling vs. manual distribution

**Where Dunford uses it:** talks, Business of Software.

**The situation:** Sampler is a product-sampling platform for CPG brands. Legacy alternative: manual in-store sampling — expensive, unmeasurable, imprecise targeting. Sampler's differentiation: digital-first, targeted by segment, measurable outcomes.

**The positioning move:** frame the competitive alternative as *manual sampling* (the actual status quo), not as other digital-sampling tools. This surfaces the differentiated value themes (measurement, targeting, cost efficiency) against a frame where they land hard.

**Why the case is instructive:** small example illustrating how the choice of competitive alternative determines whether your value themes land. Against other digital-sampling tools, Sampler looks incremental. Against manual sampling, Sampler looks transformative.

## Watcom — mobile-friendly SQL vs. heavy servers

**Where Dunford uses it:** older story from her early career; referenced in Business of Software talks.

**The situation:** Watcom was building a lightweight SQL database in an era dominated by heavy enterprise database servers (Oracle, Sybase, DB2). Standard positioning: "SQL database" — puts them against the giants on features they couldn't match.

**The reposition:** "mobile-friendly SQL" (for the emerging market of mobile and embedded applications). Different alternatives (embedded databases, custom storage), different value themes (small footprint, no admin overhead, works offline), different buyer.

**Why the case is instructive:** an early example of Dunford's method — choose a market frame where your unique attributes are the winning attributes, not the losing ones.

## Segway / Magic Leap — cautionary tales

**Where Dunford uses it:** talks and Substack essays as anti-examples.

**The situation:** both products had massive hype and press coverage. Neither had a clear, positioned value story for a specific best-fit customer. Segway launched as a "revolutionary transportation device" without a specific market frame (was it for police? tourists? commuters? mall security?). Magic Leap launched as a "spatial computing platform" without a clear use case that justified the price.

**Why the cases are instructive:** hype ≠ positioning. Media buzz doesn't tell buyers what category you're in, who the best-fit customer is, or why the value theme is important. Both are examples of what happens when you skip the 5 components and let the media (or the product's origin story) name the category for you.

## Google vs. Ask Jeeves; Facebook vs. Myspace

**Where Dunford uses it:** Quickstart Guide and Substack essays arguing against "must-create-a-category" logic.

**The point:** Ask Jeeves invented the "natural-language question-answering" category. Google entered the existing "search engine" category and won it. Myspace invented "social networking" as a mass-market category. Facebook entered the same category and won it.

**Why the cases are instructive:** first-movers often lose. The market-education tax new-category creators pay is real, and fast-followers can arrive with sharper positioning against a market that's already been taught to want the thing. The 90%-of-recent-public-tech-IPOs-won-in-existing-categories datapoint sits alongside these cases.

**Also instructive:** these cases push back on Play Bigger / category-design orthodoxy without denying that category creation ever works — it works sometimes, but the base rate is unfavorable, so treating it as a default is bad odds.

## Anonymized "airline maintenance software" client

**Where Dunford uses it:** Business of Software talks, especially *The Hard Parts of Positioning*.

**The situation:** an enterprise B2B software company where the four functions (founder, marketing, sales, product) described the product in four different ways. The founder called it "the operating system for airline maintenance". Marketing called it "predictive maintenance analytics". Sales called it "the reason your planes stop breaking mid-flight". Product called it "a data platform for reliability engineering".

**The diagnosis:** classic elephant/blindfold — every function was touching a different part of the elephant. Cross-functional workshop surfaced that they had different beliefs about who the best-fit customer was (VPs of maintenance vs. reliability engineers vs. CFOs), and until they resolved that, no positioning would stick.

**Why the case is instructive:** illustrates the diagnostic symptom "different functions describe the product differently" and the required intervention (cross-functional workshop with evidence).

## Salesforce moving upmarket

**Where Dunford uses it:** talks as a repositioning example.

**The situation:** Salesforce's evolution from SMB CRM ("No Software" positioning against enterprise CRM incumbents like Siebel) to enterprise CRM leader itself. Different market category, different competitive alternatives, different best-fit customer, different value themes — at each stage.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows repositioning as an ongoing process, not a one-time event. Salesforce has re-run the exercise multiple times across the product's life.

## The anonymized "300 companies" dataset

**Where Dunford uses it:** Business of Software talk *The Hard Parts of Positioning: Learnings from 11 years and 300 technology companies*.

**The situation:** Dunford's consulting practice has worked with 300+ technology companies on positioning. The talk pulls anonymized patterns from that dataset — the recurring failure modes, the recurring "aha" moments, the frequency of each anti-pattern.

**Key patterns from the talk (as she has publicly summarized):**
- Most positioning problems come from the market category being wrong, not the value themes.
- Cross-functional disagreement is the #1 symptom that positioning needs a rework.
- Teams underestimate the status quo as a competitive alternative — most B2B pitches ignore it and lose to it.
- Category creation attempts fail more often than they succeed.
- Sales-in-the-room is a hard requirement for repositioning to stick.

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the base-rate evidence for the framework. When Dunford tells you "here's the pattern I see across 300 companies", that's empirical grounding for the heuristics.
