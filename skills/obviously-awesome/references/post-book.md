## Sales Pitch (2023) — the sales-conversation extension of positioning

Published Oct 2023. Bolts onto positioning as the operational bridge into live sales conversations.

**Core thesis (Lenny's podcast, Oct 22, 2023):**
> "The pitch was never about 'pushing' our products on buyers. It was always about helping customers make confident purchase decisions." Buyers "are more worried about messing up than they are about missing out."

**Anchor stat used in every talk:** 40–60% of B2B deals end in **"no decision"**. That's the real enemy — not competitors. A pitch built to beat a competitor while ignoring buyer paralysis loses the majority of deals to paralysis regardless.

**The 8-step pitch, in two halves — Setup + Follow-Through.** Full description in `references/method.md` §Sales Pitch. Summary:

**SETUP (align on the market before you talk about your product):**
1. **Insight** — "The thing we understand about the market that the others do not." The hardest and most crucial step.
2. **Alternatives** — grouped by approach (status quo, DIY, spreadsheets, competing products), not vendor names. Present pros/cons objectively.
3. **The Perfect World** — the ideal purchase criteria a buyer sharing your worldview would use.

**FOLLOW-THROUGH:**
4. **Introduction** — name the product and market category.
5. **Differentiated Value** — features that are BOTH unique AND valuable, grouped into the 1–4 value themes.
6. **Proof** — case studies and testimonials matched to prospect's industry/size.
7. **Objections (optional)** — address unspoken concerns proactively.
8. **The Ask** — explicit next step.

**Why other pitch shapes fail** (from the book + Lenny summary): walkthrough (assumes buyer knowledge), problem/solution (competitors define same problem), vision narrative (unproven), hero's journey (engaging but doesn't help buyer decide).

**Rollout test:** the pitch is ready when reps stop requesting changes AND reps prefer the new pitch to their old deck. If they quietly go back to their own decks, the pitch didn't stick — usually because sales wasn't in the room during design.

**Source:** *Sales Pitch: How to Craft a Story to Stand Out and Win* — April Dunford, Ambient Press, 2023. [Amazon](https://www.amazon.com/Sales-Pitch-Craft-Story-Stand/dp/1999023021)

## Obviously Awesome 2nd expanded edition (2026)

Announced Jan 12, 2026 on Substack: [*Announcement: Obviously Awesome — new updated and expanded edition*](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/announcement-obviously-awesome-the).

### The restructure — 10 steps → 5 steps

The 2019 book had a 10-step process. The 2026 edition restructures to **5 steps matching the 5 components** — "the first few steps were really pre-work for a positioning exercise, and the final steps described work that needed to happen after the exercise" (Substack, Jan 2026).

**New 5-step exercise:**
1. Let go of where you came from
2. Isolate your uniqueness
3. Map attributes to value themes
4. Who cares? (best-fit customers)
5. Choose your best market frame of reference

Plus optional layer 6: **Trend**.

## Value vs. Objection Handling — one of the sharpest post-book distinctions

**Source:** *Value vs. Objection Handling* — Substack, Feb 6, 2025.

Teams conflate these constantly. Dunford's distinction:

- **Differentiated value** = what makes buyers *choose* you. Something unique AND valuable that opens the door to "yes".
- **Objection handling** = what stops them from *not-choosing* you. Table-stakes removal, defense against blockers.

**Practical test:** for every "value" candidate in your pitch, ask: does this cause a buyer to sit up and choose us? Or does it remove a reason not to? First is differentiated value. Second is objection handling — belongs in step 7 of the sales pitch, not step 5.

**Why it matters:** teams that pack step 5 (differentiated value) with objection removals (SOC 2, GDPR, uptime SLAs) dilute the actual value story and lose because buyers can't find a reason to choose them beyond "they check the boxes."

## The "No Differentiation" Illusion

**Source:** *The "No Differentiation" Illusion* — Substack, Sep 19, 2024.

Teams often conclude "there's no real differentiation between us and X" and give up on positioning. Dunford: usually wrong, six sources of the illusion:

1. **Value blindness** — you have differentiation but haven't translated features to value themes.
2. **Product illiteracy** — marketing/sales don't understand the product at depth.
3. **Inside-out competitor views** — you look at competitors' marketing, not what they deliver in deals.
4. **Loss obsession (whale bias)** — one big lost deal skews the view.
5. **Product pessimism** — you underrate what you have because you know the flaws.
6. **Sloppy segmentation** — you're comparing yourself in a segment where you don't win.

**Non-product sources of differentiation to test** before concluding there's none:
- **Pricing** — usage-based vs. per-seat, all-in vs. modular, transparent vs. quote-based
- **Partnership ecosystems** — deeper integrations, exclusive marketplaces
- **Distribution** — direct vs. channel-only, self-serve vs. sales-led
- **Wrapped services** — managed vs. self-serve, included vs. add-on
- **Data / model** — proprietary corpus, exclusive telemetry, longer training history
- **Company structure** — mutual, non-profit, benefit corp

**Signature line from the essay:**
> "They are not a true alternative if they don't show up on a shortlist."

## Positioning in the Age of AI (May 2026)

**Sources:** *Positioning in the Age of AI* — Substack, May 21, 2026 · *In the Age of AI, You Need a Point of View* — Substack, Jun 11, 2026.

Key positions Dunford has taken:

- **Ignore your future competition in your positioning, but never in your roadmap.** AI-native competitors will keep emerging; you can't position against a moving future. Position against today's shortlist; hedge with your roadmap.
- **"Smoking their competitor's marketing"** — Dunford's warning about teams whose positioning is a fantasy version of what a competitor claims to do (from that competitor's homepage) rather than what the competitor actually delivers in deals.
- **AI as trend, not category** — the trend/layer principle still applies. Layer AI on top of your value only if it makes the value more obvious to the target. Don't lead with "AI-powered" unless the AI is the differentiated value.
- **The "point of view" imperative** — in an AI-flooded market where every homepage sounds the same, having a specific point of view on how the market is changing is what earns attention. Companies without a POV get lost in the sameness.

## Multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona (post-book systematization)

**Source:** *A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas* — Substack.

The 2019 book didn't systematize multi-product positioning. Dunford has since:

- **Single product** → don't split company vs. product positioning. Consolidate.
- **Multi-product** → 3 options: umbrella positioning, lead-product model, product family/platform. Choose based on how products relate to buyers.
- **Multiple segments** → one positioning suffices if differentiated capabilities are consistent; tailor content and case examples per segment, not the positioning itself.
- **Multiple personas** → position for the **champion** (internal deal advocate); handle other stakeholders through **objection handling**, not separate positioning.

The champion-only rule is the single biggest post-book operational addition to the framework.

## How to Manage a Big Positioning Change

**Source:** Substack — *How to Manage a Big Positioning Change* (link to the "special Lenny's podcast drop" essay).

Practical guidance for repositioning a company after a pivot or acquisition:

- **CEO must sponsor** — otherwise it dies in the first quarter.
- **Sales must be in the room** during the exercise — otherwise they don't switch pitches.
- **Regular twice-a-year check-in** even without symptoms.
- **Acquisitions need their own combined-positioning exercise** — the acquiring company's positioning doesn't automatically extend to the acquired product, and vice versa. Run a fresh 5-component exercise for the combined entity.
- **Change management framing:** treat positioning changes like product launches internally. Talk track, training, collateral rollout, metrics on adoption.

## What to do When a Competitor Lies

**Source:** Substack, Jan 16, 2025.

Handling misrepresentation in-deal. Don't get emotional — reads as insecure. Prepare a clean talk track:

1. **Name the claim** the competitor is making, without heat.
2. **Provide evidence** that refutes it (data, customer example, third-party validation).
3. **Return to your value story** — don't dwell on the competitor.

Rehearse with reps. The calm, prepared response builds trust — the emotional response validates the competitor's frame.

## How to Turn a Competitor's Strength into a Weakness

**Source:** Substack, Dec 19, 2024.

Reframing move: describe the competitor's celebrated strength in terms of the trade-off it required.

**Examples:**
- "They're enterprise-grade" → "Yes, and that means 6-month deployments and mandatory professional services."
- "They have every feature" → "Yes, and that means 200-page contracts and a $500K minimum."
- "They're the market leader" → "Yes, and that means you're one of 10,000 accounts to their CS team."

The strength doesn't disappear; it earns its cost. Best-fit buyers who don't want that cost then have a reason to look elsewhere.

## Market Insight and Communicating Your Company's Point of View

**Source:** Substack — *Market Insight and Communicating Your Company's Point of View*.

Dunford's essay on the "we stink at talking about our point of view" problem. Introduces the **insight → value bridge**:

- Insight = your specific claim about how the market or the buyer's job is changing (see Sales Pitch step 1).
- The insight has to imply the value you deliver.
- If a buyer accepts the insight, the value proposition becomes obvious. If the insight is wrong or weak, the value has to fight for attention.

The bridge is why the Insight step in the Sales Pitch is "the hardest and most crucial." Get it right and the rest of the pitch flows; get it wrong and even good differentiated value lands flat.

## Adjacent framework updates

Positions Dunford has clarified post-book on adjacent frameworks:

- **Ries & Trout's *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981)** — foundational; "they defined the concept but didn't give a how-to manual." The Mad-Libs positioning statement template inherited from that era is now retired ("pointless but potentially dangerous").
- **Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm*** — direct influence; the technology adoption cycle informs segmentation and market-category thinking.
- **Clayton Christensen / JTBD** — upstream input; "what would customers do if we didn't exist?" is a JTBD move.
- **Category Design / *Play Bigger*** — explicitly skeptical of "you must create your own category." 90% of recent public tech companies won in existing categories.
- **Andy Raskin's Strategic Narrative** — complementary. Positioning tells you *where you fit in the market*; strategic narrative tells you *why now, why this change matters*. Raskin's structure maps closest to Dunford's Setup phase (Insight + Perfect World).
