# Obviously Awesome — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Dunford's "here's how it actually plays out" moves — that separate applying positioning well from doing one of the four things people call positioning that aren't (branding, messaging, copywriting, category creation). Attribution is precise: this from the 2019 book, this from a 2024 Substack essay, this from the 2023 Sales Pitch book.

## Do's

### Start with competitive alternatives, not with your product

The single most-repeated correction. Teams start positioning by listing their own features and asking "what makes us different?" — which produces answers only relative to phantom competitors. Start instead with **what would customers do if we didn't exist?** and build the shortlist from real buyer behavior.

**How to apply:**
1. Pull 5 recent won deals, 5 recent lost-to-competitor, 5 recent lost-to-no-decision.
2. For each, list what the buyer was actually comparing you against — including the status quo, DIY, spreadsheets, adjacent categories.
3. That's your competitive alternatives set. Position against *that*, not against the vendor list marketing keeps in a slide.

**Author's words:**
> "What would customers do if we didn't exist?" — repeated across essays and talks; the single most-used diagnostic.

### Get the cross-functional team in the room

Positioning is not a marketing exercise. Do it with founder/CEO, marketing head, sales leadership (+ 1–2 experienced AEs), product management, and customer success — together.

**Why:** every function sees a different part of the elephant. Marketing sees campaigns and analyst reports. Sales sees the shortlist in-deal. CS sees why customers stay. Product sees the roadmap. Positioning that ignores any of them dies in the field.

**Author's words:**
> "The guy on the tail says, 'It's a snake,' and the guy on the leg says, 'It's a tree.'" — the elephant/blindfold parable, used constantly.

### Insist the CEO sponsors it

If the CEO isn't in the room and doesn't own the outcome, the new positioning doesn't stick. Sales won't switch pitches. Marketing will keep the old homepage. Product will drift toward its own words.

From *How to Manage a Big Positioning Change*: the CEO must sponsor for a repositioning to hold across the org — otherwise it dies in the first quarter.

### Sell what's on the truck

Position what the product actually is today, not what the roadmap says it will be. Buyers evaluate what they can buy now. Roadmap-positioning tanks credibility the moment sales has to backpedal.

**Author's words:**
> "Sell what's on the truck." — recurring shorthand across her talks.

### Use the "so what?" bridge from features to value

For every unique attribute, force yourself to answer *"so what?"* two or three times. Each iteration walks up the ladder from feature → capability → outcome the buyer cares about. Stop when you reach an outcome a CFO would nod at.

### Match proof to the prospect

Case studies and logos are proof, not decoration. Match by industry, size, buying pattern, and — critically — the value theme the prospect is buying. A generic "here are our biggest logos" slide is worse than no proof.

### Position for the champion, handle others through objections

From *A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas*. Champions carry the deal internally. Position the value story to them. Anticipate every other stakeholder's concerns (CFO on price, IT on integration, procurement on legal) and equip the champion with pre-answered objections — but don't dilute the positioning trying to speak to all of them at once.

### Test in live sales conversations, not in slides

The pitch is a hypothesis until reps run it. Ship it to a handful of experienced AEs, listen to the calls, and iterate on what actually lands. The **rollout test** from *Sales Pitch*:
1. Reps stop requesting changes.
2. Reps prefer the new pitch to their old deck.

If reps quietly slip back to their own decks after launch, the pitch didn't stick.

### Revisit positioning on a cadence

Twice a year, even without symptoms. Every major trigger (pivot, acquisition, new leadership, meaningful competitor entry, tech disruption like AI). Positioning is not once-and-done.

**Source:** *When To Revisit Your Positioning* (Substack, Sep 5, 2024).

## Don'ts

### Don't write a positioning statement as a Mad-Libs template

The "For [target] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [key benefit] unlike [alternative]" template is the single most-cited example of positioning-that-isn't. It flattens a 5-component decision into blanks, and assumes one right answer per blank when most products can plausibly sit in multiple categories.

**Author's words:**
> "Not only pointless but potentially dangerous." — on the fill-in-the-blank positioning statement, from her Quickstart Guide.

### Don't confuse positioning with branding

Branding is visual identity, tone, colors, imagery. Positioning is the strategic frame that tells you what the branding needs to convey. If you sell security software to banks, positioning tells you the branding should convey trust — but the color palette and logo mark are not positioning.

**Author's words:**
> "Storytelling, messaging, a tag line, your vision, branding — these are all examples of things that happen AFTER positioning. Each of these things requires positioning as an input."

### Don't confuse positioning with messaging

Messaging is the specific phrases that communicate positioning across channels and personas. Positioning is strategic; messaging is tactical. You can rewrite messaging weekly; you should rewrite positioning maybe twice a year.

### Don't confuse positioning with copywriting

A bad homepage is a symptom, not the disease. You cannot diagnose positioning from outside — you need to know the actual buyer, the actual shortlist, the actual won/lost patterns. Someone who says "help me fix our positioning" while showing you a paragraph of homepage copy usually needs to back up to the workshop.

### Don't create a new category as your default move

**Author's words:**
> "We need to educate the market" is usually a red flag that you're trying to create an unnecessary new category.

From her Quickstart Guide: 90% of recent public tech companies won in *existing* categories. Google beat Ask Jeeves. Facebook beat Myspace. First-movers frequently lose to fast-followers who let someone else pay the education tax and then win on execution.

Create a category only when: the existing categories genuinely can't hold your value, you have the marketing budget to educate for years, and you're willing to bet the company on it.

### Don't have more than 4 value themes

More than that and the pitch collapses. Buyers can't hold 7 things in working memory. Sales can't pitch consistently. The differentiation dilutes.

### Don't confuse differentiated value with objection handling

The clearest single distinction Dunford has made post-book, from *Value vs. Objection Handling* (Substack, Feb 6, 2025):

- **Differentiated value** = what makes buyers *choose* you.
- **Objection handling** = what stops them from *not-choosing* you.

Teams conflate these constantly — treating "we have SOC 2" as differentiated value when it's actually just an objection to remove. Real differentiated value is unique AND valuable. Objection handling is table-stakes AND removes a blocker.

### Don't position around a trend

Layer trend on top only if it makes the value more obvious. Don't lead with it. From *Positioning in the Age of AI* (May 2026): *"Ignore your future competition in your positioning, but never in your roadmap."* Teams positioning primarily around AI in 2026 are following a fad; the ones positioning around real differentiation and letting AI amplify it are building durable moats.

### Don't chase phantom competitors

If the "competitor" never shows up on a real buyer's shortlist, it's a phantom. Waste of positioning capital. Analyst quadrants are full of phantoms.

### Don't let sales pitch = investor pitch

Different audiences, different jobs. Investor pitch persuades on the size and defensibility of the opportunity; sales pitch helps a specific buyer make a specific purchase decision. Blending them wrecks both.

**Author's words:**
> "Why Your Investor Pitch Is Ruining Your Sales Pitch" — Blog, Sep 2019.

### Don't try to fix positioning by rewriting the homepage

Positioning is upstream of copy. If the homepage feels wrong, the positioning is probably wrong. Rewriting copy without redoing the positioning produces prettier symptoms.

### Don't reposition without sales in the room

If sales isn't there while you're deciding, sales won't switch pitches after. Positioning stays as a marketing deck and dies within a quarter.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The "no differentiation" illusion

From *The "No Differentiation" Illusion* (Substack, Sep 19, 2024). Teams claim "there's no real differentiation between us and X" — usually wrong. Six sources of the illusion:

1. **Value blindness** — you have differentiation but haven't translated features to value.
2. **Product illiteracy** — marketing/sales don't understand what the product actually does at depth.
3. **Inside-out competitor views** — you look at competitors' marketing, not what they actually deliver in deals.
4. **Loss obsession** — one big lost deal (whale bias) skews your view of the field.
5. **Product pessimism** — you underrate what you have because you know its flaws intimately.
6. **Sloppy segmentation** — you're comparing yourself to a competitor that wins in a different segment.

Non-product differentiation sources to test before concluding there's none: **pricing, partnership ecosystems, distribution, wrapped services**.

### The whale bias

Sales overweights the one giant deal they lost last quarter and rewrites positioning around it. That deal may not have been best-fit. Check the pattern across 10 recent deals, not the one that hurts most.

### Horizon competitors vs. what sales actually sees

Product often fixates on rivals that never show up in sales conversations. Positioning should reflect the shortlist buyers *actually* compare, not the rivals product wants to worry about.

### The competitor "strength" that's actually a trade-off

From *How to Turn a Competitor's Strength into a Weakness* (Substack, Dec 19, 2024). Describe the competitor's celebrated strength in terms of the trade-off it required. "They're enterprise-grade" → "Yes, and that means 6-month deployments and mandatory professional services engagements." The strength doesn't disappear; it earns its cost.

### When a competitor lies about you

From *What to do When a Competitor Lies* (Substack, Jan 16, 2025). Don't get angry or emotional in-deal — that reads as insecure. Prepare a clean talk track that names the claim, provides the evidence that refutes it (calmly), and moves on. Rehearse the talk track with reps.

### The "we're the best" statement

Fails Dunford's implicit coin-flip test: if you and the competitor both say "we're the best" and the buyer can't tell the difference, you've said nothing. "Best" is meaningless without a specified frame — best at what, for whom, against which alternatives.

### Assuming novelty beats clarity

**Author's words:**
> "It's always better to be a little boring than completely baffling."

New categories, novel taglines, invented vocabulary — all fail the "does the buyer understand in 30 seconds?" test. Boring-but-clear wins.

## Pro tips

### The elephant/blindfold parable as workshop opener

Open every workshop with the parable. Sets the expectation that different functions see different parts, and that's the point of getting them in the room. Prevents the loudest voice from winning.

### The "opening scene of a movie" analogy for positioning

Explain positioning as context-setting: the opening scene tells you what genre you're watching. A romantic-comedy opening scene sets one expectation; a horror opening scene sets another. Same product can be framed as different genres, and the frame determines whether the buyer's response is "oh, that's exactly what I need" or "meh, I don't get it".

### Anchor sales-pitch redesigns with the 40–60% no-decision stat

Every sales-pitch talk Dunford gives anchors on this: 40–60% of B2B deals end in no decision. The stat reframes the whole conversation — the enemy is buyer paralysis, not competitors. Reps who internalize this stop pitching against the vendor list and start helping the buyer make a confident decision.

### Watch Gong calls, not the slide deck

The evidence for what's working is in live sales conversations. Read win/loss transcripts. Listen for the moment the buyer's tone shifts — that's where a value theme landed or missed. Slide decks are theater; calls are ground truth.

### Sell to Perfect World, not to Product

In the Sales Pitch flow, the Perfect World step (step 3) does the persuasion. By the time you introduce the product (step 4), the buyer has already agreed on the ideal criteria — you're just naming what fits.

### Position against status quo as your default alternative

If the honest #1 alternative is doing nothing, position against doing nothing. Most B2B pitches ignore this and lose to the buyer choosing to postpone.

## Anti-patterns (the "bad positioning" Dunford explicitly names)

### Positioning as an internal opinion battle

**What it looks like:** the marketing team, sales team, and CEO have three different one-sentence descriptions of the product; they argue about which is right.
**Why it fails:** *"Marketing never wins the battle of opinions."* Opinions vs. opinions is unbounded. Only evidence — from real buyers, real wins, real losses — settles positioning.
**How to redirect:** call a cross-functional workshop with the evidence in the room. Force the debate to be grounded in what buyers actually do.

### Fighting phantom competitors

**What it looks like:** competitive matrices full of vendors nobody ever meets in deals.
**Why it fails:** you're allocating positioning capital to fights you don't have. Meanwhile the real fights (status quo, DIY, adjacent category) go unaddressed.
**How to redirect:** rebuild the competitive alternatives list from the last 20 real deals. Anyone not on 3+ shortlists gets cut from the positioning surface (they may still matter for competitive intel).

### New category creation as default

**What it looks like:** "we need to invent a new category and educate the market."
**Why it fails:** first-movers frequently lose. 90% of recent public tech IPOs won in existing categories. Category creation demands years of marketing budget and forecloses fast-follow positioning.
**How to redirect:** name every existing category the product could plausibly live in. Rank them by how obvious the value becomes in each frame. Pick the strongest existing category before considering invention.

### Too many value themes

**What it looks like:** 6, 7, 12 value themes in the pitch.
**Why it fails:** buyer can't hold them, sales can't pitch them, differentiation dilutes.
**How to redirect:** cluster ruthlessly. Cap at 4. If you can't decide which 4, you don't understand the buyer well enough yet.

### Value vs. objection handling confusion

**What it looks like:** SOC 2, GDPR compliance, uptime SLAs treated as "differentiated value" in the pitch.
**Why it fails:** those are table-stakes objection removals, not reasons to choose you. Treating them as value dilutes the actual value themes.
**How to redirect:** for every "value" candidate, ask: does this make buyers *choose* us, or does it stop them from *not-choosing* us? First = value. Second = objection.

### Positioning as branding / messaging / copywriting / vision-setting

**What it looks like:** the "positioning project" produces a new logo, a new tagline, a rewritten homepage, or a mission statement — but not a decision about competitive alternatives, category, and best-fit customer.
**Why it fails:** you've addressed the symptoms, not the disease. The strategic frame is unchanged.
**How to redirect:** ask what changed about *(a) who the best-fit customer is, (b) what category we tell buyers we're in, (c) which competitive alternatives we're beating, and how.* If none of the three changed, no positioning happened.

## Positioning vs. Messaging vs. Branding — the crisp distinction

The distinction Dunford enforces constantly. Keep this on hand.

| Concept | What it is | Where it lives | Cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Positioning** | The strategic frame — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value themes, best-fit customers, market category. Defines the market and why you win it. | Cross-functional workshop output. One-page canvas. Input to everything else. | Revisit ~2x/year or on major triggers. |
| **Messaging** | The specific phrases that communicate the positioning across channels and personas. | Copy: homepage, emails, sales collateral, event booths. | Iterate continuously as channels change. |
| **Branding** | Visual identity, tone, colors, imagery, name. | Design system, brand guidelines, agency deliverables. | Rare — full rebrands every 5+ years. |

**Dunford's rule:** positioning is upstream. If positioning is wrong, better messaging or better branding cannot fix it. If positioning is right, messaging and branding become downstream execution problems.

## Common misapplications (teams saying they're doing positioning but aren't)

- **Copywriting** — rewriting the homepage. See above.
- **Branding** — new logo, new color palette. Downstream artifact.
- **Messaging** — new taglines, new headline. Also downstream.
- **Vision-setting** — writing where we want to be in 5 years. That's strategy, not positioning.
- **Category creation as shortcut** — "we'll be the leader of a new category we invent." Rare that it works.
- **Investor-pitch upgrade** — persuading VCs is not the same job as helping buyers decide.
- **Sales-training on the old pitch** — training reps on a broken pitch doesn't fix positioning.

**Test for the real thing:** did a decision get made about *(a) who the best-fit customer is, (b) which market category we're in, (c) which competitive alternatives we're beating and how?* If none of the three changed, no positioning happened.
