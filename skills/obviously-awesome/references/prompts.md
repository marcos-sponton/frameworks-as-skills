# Obviously Awesome — Prompt templates

> Copy-paste templates users can adapt. Each starts with a user situation and invokes the skill in a shape Claude can execute well.

## Run a positioning workshop from scratch

```
I want to run a positioning exercise on {{product/company}} using April Dunford's method.

Context:
- Product: {{describe in 3-5 sentences}}
- Stage: {{early / growth / mature — if pre-PMF, note that explicitly}}
- Current situation: {{who the customers are today, what functions describe the product differently, what symptoms triggered this}}
- Who's in the room: {{list functions}}
- Evidence available: {{recent won deals, lost deals, sales-call recordings, customer interviews — what you actually have}}

Please walk me through the 5 components as a set, starting with competitive alternatives.
When my answers sound weak or generic, challenge them using Dunford's explicit critiques.
Push me toward the "what would customers do if we didn't exist?" test on competitive alternatives.
Enforce the champion-only rule if multiple personas come up.
Cap value themes at 4. If I try more, force clustering.
Cite Dunford sources (book chapter, Substack essay, podcast episode) when you introduce a specific device.
```

## Critique existing positioning

```
Here's our current positioning:

{{paste positioning statement / homepage headline / one-pager / deck excerpt}}

Please critique it against Dunford's method. Specifically:
1. Which of the 5 components are missing or weak?
2. Is this positioning, or is it actually branding / messaging / a tagline / a mission statement? (Push back on the confusions.)
3. What competitive alternatives is this positioned against — and are those the alternatives real buyers actually shortlist?
4. Is the market category the one where our value becomes obvious in 30 seconds?
5. Are the value themes real differentiated value, or are some of them table-stakes objection handling?
6. What would Dunford push back on hardest here?
7. What's our strongest component that's worth doubling down on?
```

## Design or rebuild a B2B sales pitch

```
I need to rebuild our sales pitch using April Dunford's 8-step structure from Sales Pitch (2023).

Context:
- Product: {{describe}}
- Best-fit customer: {{who champion is, what industry, what size}}
- Current pitch shape: {{walkthrough / problem-solution / vision / hero's journey / other}}
- What's not working: {{losing to no decision? losing to specific competitors? deals stalling in eval?}}
- Positioning inputs: {{if you've done a positioning exercise, paste the 5 components}}

Please walk me through the 8 steps in order (Setup: Insight, Alternatives, Perfect World / Follow-Through: Introduction, Differentiated Value, Proof, Objections, Ask).
Anchor on the 40–60% "no decision" frame throughout — the enemy is buyer paralysis, not competitors.
For the Insight step (the hardest), push me to state a genuine point of view about how the market or the buyer's job is changing.
For the Alternatives step, force me to include status quo / DIY / spreadsheets, not just vendor names.
Warn me when I'm about to introduce alternatives the buyer hasn't already considered.
End with a specific Ask, not "let us know what you think".
```

## Evaluate competitive alternatives (kill the phantom competitors)

```
Help me rebuild our competitive alternatives list using Dunford's method.

Current list (what marketing puts on our competitive matrix): {{paste}}

Please:
1. Ask what evidence we have from real deals — win/loss data, sales-call transcripts, actual shortlists buyers presented.
2. Identify which entries are phantom competitors (never show up on real shortlists).
3. Force us to name the status quo / DIY / spreadsheet / adjacent-category alternatives buyers actually consider.
4. Rank alternatives by how often they appear on real shortlists, not by marketing's fear.
5. Highlight where the 6 sources of "no differentiation" illusion might be blinding us (value blindness, product illiteracy, inside-out competitor views, whale bias, product pessimism, sloppy segmentation).
6. Reference Dunford's essay "The No Differentiation Illusion" when relevant.
```

## Translate positioning to messaging

```
Here's our positioning (the 5-component canvas):

{{paste}}

Please translate this into messaging for:
- Homepage headline + subhead + hero section
- 3-sentence elevator pitch
- Cold email opener
- Sales-slide "who we are" section
- Analyst-briefing one-pager

Rules:
- Do NOT rewrite the positioning. Messaging is downstream of positioning.
- Use Dunford's vocabulary (competitive alternatives, best-fit, differentiated value, market category) in the positioning — but the messaging can be in the buyer's vocabulary.
- Keep messages boring-and-clear over novel-and-baffling. ("It's always better to be a little boring than completely baffling.")
- Match the market category consistently across all channels.
```

## Handle a competitor who misrepresents you

```
A competitor is telling prospects {{describe the claim they're making about us}}.

Using Dunford's playbook from "What to do When a Competitor Lies" (Substack, Jan 2025):
1. Draft a clean talk track for reps to use in-deal.
2. Structure: name the claim without heat, provide the evidence that refutes it, return to our value story.
3. Anticipate follow-up questions the buyer will ask after hearing the refutation.
4. Also apply "How to Turn a Competitor's Strength into a Weakness" if relevant — reframe the strength in terms of the trade-off it required.

Do NOT recommend responses that are emotional, defensive, or that dwell on the competitor. Dunford is emphatic: calm and evidence-based, then return to our story.
```

## Decide whether to create a new market category

```
We're considering positioning as {{new category name}} — a category that doesn't fully exist yet.

Apply Dunford's skepticism from the Quickstart Guide and Substack essays:
1. Name every existing category the product could plausibly live in.
2. For each existing category: how obvious does our value become in that frame? What's the shortlist we'd compete against?
3. What's the cost of educating the market on the new category — years of marketing budget, sales-call friction, analyst confusion?
4. What's our appetite for that cost? Do we have the war chest and patience?
5. Reference the 90%-of-recent-public-tech-IPOs-won-in-existing-categories datapoint.
6. Reference Google-vs-Ask-Jeeves and Facebook-vs-Myspace as evidence for the fast-follower advantage.
7. Only recommend category creation if the existing categories genuinely can't hold our value AND we have the appetite.
```

## Diagnose a multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona positioning problem

```
Our situation:
- Products: {{list}}
- Segments: {{list}}
- Personas: {{list}}

Currently our positioning is trying to do all of the above simultaneously and it's stretching thin.

Apply Dunford's guide (Substack: "A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas"):
1. Multi-product: which of the 3 options fits — umbrella positioning, lead-product model, or product family/platform? Why?
2. Multi-segment: are the differentiated capabilities consistent across segments? If yes, one positioning + tailored content per segment. If no, we may need separate positioning per segment.
3. Multi-persona: name the champion (the internal deal advocate). Position for them. Enumerate the other stakeholders and handle them through objection handling, not separate positioning.
4. Warn me if I'm trying to solve a positioning problem that's really a portfolio problem (too many products for one company to position coherently).
```

## Reposition after a pivot / acquisition / major market shift

```
We're repositioning because {{pivot / acquisition / new leadership / AI disruption / market consolidation}}.

Apply Dunford's guidance from "How to Manage a Big Positioning Change":
1. CEO sponsorship — is our CEO actually going to own this, or is this a marketing project?
2. Sales in the room — are we running the exercise with sales leadership + experienced AEs? (If no, stop; it won't stick.)
3. For acquisitions specifically: don't extend the acquirer's positioning; run a fresh combined-positioning exercise for the merged entity.
4. Change management: treat the rollout like a product launch internally. Talk track, training, collateral, metrics on adoption.
5. Regular cadence: after this repositioning, schedule the next 6-month check-in.
6. Reference "When To Revisit Your Positioning" if the trigger was symptom-based.
```

## Positioning in the age of AI

```
We're a {{product}} in {{category}}. AI-native competitors are emerging. We need to decide how to position.

Apply Dunford's framing from "Positioning in the Age of AI" (Substack, May 2026) and "In the Age of AI, You Need a Point of View" (Jun 2026):
1. Are we positioning against today's shortlist or against a future imagined competitor? (Position against today's shortlist; the roadmap handles the future.)
2. Are we "smoking their marketing" — assuming competitors deliver what their homepage claims, rather than what they actually deliver in deals?
3. Is AI our differentiated value, or a trend we should layer only if it makes value more obvious?
4. What's our specific point of view on how the market is changing? In an AI-flooded market where every homepage sounds the same, POV is what earns attention.
5. Watch for the trap of leading with "AI-powered" when the AI isn't the differentiator.
```
