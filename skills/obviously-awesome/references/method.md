# Obviously Awesome — Method

> The canonical description of Dunford's method in her own terms. Fidelity is the point — the framework is opinionated, and softening any of the components ("competitors" instead of "competitive alternatives", "value" instead of "differentiated value") collapses the method into generic marketing language.

## Definition

Dunford's operating definition:

> "Positioning is the act of deliberately defining how you are the best in the world at delivering something a well-defined set of customers deeply cares about."
> — April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019)

Companion framing she uses in almost every talk:

> "Positioning is like the opening scene of a movie — it sets the context."

**What "positioning" is NOT** (she attacks these constantly — see `voice-and-tone.md` and `heuristics.md`):

- NOT a tagline or a positioning statement (Mad-Libs templates are "not only pointless but potentially dangerous")
- NOT branding (branding is downstream)
- NOT messaging (messaging is the tactical expression of positioning)
- NOT a mission statement
- NOT what you say about yourself in a vacuum ("positioning is what puts you in a competitive frame")

## The 5 Components of Effective Positioning

Answered as a set. A change to one forces changes to the others.

### 1. Competitive alternatives

**What it asks:** What would our best-fit customers do if we didn't exist?

**The trap it kills:** thinking "competitors" means "vendors we've heard of". Real competitive alternatives are the *shortlist a real buyer is actually comparing you against*, which usually includes:

- Direct competing products
- **The status quo** — doing nothing / keep suffering
- **DIY** — build it internally, hire an intern, cobble together spreadsheets
- **Adjacent categories** — a different-shaped product that solves the same problem
- **Ecosystem workarounds** — Zapier + 3 other tools

**Author's words:**
> "They are not a true alternative if they don't show up on a shortlist." — *The "No Differentiation" Illusion*, Substack, Sep 2024

**Diagnostic question:** "What would customers do if we didn't exist?" If the honest answer is *"nothing, they'd keep suffering"*, the status quo is your #1 competitor. Most B2B pitches ignore this — and lose to it. 40–60% of B2B deals end in **"no decision"** (her anchor stat) — that's the status quo winning.

### 2. Unique attributes

**What it asks:** What features / capabilities / structural facts do we have that the competitive alternatives do not?

**Sources of uniqueness are not only product features.** Dunford is explicit — from *The "No Differentiation" Illusion*:

- **Product features / capabilities** (obvious)
- **Pricing model** (usage-based when everyone else is per-seat)
- **Partnership ecosystems** (integrated where competitors are standalone)
- **Distribution** (direct where competitors are channel-only, or vice versa)
- **Wrapped services** (managed vs. self-serve)
- **Data / trained model** (proprietary corpus, exclusive telemetry)
- **Company structure** (mutual ownership, non-profit, benefit corp)

**Anti-move:** listing 40 features. You want the attributes that, when compared to what competitors *actually* offer, are honestly unique. If a competitor could tomorrow list the same attribute truthfully, it isn't unique.

### 3. Value themes

**What it asks:** So what? What business outcome does each unique attribute enable a best-fit customer to achieve?

**Structure:** cluster the unique attributes into **1–4 value themes** — no more. Each theme is written in the buyer's outcome vocabulary, not the product's feature vocabulary.

**The "so what?" bridge.** Features aren't value. A feature is a fact about the product; a value theme is what that fact lets the customer *do differently* in their business. Translate every attribute across the bridge:

- Feature: "single-tenant deployment"
- Value: "meet SOC 2 without an architecture review from your security team"

**Author's words:**
> "You have to translate to value the buyer actually cares about." — Substack essays, repeated framing

**Cap it at 1–4.** More than that and the pitch collapses — the buyer can't hold 7 things in working memory, sales can't pitch it consistently, and the differentiation dilutes.

### 4. Best-fit customers

**What it asks:** Who is the customer segment that values the differentiation most — i.e., where does the value theme land hardest?

**Not "target market" in the demographic sense.** Best-fit is defined by **who cares about what makes you different**. Look at:

- Who buys fastest?
- Who has the highest NPS / lowest churn?
- Who expands the most?
- Whose competing alternatives you *actually* beat consistently?

If your differentiation matters most to Fortune 500 CISOs, that's your best-fit — even if your website copy speaks to "any team that wants better security".

**Multi-segment nuance:** if the differentiated capabilities are consistent across segments, one positioning suffices — you tailor content and customer examples per segment, not the positioning itself. See "Multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona" below.

### 5. Market category (frame of reference)

**What it asks:** What market do we tell customers we're in — the one where our value becomes obvious?

**This is the highest-leverage lever in the whole framework.** Change nothing about the product, change the category — and win-rate / deal size can transform (see Janna Systems in `examples.md`).

**Rules Dunford enforces:**

- **Pick a category customers already understand** unless you have overwhelming reason to invent one. From her Quickstart Guide: 90% of recent public tech companies won in *existing* categories, not new ones. Google (search) beat Ask Jeeves (Q&A category). Facebook (social network) beat Myspace.
- **The category defines the alternatives.** Position as "CRM" → you're compared to Salesforce. Position as "CRM for investment banks" → you're compared to industry-specific point solutions and Excel.
- **Category-frame test:** does saying the category out loud make your value obvious to a best-fit buyer within 30 seconds? If not, wrong category.
- **The chosen category has to be defensible in a sales conversation.** If reps have to argue about the category before they can pitch the product, the category is wrong.

**Author's words:**
> "Weak positioning is usually the result of hanging on to a 'default' market position that is rooted in the history of the product idea." — *Obviously Awesome*

### Optional 6th layer: Trend

Layer a relevant trend on top (AI, remote-first, sustainability, agentic workflows) **only if it makes your value more obvious to the target**.

**Dunford's warning:** don't lead with the trend — the trend is a spice, not the meal. Teams positioning around trends without a real value differentiation get exposed when the trend cools. From her *Positioning in the Age of AI* essay (May 2026): *"Ignore your future competition in your positioning, but never in your roadmap."*

## The 5-step exercise (2026 restructure)

The 2019 book had 10 process steps. The 2026 2nd edition restructures to **5 steps**, matching the 5 components — "the first few steps were really pre-work for a positioning exercise, and the final steps described work that needed to happen after the exercise" (Substack announcement, Jan 12, 2026).

**The 5-step positioning exercise:**

1. **Let go of where you came from** — release the default position that lives in your product's origin story. This is the step teams skip and pay for.
2. **Isolate your uniqueness** — list unique attributes across product, pricing, ecosystem, distribution, wrapped services, data.
3. **Map attributes to value themes** — cluster into 1–4 outcome-vocabulary themes.
4. **Who cares?** — identify the best-fit customer segment where the value lands hardest.
5. **Choose your best market frame of reference** — the category where your value becomes obvious.

**Optional step 6:** layer a trend if it makes the value more obvious.

**Pre-work (before you convene the workshop)** — added explicitly in the 2026 edition:
- Decide what's being positioned (company, product, product line — see multi-product below)
- Decide the audience (which best-fit segment; if multiple, position for the primary)
- Gather evidence: recent win/loss data, sales-call recordings (Gong), customer interviews with recent wins and losses, competitive research from real deals, current messaging inventory
- Convene cross-functional team: founder/CEO (must sponsor), marketing head, sales leadership + 1–2 experienced AEs, product management, customer success

**Post-work (after the exercise):**
- Build the sales pitch (see 8-step Sales Pitch below) — sales in the room, or it won't stick
- Translate to messaging: homepage, email sequences, sales collateral, event booth copy
- Instrument tests via live sales conversations
- Schedule a repositioning check-in every 6 months

## The 8-step Sales Pitch

From *Sales Pitch* (2023) and the Lenny Rachitsky episode (Oct 22, 2023). This is the operational extension of positioning into the actual buyer conversation.

**Core thesis:** the enemy isn't competitors, it's **"no decision"** — 40–60% of B2B deals end there. Buyers "are more worried about messing up than they are about missing out." The pitch's job is to help buyers make confident purchase decisions, not to push product.

**Two halves: Setup + Follow-Through.** Setup gets aligned on the market first, before you talk about your product. Follow-Through introduces the product against that aligned frame.

### SETUP

**1. Insight — "The thing we understand about the market that the others do not."**

The hardest and most crucial step. Ask: *"What do best-fit prospects need to know to understand why our differentiated value is important?"* This is your **point of view** on the market. Not a sales line — a genuine claim about how the market or the buyer's job is changing that grounds everything after.

**2. Alternatives — grouped by approach, not vendor names.**

List the approaches a buyer could take. Always include: **status quo, DIY, spreadsheets, doing nothing**. Present pros/cons of each approach objectively.

**Warning:** don't introduce approaches the buyer hasn't already considered — that stalls the deal further, adding new evaluation branches instead of narrowing the shortlist.

**3. The Perfect World — the criteria a buyer sharing your worldview would use.**

If the buyer accepted your Insight from step 1, what would their ideal purchase criteria be? This is where you make explicit the ideal shape of a solution — before you name your product. The buyer nods along, and now the frame is set.

### FOLLOW-THROUGH

**4. Introduction — name the product and market category.**

*"We're [product name], a [market category]."* Short. This is where the positioning work pays off — the market category has to make the value obvious.

**5. Differentiated Value — the value only you can deliver.**

Features that are BOTH unique AND valuable, grouped into the 1–4 value themes from the positioning exercise. Each theme opens with the outcome, then evidence.

**6. Proof — matched to prospect's industry/size.**

Case studies, testimonials, quantified customer results. Not generic logos — targeted proof that this specific value theme lands for buyers who look like the prospect.

**7. Objections (optional) — address unspoken concerns proactively.**

If there's a known concern (price, migration cost, missing feature) that will surface in later stakeholder conversations, name and defuse it here rather than let the champion have to defend you alone.

**8. The Ask — explicit next step.**

Not "let us know what you think". A concrete proposal for the next step, sized to where the deal is.

### Why other common pitch shapes fail

From the Lenny summary + *Sales Pitch* book:

- **Walkthrough pitch** — assumes buyer knowledge, kills discovery, wastes the meeting.
- **Problem/solution pitch** — competitors define the same problem identically; you erase your own differentiation by starting there.
- **Vision narrative** — visions aren't proven; multiple "new ways" exist; buyer can't tell yours apart.
- **Hero's journey** — engaging as a talk but doesn't help the buyer decide.

### Rollout test

Per *Sales Pitch* — the pitch is ready when:
1. Reps stop requesting changes.
2. Reps prefer the new pitch to their old deck.

If reps quietly go back to their own decks after the launch, positioning didn't stick — usually because sales wasn't in the room.

## Multi-product / multi-segment / multi-persona

From the Substack essay *A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas*.

### Single product stage — don't split

If you have one product, don't split company vs. product positioning. Consolidate. Two positioning frames confuse customers and split the team's attention.

### Multi-product — three options

Pick one based on how the products relate:

- **(a) Umbrella positioning** — one company-level frame covers all products (works when products share the same value themes for the same best-fit segment).
- **(b) Lead-product model** — position the primary product; secondary products assume the buyer already owns the primary (works when there's a clear anchor).
- **(c) Product family / platform** — each product has its own positioning under a platform-level frame (works at scale when products serve different jobs).

### Multiple segments

One positioning suffices IF the differentiated capabilities are consistent across segments. Tailor **content and customer examples** per segment (case studies, industry-specific messaging) — not the positioning itself.

### Multiple personas — the champion rule

Position for the **champion** — the internal deal advocate. Handle other stakeholders (CFO, IT, procurement, end user) through **objection handling**, not through separate positioning.

**Author's words:**
> "You position for the champion. Everyone else gets handled through objections." — *A Guide to Positioning with Multiple Products, Segments and Personas*

### When to revisit positioning

From *When To Revisit Your Positioning* (Substack, Sep 2024):

- Symptoms (see `heuristics.md` for full list): new prospects don't get it, sales needs an extra call to explain, customers compare you to the wrong alternatives, functions describe you differently, losing to no decision more than to competitors.
- Market shifts: new entrants, tech disruption (AI is the current forcing function), category consolidation.
- Regular cadence: check-in a couple times a year even without symptoms.
- Big triggers: acquisition (needs a fresh combined-positioning exercise), pivot, new leadership.
