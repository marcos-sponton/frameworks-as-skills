## Twitter — early Floodgate bet, movement of early believers

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), Lenny's podcast (2024), *Starting Greatness* episodes, essays.

**The situation:** Maples was one of the earliest investors in Twitter via Floodgate. At the time, the pitch sounded like SMS-status updates — pattern-matchers dismissed it as trivial.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection:** smartphones + always-on mobile connectivity → real-time public microcasting became newly possible.
- **Insight:** people would broadcast to strangers in short-form public messages, and this would create a new form of public discourse (non-consensus at the time — dismissed as "what I had for lunch").
- **Idea:** the specific product.
- **Movement:** small group of early believers (tech-Twitter, journalists) who tolerated rough product and evangelized.

**Why the case is instructive:** the pitch was dismissed as trivial by pattern-matching investors. Non-consensus at the time; obvious in hindsight. Illustrates why "sounds crazy now" is often signal.

## Twitch (ex-Justin.tv) — the pivot as insight-discovery

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), Lenny's podcast (2024), *Starting Greatness* episodes.

**The situation:** Justin.tv originally attempted mainstream personal livestreaming ("I'll stream my life"). Timing was wrong; mainstream audiences weren't ready. The founders pivoted to game-streaming and became Twitch.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection:** broadband quality reached the threshold for watchable live video streaming *and* gaming culture reached mainstream mass — converging inflections.
- **Insight:** people would watch other people play video games. Non-consensus at the time ("who would watch that?"), right.
- **Idea:** the product.
- **Movement:** early gaming communities who evangelized the platform.

**Why the case is instructive:** two lessons. (1) The pivot was not "iterate the product until it works" — it was rediscovering the *insight* on a different segment with a different timing. (2) Right about the future (streaming), wrong about the timing and segment at first. Goldilocks moment matters.

## Lyft — GPS inflection + movement identity

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), Lenny's podcast (2024), *Starting Greatness*.

**The situation:** Lyft (and Uber) rode the GPS-in-phone inflection into peer-to-peer transportation. Lyft distinguished itself with the pink mustache — a movement/identity signal.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection:** iPhone 4s shipped with a GPS chip precise to ~1 meter → an app could locate people in real time for coordinated pickup. This capability didn't exist before.
- **Insight:** strangers would ride in strangers' personal cars, mediated by a phone app. Non-consensus at the time.
- **Idea:** the app + driver network.
- **Movement:** the pink mustache signaled community identity — drivers and riders were part of something, not just using a service.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetypal inflection story (specific moment, specific mechanism, specific new capability) *and* archetypal movement story (identity, not just transaction).

**Quotable moment (paraphrased):**
> "The GPS chip in the iPhone 4s allowed somebody to build an app that could locate people within one meter accuracy with an algorithm. That's an inflection — before that, you couldn't."

## Okta — SaaS wave, identity as new layer

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), Floodgate portfolio essays.

**The situation:** Okta (Todd McKinnon and Frederic Kerrest) rode the SaaS wave with an identity-management insight — that as enterprises adopted many SaaS products, identity would need to be a unified layer stitching them together.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection:** SaaS proliferation (Salesforce, Workday, and dozens more) + browser as universal client → enterprise identity fragmentation became a genuine problem.
- **Insight:** identity would become the new "layer" stitching SaaS applications together — a category that hadn't existed as a standalone product before.
- **Idea:** Okta's identity-as-a-service product.

**Why the case is instructive:** the insight was "plausible" (in Maples's backcasting taxonomy: non-obvious but logical). And the inflection (SaaS proliferation) is specific — not "the cloud is big" but *"as enterprises accumulate dozens of SaaS products, identity becomes a distinct layer."*

## Airbnb — trust-via-social-proof insight

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), various podcast appearances.

**The situation:** Airbnb's insight was that people would rent strangers' homes if trust could be established via social proof and reviews. Multiple observers dismissed it initially.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection:** Facebook Connect (identity via social graph) + smartphone GPS (coordination) → trust between strangers became newly possible at internet scale.
- **Insight:** people would trust a stranger's home based on reviews and social proof, and would prefer the experience to a hotel for many trips. Non-consensus, right.
- **Idea:** the marketplace.
- **Movement:** early hosts and guests who evangelized as much for the experience of connection as for the price.

**Why the case is instructive:** Four Seasons (the incumbent) couldn't replicate — the model was structurally incompatible with their operations. Also: the founders were *living in the future* (they rented out their own apartment first to make rent).

## Chegg — Textbookflix Implementation Prototype

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), *Starting Greatness*.

**The situation:** Chegg, facing cash depletion and Facebook eating their social-network business, needed a new insight fast. They tested textbook rental via a fake checkout page — Textbookflix — that "crashed" at checkout. They never fulfilled a single order; the goal was to measure willingness-to-pay at randomized prices ($35–$75) — to measure *desperation, not interest*.

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- The **Implementation Prototype** is a discovery tool, not an MVP. It's designed to reveal which segments feel *desperation* and which benefits matter most.
- Randomizing price surfaced the willingness-to-pay curve.
- The fake checkout was cheaper than actually building fulfillment — and the answer was clearer.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetypal illustration of the difference between measuring interest (MVP) and measuring desperation (Implementation Prototype). Also: the discovery unlocked investor funding and turned Chegg into a public company.

## Marc Andreessen at NCSA — "living in the future" archetype

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), Starting Greatness (Andreessen interview episode).

**The situation:** As a network administrator at NCSA (National Center for Supercomputing Applications), Andreessen used and built web browsers before they existed as consumer products. He was already living in a future where the browser was the primary interface.

**How Maples reads the case:**
- Andreessen's insight (that everyone would use browsers) wasn't extrapolated from the present — it was earned from immersion in the future scenario.
- This is the archetype of the **"living in the future"** move.

**Why the case is instructive:** insights come from immersion, not surveys. The story is a canonical answer to "how do I find a breakthrough insight?"

## Wright Brothers — outsider with earned expertise

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), essays.

**The situation:** The Wright Brothers were bicycle mechanics, not credentialed physicists or aeronautical engineers. Yet they cracked powered flight before the credentialed field did — because their hands-on expertise in bicycle mechanics (balance, control, feedback loops) transferred to flight in ways the credentialed approach missed.

**How Maples reads the case:**
- Outsider status was an asset, not a handicap. They hadn't been trained inside the failed pattern.
- Their expertise in an adjacent domain (bicycles) turned out to be the right earned expertise for the new problem (flight).

**Why the case is instructive:** the credentialed field's approach was pattern-matching against known aeronautics; the Wright Brothers were pattern-breaking. It's Maples's favorite historical archetype and he opens many talks with it.

## Tesla / SpaceX — Musk as "preposterous" future-envisioner

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), various essays.

**The situation:** Musk's early bets (Tesla making viable EVs, SpaceX reusable rockets) were dismissed as "preposterous" — Maples's third category of imagined futures in the backcasting process (plausible / possible / preposterous).

**How Maples reads the cascade:**
- **Inflection (Tesla):** lithium-ion battery cost curve + regulatory shifts on emissions + software-defined vehicles → EVs at scale became newly possible.
- **Insight:** consumers would prefer premium EVs to premium gasoline cars *if* the range, performance, and charging infrastructure crossed thresholds. Non-consensus at the time (auto industry believed EVs were a compliance product).
- **Movement:** Tesla's mission was framed as sustainable-energy adoption, not out-competing OEMs. Believers bought Model S in 2013 with a thin charging network.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows the "won't" moat (legacy OEMs *could* have built EVs but wouldn't cannibalize combustion margins) and shows movement-as-mission. Also: Musk operates in the "preposterous" future category as a matter of practice — a stance the pattern-matching investor world resists.

## Stripe — demo as argument (8 lines of Ruby)

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024), essays on Heresy.

**The situation:** Stripe replaced weeks of merchant-account paperwork with 8 lines of Ruby. The demo made the difference feel obvious in 30 seconds — you couldn't argue against the delta.

**How Maples reads the case:**
- **Inflection:** developer-first infrastructure adoption in the 2010s — engineers, not procurement, chose tools.
- **Insight:** payments could be a developer tool, not a compliance product. Non-consensus at the time.
- **Demo:** 8 lines of Ruby — the argument didn't need to be made in a deck.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetypal "Demo" answer to Maples's stress test (Refusal, Heresy, Inflection, Demo). If your product's advantage requires a deck to explain, you may not yet have a Demo-quality insight.

## Figma — demo as argument (live collaboration)

**Where Maples uses it:** *Pattern Breakers* (2024).

**The situation:** Figma showed live multiplayer collaboration on design files at a moment when the design tool orthodoxy was file-based single-user editing. The demo made the delta feel obvious.

**How Maples reads the case:**
- **Inflection:** browser capabilities (WebGL, WebSockets) reached a threshold where a design tool could run fully in-browser with real-time collaboration.
- **Insight:** design would move from a solo file-based practice to a collaborative browser-based one — same shift Google Docs made for writing.
- **Demo:** live collaboration in the browser — the argument was self-evident once you saw it.

**Why the case is instructive:** paired with Stripe as the "Demo" archetype. Also: Figma's insight sat next to Google Docs as an obvious analogy in hindsight — but for years, "design must be a file-based desktop app" was consensus.

## Spotify — access replacing ownership

**Where Maples uses it:** essays on Heresy.

**The situation:** Spotify (and streaming more broadly) replaced ownership with access. Music consumers moved from "own an album" to "have access to all albums."

**How Maples reads the case:**
- **Inflection:** broadband + smartphone → always-available streaming became newly possible.
- **Insight:** consumers would prefer access to ownership for music, given the trade-off. Non-consensus in the iTunes era.
- **Idea:** the streaming subscription.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows an insight about *behavior shift*, not just technology. The technology enabled it, but the insight was about how humans would relate to music.
