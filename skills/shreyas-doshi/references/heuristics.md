# Shreyas Doshi's PM Frameworks -- Heuristics

> Do's, don'ts, gotchas, pro tips, anti-patterns, common misapplications. Every item attributed to a specific source.

## Do's

### 1. Prefix your to-do list with L, N, or O every day
The classification itself changes behavior. Before you start working, look at every task and label it. You'll immediately notice how much energy you've been spending on O tasks.
*Source: LNO thread (Jun 2021)*

### 2. Run pre-mortems 1-3 months before launch
Not the week before. You need enough context to identify real risks AND enough time to address them. A pre-mortem the day before launch is a panic meeting, not a pre-mortem.
*Source: Pre-mortem template (Coda/Superhuman Docs)*

### 3. Ask "is this the best thing I could be doing?" not "is this worth doing?"
The second question accepts ROI logic -- almost everything is "worth doing." The first forces opportunity cost thinking and surfaces high-value alternatives.
*Source: Opportunity Cost thread (Jun 2021)*

### 4. Match your communication level to the stakeholder
C-suite wants Impact. Your engineering team wants Execution. Cross-functional partners often want Optics. Drowning executives in execution detail loses the audience. Giving engineers only vision without detail frustrates them.
*Source: Three Levels, Knowledge Project #175*

### 5. Stack rank customer problems holistically
Never discuss a customer problem in isolation. Ask them to rank it against everything else they face. The Focusing Illusion makes every problem seem important when you discuss it alone.
*Source: CPSR thread (May 2020)*

### 6. Create sticky vocabulary for risks
"I recently thought of a Tiger" is more natural and less pessimistic than "I have a concern about the project." The vocabulary does the teaching and makes risk-surfacing a normal part of team conversation.
*Source: Pre-mortem Medium essay*

### 7. Hire go-getters over frustrated geniuses for ambiguous roles
Agency beats raw talent when conditions are imperfect. A go-getter in an ambiguous role will find a way; a frustrated genius will blame the conditions.
*Source: High Agency thread (Jun 2020), Knowledge Project #175*

### 8. Point the truth inward before pointing it outward
Before using an insight to influence others, ask what it means about your own defaults. The wise person sees the Antithesis -- the inward application.
*Source: Antithesis Principle, Substack (Jul 2026)*

### 9. Investigate strategy before blaming execution
When a project is struggling, don't jump to "we have an execution problem." Ask: Is the goal clear? Do people agree on the path? Are structural blockers making even perfect execution fail?
*Source: Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2022)*

### 10. Create the follow-up document after a pre-mortem
The meeting is not the output. The action plan with verbatim threats, prioritized Tigers/Elephants, proposed mitigations, and DRIs is the output. Without it, the meeting was theater.
*Source: Pre-mortem template*

### 11. Listen to what is being said, not to prepare your response
> "When you listen just to what is being said, and you're fully present, the responses craft themselves." -- Shreyas Doshi, Amplitude blog
*Source: Amplitude conversation with John Cutler, Lenny's Podcast live episode (Oct 2024)*

### 12. Make hiring decisions based on stage and archetype fit
Early-stage drowning in execution debt needs an Operator. Weak product needs a Craftsperson. No clear direction needs a Visionary. Don't hire based on which archetype sounds most impressive.
*Source: Three Types thread (Mar 2021), 20 Product podcast*

## Don'ts

### 1. Don't perfectionism-spray across all tasks
Perfectionism on Overhead tasks is pure waste. Save your best effort for Leverage tasks. The PM who spends two hours formatting meeting notes (O task) and rushes through a product strategy review (L task) has inverted the framework.
*Source: LNO thread*

### 2. Don't run a standard pre-mortem without the Tiger/Paper Tiger/Elephant labels
Without the lexicon, people forget the meeting's output within days. The vocabulary creates ongoing psychological safety and makes risk-surfacing a natural part of team conversation, not a one-time event.
*Source: Pre-mortem Medium essay*

### 3. Don't confuse ROI with opportunity cost
A task with tiny cost and low value has infinite ROI but near-zero importance. ROI makes everything look worth doing. Opportunity cost forces you to choose the best thing.
*Source: Opportunity Cost thread*

### 4. Don't assume execution problems are execution problems
Investigate strategy clarity, team alignment, and incentive structures first. Most "execution problems" dissolve when you fix the underlying strategy or culture issue.
*Source: Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2022)*

### 5. Don't discuss a customer problem in isolation
The Focusing Illusion will make it seem more important than it is. Always contextualize against the customer's full problem landscape.
*Source: CPSR thread*

### 6. Don't chase humility as a performance
> "Chasing humility will surely keep you busy, but it is pointless." -- Shreyas Doshi, Substack
Public vulnerability "with a veneer of altruism" is ego in disguise. The real question: "Where did I change my mind this week because of someone else's perspective?"
*Source: The Humility Trap, Substack (Feb 2026)*

### 7. Don't worship "bias for action" uncritically
> "Speed does not equal velocity. If you speed in the wrong direction, it only takes you farther from where you want to be." -- Shreyas Doshi, 5 Big Ideas
Weigh total research costs against total build costs. For a large team on a risky build, 2 weeks of research can save 24 weeks of team effort.
*Source: 5 Big Ideas analysis*

### 8. Don't let combative PM-vs-PM dynamics run alignment
When two type-A PMs negotiate directly, organizational results suffer because each prioritizes winning the argument. Route critical alignment through EMs, TLs, or Designers who care about outcomes, not status.
> "The smarter the person, the better the excuse." -- Shreyas Doshi, Substack
*Source: Route Around Big Egos, Substack (Jan 2026)*

### 9. Don't use your debate skills to extract false buy-in
> "Would you rather have a successful product review meeting, or a successful product?" -- Shreyas Doshi
Charisma and persuasion can silence skepticism and create artificial alignment. Ask "What am I missing?" instead of "Do you agree?"
*Source: 5 Big Ideas analysis*

### 10. Don't identify so strongly with your PM archetype that you can't flex
Everyone has a preferred type (Operator/Craftsperson/Visionary), but great PMs move between modes. If you're a Craftsperson who refuses to do Operator work, you're limiting your impact.
*Source: Three Types thread*

## Gotchas

### LNO classifications shift weekly
A task that was Leverage last week can become Neutral this week as priorities evolve. The framework requires regular reassessment -- it's a daily practice, not a one-time exercise.
*Source: LNO thread, Dualoop blog analysis*

### Pre-mortems can become theater
If the meeting leader doesn't create the follow-up action plan with DRIs and verbatim threats, the meeting was performative. The pre-mortem's value is 100% in the follow-through.
*Source: Pre-mortem template*

### "High agency" is not "bulldoze everyone"
Shreyas explicitly separates resourcefulness from aggression. Agency is about finding a way despite imperfect conditions, not forcing your way through people. Agency without integrity is dangerous -- and Shreyas makes this non-negotiable, "not even for Game Changers."
*Source: High Agency thread*

### Product Sense sounds vague but Shreyas defines it precisely
Five components: empathy, simulation skills, strategic thinking, taste, creative execution. If someone claims "product sense" without being able to articulate these components, they're hand-waving. It's learnable, not innate.
*Source: Maven course, Substack essay (Mar 2026)*

### The Three Levels is NOT a hierarchy
Impact is not "better" than Execution is not "better" than Optics. They are different planes of operation. A PM who only operates at Impact level is ineffective. The framework is about matching levels to context, not climbing a ladder.
*Source: Three Levels thread, Amplitude blog*

### The Antithesis Principle requires genuine self-examination
It's easy to read the examples and nod along. The hard part is actually applying it to your own behavior: where are you using a truth as an outward tactic while ignoring its inward warning?
*Source: Antithesis Principle, Substack (Jul 2026)*

### Shreyas's frameworks assume empowered teams
> "Don't believe everything I write" and "Don't take everything and just implement it." -- Shreyas Doshi, Amplitude blog
His advice reflects environments where PMs have real authority and autonomy. In heavily constrained organizations (feature factories, command-and-control cultures), the frameworks still provide diagnostic value but may not translate directly to action.
*Source: Amplitude conversation with John Cutler*
