# Shreyas Doshi's PM Frameworks -- Examples

> Real cases Shreyas has shared publicly. He draws primarily on his own experience at Stripe, Twitter, Google, and Yahoo, plus patterns from coaching hundreds of PMs. His cases are practitioner-grounded, not Harvard Business School style.

---

## 1. Stripe pre-mortem culture (Pre-mortems)

**Source**: Lenny's Podcast (Jun 2022), Pre-mortem Coda/Superhuman template.

Shreyas introduced pre-mortems at Stripe, where they became embedded in the product culture. The Tigers/Paper Tigers/Elephants vocabulary stuck because team members could naturally reference risks in everyday conversation:

"I recently thought of a Tiger" became a normal thing to say in a hallway or Slack, which is far more natural and less pessimistic than "I have a concern about the project."

The key innovation wasn't the meeting itself (Klein's pre-mortem already existed) -- it was the vocabulary that extended the meeting's value into daily work. At Stripe, the pre-mortem was "a tiny time investment, with major benefits."

**What this illustrates**: The naming move is the pedagogical innovation. Sticky vocabulary > meeting format.

## 2. The PM who perfected meeting notes (LNO)

**Source**: LNO thread (Jun 2021), Lenny's Podcast.

Shreyas describes a common pattern: a PM who spends enormous energy formatting meeting notes, organizing the workspace, maintaining status updates, and ensuring every follow-up email is beautifully written. These are all Overhead tasks.

Meanwhile, the same PM rushes through their product strategy review (a Leverage task) because they've exhausted their best energy on O tasks. The impact review comes around and the results are disappointing.

The LNO diagnosis: they were applying Leverage-level perfectionism to Overhead tasks. "Perfectionism is welcome" for L tasks. "Done is better than perfect" for O tasks.

**What this illustrates**: The most common LNO failure is not doing too little -- it's doing too much of the wrong type. Perfectionism-spraying is the anti-pattern.

## 3. The B2B discovery pivot (CPSR / Focusing Illusion)

**Source**: B2B Product Management Story thread (Apr 2021, https://x.com/shreyas/status/1376033582305615873), OpinionX blog.

A team was building a solution for a specific B2B customer problem. When they applied the CPSR framework with 600 target customers, ranking 45 problem statements, their original focus problem ranked dead last.

But the top 7 ranked problems revealed five similar pain points worth pursuing. The team pivoted to address these problems. Results:
- Multiple paying customers within a week
- 3x improvement in landing page conversion
- Initial customer testimonials

**What this illustrates**: The Focusing Illusion is real and measurable. When you discuss a single problem in isolation, customers validate it. When you force a stack rank against everything else, the truth emerges. This isn't a theoretical risk -- it's a common cause of product failure.

## 4. Low Agency Bob vs High Agency Alice (High Agency)

**Source**: High Agency thread (Jun 2020, https://x.com/shreyas/status/1276956836856393728).

A recurring teaching device contrasting two personas with identical talent but opposite agency:

**Low Agency Bob** externalizes problems: "That's someone else's responsibility." "The conditions aren't right." "I can't do anything about that." He waits for perfect conditions, blames circumstances, and eventually capitulates to systemic pressures.

**High Agency Alice** focuses on controllable factors: "What can I do given these constraints?" "How do I make progress despite this?" She finds a way to get what she wants without waiting for conditions to be perfect.

Same talent. Radically different impact. Over a career, Alice becomes a Game Changer while Bob remains a Frustrated Genius.

**What this illustrates**: Agency is not about intelligence or capability -- it's about the relationship with circumstances. The same talent deployed with high agency produces 10x the results.

## 5. The product review gone wrong (Three Levels)

**Source**: Knowledge Project #175.

A PM comes into an executive product review loaded with execution-level detail: sprint velocity, bug counts, feature completion percentages, technical architecture decisions.

The executives want Impact-level discussion: What does this mean for the business? How does this affect our market position? What's the customer impact?

Neither side understands the mismatch. The PM thinks the executives "don't care about the details." The executives think the PM "doesn't understand the business."

**Shreyas's diagnosis:**
> "Oftentimes conflict between people and teams arises mainly because we're not talking at the same level."

The fix: match your communication level to your audience. C-suite wants Impact. Engineering wants Execution. Cross-functional partners often want Optics.

**What this illustrates**: Most team conflicts are level mismatches, not personality conflicts or competence issues. Diagnosing the level unlocks the resolution.

## 6. Two combative PMs (Route Around Big Egos)

**Source**: Route Around Big Egos, Substack (Jan 2026).

Two type-A PMs from different teams need to align on a cross-functional project. Each is talented, each has strong opinions, and each enters the negotiation determined to win.

The result: meetings devolve into status competitions. Both PMs care about outcomes -- but they care more about not losing the argument. Progress stalls.

Shreyas's solution: route the alignment through EMs, TLs, or Designers who care less about inter-PM status and more about shipping the right thing. This requires operating "behind-the-scenes" and may deviate from standard inter-team processes, but produces superior outcomes.

> "The smarter the person, the better the excuse" for why they're not the problem.

**What this illustrates**: Sometimes the highest-leverage PM move is removing yourself from the negotiation and letting someone without ego-stake handle the alignment.

## 7. The frustrated genius on an ambiguous project (High Agency + Hiring)

**Source**: Knowledge Project #175.

Shreyas describes a pattern from coaching: a PM with exceptional analytical talent is placed on a highly ambiguous project -- new market, unclear requirements, no existing playbook. The PM freezes. They know how to optimize an existing product but don't know how to navigate ambiguity without clear direction.

Meanwhile, a less-talented but high-agency Go-Getter on a similar project figures it out: talks to 30 customers in a week, ships a prototype, learns from the failure, iterates.

Shreyas's hiring lesson: "Given a choice between hiring a frustrated genius and hiring a go-getter, if it is a role that has a high degree of ambiguity, I would rather hire the go-getter than the frustrated genius."

**What this illustrates**: For ambiguous roles, agency is more predictive of success than talent. Match the person to the role's ambiguity level.

## 8. The dashboard nobody checks (Amplitude conversation)

**Source**: Amplitude blog conversation with John Cutler.

A team builds a beautiful product metrics dashboard. The dashboard is analytically rigorous, covers every KPI, and took weeks to build. Six months later, nobody checks it regularly.

Shreyas's diagnosis: the team built a dashboard but not a dashboard product. They didn't track usage, didn't reduce friction to access it, and didn't create rituals around it.

His prescription: treat metrics dashboards as products. Specify expected metrics upfront in PRDs. Monitor who actually views dashboards. Reduce friction (pin browser tabs, send email summaries). Measure engagement with the measurement tool itself.

**What this illustrates**: Product thinking applies to internal tools as much as external products. If nobody uses your dashboard, the dashboard failed as a product.

## 9. The "successful product review meeting" trap (Focusing Illusion + Persuasion)

**Source**: 5 Big Ideas analysis.

A PM with exceptional debate skills walks into a product review. They present their strategy with charisma and conviction. Every stakeholder nods. The review is declared a success.

Six months later, the product fails. In retrospect, the PM's persuasion silenced skepticism. Doubters stayed quiet because disagreeing felt socially costly. The PM extracted false buy-in.

Shreyas's reframe:
> "Would you rather have a successful product review meeting, or a successful product?"

His antidotes:
- Ask "What am I missing?" instead of "Do you agree?"
- Document ideas for asynchronous feedback (reduces social pressure)
- Prioritize market validation over meeting success

**What this illustrates**: Debate skill is a PM superpower that can backfire. The same charisma that aligns a room can also suppress the dissent you need to hear.

## 10. The "bias for action" backfire (Speed vs Velocity)

**Source**: 5 Big Ideas analysis.

A large team (20+ people) has a strong "bias for action" culture. They skip a 2-week research phase and jump straight to building. The build takes 12 weeks. At launch, they discover they built the wrong thing.

Total cost: 12 weeks x 20 people = 240 person-weeks wasted.

If they'd done 2 weeks of research with 2 people first, worst case: 4 person-weeks invested. Best case: they discover the problem early and redirect the 240 person-weeks to the right build.

Shreyas's principle:
> "Speed does not equal velocity. If you speed in the wrong direction, it only takes you farther from where you want to be."

**When bias for action is right**: Small team, lightweight MVP, learning-by-doing is faster than desk research.
**When bias for action is wrong**: Large team, high build cost, research can prevent expensive mistakes.

**What this illustrates**: "Bias for action" is not always a virtue. The right heuristic depends on team size and build cost. Weigh total research costs against total build costs.
