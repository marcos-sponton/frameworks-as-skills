## W.W. Grainger — Challenger in operation

**Where Dixon uses it:** *The Challenger Sale* (2011), later training material and appearances.

**The situation:** W.W. Grainger is a large MRO (maintenance, repair, and operations) distributor. Historically, MRO reps sold on the "we have everything you need in stock" pitch — commodity distribution, price competition, features fight.

**How Challenger changed the pitch:**
- Instead of "here's our catalog," Grainger reps were taught to walk into a customer's operation and *reframe* what MRO cost the customer.
- The Commercial Insight: MRO is not a line item — it's an operational risk cost. Every time a critical part is out of stock, the customer loses production time worth 100x the part's price. Most companies systematically under-account for this.
- This insight leads uniquely to Grainger's differentiator: the depth-of-stock + rapid-fulfillment operation.

**Why the case is instructive:** the Teach didn't start with the product. It started with a *reframe of how the customer measures cost*. It passed all four of Dixon's Commercial Insight tests. And it made every subsequent feature conversation Grainger-favorable, because the buyer was now measuring on Grainger's terms.

## ADP — Teach that leads uniquely to your differentiator

**Where Dixon uses it:** *The Challenger Sale* (2011).

**The situation:** ADP (payroll and HR services) reps competed against buyers' default assumption that in-house payroll was cheap.

**The Commercial Insight:** *the true cost of in-house payroll is systematically hidden.* When you account for management time on compliance, error correction, turnover risk when the person running payroll leaves, and audit exposure — the "cheap" in-house option is significantly more expensive than it looks. And this cost is invisible to the buyer's current accounting.

The insight passed all four tests. It reframed the buyer's understanding of their own business. And critically, it led uniquely to ADP's differentiator (managed service at scale) — competitors couldn't use the same insight without pointing to a full-managed model.

## The Skeptic-first move — synthetic example

**Source:** *The Challenger Customer* (2015) research + Dixon's post-book appearances.

**The situation:** you're selling a $500k enterprise platform into a 7-person buying committee at a mid-cap company. You've been getting time with two Go-Getters (VP of Operations, Director of Analytics) who love the product. The deal has been "moving" for six weeks. There's one committee member — the VP of IT — who's been polite in meetings but keeps sending detailed technical objections by email.

**The default (Talker-focused) move:** keep working with the Go-Getters. They love it, they'll bring the IT VP along. Assume the IT VP is a Blocker or a footnote.

**The Skeptic-first move:** the IT VP is the Skeptic — not a Blocker. Skeptics look like blockers because they push back hard early. But once won over, their credibility with peers is unmatched.

**What to do:**
- Take the IT VP's objections seriously and answer them substantively — even the ones that are inconvenient
- Bring the disconfirming data yourself (competitor comparisons, real deployment horror stories from other customers, honest year-1 cook's tour)
- Do not overpromise — the Skeptic hears overpromising as evidence you don't understand the risk
- If they concede a point, do NOT close on it immediately — let them integrate

**Why it works:** when the IT VP eventually stops pushing back, the Go-Getters and the CFO trust their conversion more than they trust the initial enthusiasm. The deal that would have died in committee vote survives it.

## The Talker-in-costume — synthetic diagnostic

**Source:** synthesis of Challenger Customer research patterns.

**The situation:** your "champion" is a Director of Marketing who has been advocating for your product internally for four months. She reports positive internal conversations. She's asked for multiple assets to "share with the team." Meetings you've asked her to schedule with peers keep getting "pushed to next quarter."

**Diagnostic questions:**

- Ask directly: *"Who else needs to be sold on this internally, and what's the best way I can help you sell them?"*

  - Mobilizer answer: names the CFO by name, describes the specific budget cycle blocker, asks you for a one-pager framed for CFO priorities and offers a specific meeting slot in two weeks.
  - Talker answer: "Let me handle it internally — I know how this needs to land." Deflects, generalizes, buys herself time.

- Ask: *"What content that I've sent has surfaced in your internal conversations?"*

  - Mobilizer answer: names specific slides, specific quotes, specific reactions from named people.
  - Talker answer: "It's all been really helpful, they're processing it."

**What to do if you diagnose Talker:**
- Do not stop selling — the Talker is real and pleasant. But don't rely on her for the internal close.
- Ask the Talker who *else* in the org would find this most useful. If she names a Mobilizer, ask for a warm intro.
- Route around if necessary. Some sellers ask the Talker's manager if there's a broader deal-review conversation their team should be part of — sometimes the manager IS the Mobilizer.
- Bring Commercial Insight tailored to the Mobilizer, not to the Talker.

## JOLT diagnostic sequence — three faces walkthrough

**Source:** *The JOLT Effect* (2022) + [HBR June 2022](https://hbr.org/2022/06/stop-losing-sales-to-customer-indecision).

### Sequence 1: valuation-face indecision

**The situation:** enterprise buyer, 8-week discovery, keeps oscillating between the Team tier and the Enterprise tier of your platform. Asked to "compare the two" three times. Deal has stalled for the third week running.

**Judge:** ping — *"A lot of our customers at this stage get stuck comparing Team and Enterprise. What's your read on which fits your situation?"* Echo: "Honestly, we can't decide."

**Diagnosis:** valuation-face indecision. Too many options; low confidence in which fits.

**Offer:** *"Based on what you told me about your growth trajectory and the compliance requirements you mentioned in call three, if this were my best friend's business, I'd tell them Enterprise, because [specific reasons tied to their situation]. Let's take Team off the table."*

**Limit:** kill the tier comparison conversation entirely. Do not send a fourth comparison sheet. Move the agenda forward.

**Take:** offer a 90-day usage review — *"if at 90 days we're not using the Enterprise-only features, we downgrade you to Team at no penalty."* Reduces the "what if we over-bought" fear without discounting.

### Sequence 2: information-face indecision

**The situation:** SaaS mid-market buyer, has scheduled six demos, requested four customer references, asked for two additional case studies, and is asking for a technical deep-dive session. Their calendar for the next three weeks is full of "research."

**Judge:** ping — *"You've had a lot of good conversations already. What's still open that you haven't been able to answer with more research?"* Echo: long pause; then: "I guess I'm just trying to be thorough."

**Diagnosis:** information-face indecision. Endless discovery is a defense mechanism.

**Offer:** *"Here's what I'd recommend: skip the fourth reference and the second case study. I've read them; here's the two-sentence summary of what you'd learn. The signal-to-noise on more of these is low. What you actually need is one more conversation with the CFO to align on budget timeline — that's the only open item that would change the decision."*

**Limit:** cancel three of the six planned demos. Send a written summary of what would have been in them.

**Take:** offer a paid pilot with a clear exit ramp — *"you can spend 60 days getting real proof this fits, and if it doesn't, no annual commitment."* Reduces the "we missed something in due diligence" fear.

### Sequence 3: outcomes-face indecision

**The situation:** buyer is ready to sign the master agreement but keeps asking for additional protections — SLAs, penalty clauses, executive-level relationship commitments. The lawyers have been in the deal for six weeks.

**Judge:** ping — *"Something similar deals often get stuck on at this stage is fear that the platform won't hold up when your team is really pushing volume through it in Q4. Is that live for you?"* Echo: "Yeah, honestly, that's what my CEO keeps asking me."

**Diagnosis:** outcomes-face indecision. Fear the deal won't deliver.

**Offer:** *"Given that concern, here's what I'd recommend: start with a 90-day production pilot on your Q3 volume, with committed access to our implementation team and a written performance benchmark we'll hit or you're not obligated to renew."*

**Limit:** do not send another security whitepaper. Kill the low-value legal side-quests. Take the burden of Q3 performance onto your team, not their lawyers.

**Take:** *cook's-tour honesty*. Walk the CEO and the buyer through what year-1 actually looks like at another customer of similar shape — including the hard weeks and the things that broke. The honesty *reduces* fear more than another optimistic pitch would.

## The FOMU-beats-FOMO reframe — before/after

**Before (FOMO reflex):**

*Seller (email at end of quarter):* "Hi [Buyer], I wanted to let you know that our Q4 pricing expires this Friday. If you can get us signed by then, we can hold today's terms. Otherwise, we'd need to reprice in January, and there are new features rolling out that will affect the tier structure. Let me know if you want to hop on a call to make this happen."

*What this does to an indecisive buyer:* raises the cost of *choosing wrong* (locked into a decision that might be wrong) *and* the cost of *waiting* (bad price, worse features). But the buyer's fear was already fear-of-being-wrong. Now it's amplified. Deal goes silent for three weeks, then dies as no-decision.

**After (JOLT):**

*Seller (email at end of quarter):* "Hi [Buyer], quick note as we wrap Q4. I know from our last conversation you were still working through the Q3-versus-Q1 timing question with [named colleague]. If it would help, I can put together a one-pager comparing the two scenarios on total cost through your fiscal year — that would take me an hour, saves your team the analysis. And happy to talk through the honest downside of each — I don't think either is a bad choice. What would be most useful?"

*What this does to an indecisive buyer:* reduces cognitive load, takes work off their plate, signals the seller is not desperate, models the honesty that reduces outcome fear. Deal moves — maybe not this quarter, but with a real path.

## The Activator pattern — for founders and partners doing their own BD

**Source:** [HBR Nov-Dec 2023 — "What Today's Rainmakers Do Differently"](https://hbr.org/2023/11/what-todays-rainmakers-do-differently), *The Activator Advantage* (2025).

**The situation:** you're a founder or professional-services partner who has to sell your own services alongside delivering them. Time is scarce. The temptation is to sell only when the pipeline gets thin — reactive BD.

**The Activator move (research-backed):**

- **Consistent BD investment.** A small amount of BD activity every week beats large episodic pushes. Reactivity loses.
- **Warm network cultivation.** Actively maintain the relationships that could become referrers or clients — light touches, not just when you need something.
- **Proactive market shaping.** Publish, speak, teach — bring point of view into the market so buyers come to you already primed on your Commercial Insight.
- **Insight-first outreach.** When you do reach out, lead with a reframe of the recipient's world, not with what you sell.

**Why the case is instructive:** the Activator profile in professional services maps almost 1:1 to the Challenger in enterprise B2B. Same underlying principle — insight-first, proactive, willing to sit with tension — applied to a different sales context (doer-seller instead of AE).

**Corollary for founders:** the modern founder-CEO doing their own selling should not "become a Challenger." They should become an Activator, which is Challenger-behavior applied to a self-representing seller with limited BD time.
