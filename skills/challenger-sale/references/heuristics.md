# Challenger + JOLT — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Dixon's diagnostics, tests, and playbooks — that separate applying the method well from doing the version he spends most of his airtime critiquing. Attribution is precise: this comes from the 2011 book, this from the JOLT research, this from a 2024 Lenny appearance where he updated a position.

## Do's

### Run the no-decision test before you run the competitive post-mortem

The default lost-deal story ("we lost to competitor X") is wrong in the majority of complex-B2B losses. Ask the actual question first:

**Did the customer actually choose anyone?**

- If yes → competitive loss. Debrief on differentiation, pricing, Commercial Insight quality.
- If no / delayed / "we decided to wait" → **no-decision loss**. Run the JOLT diagnostic (see below). Do not accept the customer's stated reason at face value — most no-decision losses get rationalized as "budget", "timing", or "priorities" when the actual driver was indecision.

**Author's line (paraphrased):** *"40 to 60 percent of deals in the pipeline end up in no-decision. Most of them get logged in the CRM as competitive losses. The industry is misdiagnosing at scale."*

### Diagnose the profile before you coach the behavior

Don't coach every rep to "be more Challenger." Diagnose which of the five profiles they default to under pressure, then coach *toward* Challenger behaviors from where they start.

- **Relationship Builder → Challenger:** the biggest lift comes from teaching them to bring Commercial Insight and to sit with tension instead of resolving it. The rest of the T-T-T follows.
- **Hard Worker → Challenger:** they already have the willingness to make the tough call. What's missing is the *point of view* — invest in Commercial Insight training first.
- **Lone Wolf → Challenger:** the delicate one. Lone Wolves win but don't scale. Convert their instincts into repeatable T-T-T so the team can learn from them.
- **Reactive Problem Solver → Challenger:** teach them to lead conversations rather than respond to them. Insight-first, not question-first.

**Anti-move:** attempting a personality transplant. Coaching is behavioral, not identity.

### Identify the Mobilizer early — and prove they're not a Talker

The champion who *loves the product* is often a Talker. The Mobilizer is the person who **spends political capital**. The test:

**Ask directly:** *"Who else needs to be sold on this internally, and what's the best way I can help you sell them?"*

- **Mobilizer answer:** names names, describes specific internal blockers, asks you for specific content or a meeting.
- **Talker answer:** deflects ("let me handle it internally"), promises to "share it around", disappears for two weeks.

If the answer is Talker-shaped, do not stop selling — start looking for the Mobilizer elsewhere in the account. The Talker will feel good; the deal will not close.

### Bet on the Skeptic

Counter-intuitive Mobilizer play from *The Challenger Customer*: **the Skeptic is often the highest-leverage buyer to invest in**. Not the Go-Getter (easy but sometimes seen as naive), not the Teacher (persuasive but polarizing). The Skeptic asks the hardest questions early. Once won over, they carry the most weight in the buying group because their peers trust their due diligence.

**How to invest in the Skeptic:** engage the hard questions directly. Don't overpromise. Bring the disconfirming data yourself. Their trust compounds fast when they realize you're not selling them.

### Judge (J) before you Offer (O)

The JOLT sequence is not four parallel moves. It's sequential:
1. Diagnose which face of indecision is dominant (valuation / information / outcomes)
2. Prescribe O-L-T tailored to that face

**Wrong diagnosis → wrong prescription.** An outcomes-fear buyer treated with more information ("here's another case study") gets *more* indecisive. An information-fear buyer treated with a service guarantee ("we'll refund if it doesn't work") gets confused about why you're bringing up refunds when they were asking about implementation.

**Pings and echoes.** The Judge move: articulate a plausible worry, listen for the echo.

- *"A lot of our customers at this stage wonder whether the integration will hold up in Q4. How are you thinking about that?"* (probes outcomes)
- *"Some buyers in your seat get stuck comparing tier A and B. What's your read?"* (probes valuation)
- *"You've had a lot of conversations already. What are you still unsure about that you haven't been able to answer with more research?"* (probes information)

Never *"are you worried?"* — that invites a defensive no. Always *"here's what people worry about; here's what we do."*

### Sell like you'd sell to your best friend

The Offer move, articulated. If your best friend was buying this, you wouldn't hand them a spec sheet and wish them luck. You'd tell them what to buy and why, based on what you know about their situation.

**How to apply:** any time you hear yourself saying *"well, it depends on what you're looking for"* — stop. That phrase hands the indecision back to the customer, which is the *problem*. Replace with: *"Based on what you've told me about X, Y, Z, if this were my best friend, I'd tell them to pick this configuration, because these three things matter more than the rest."*

Great waiters don't ask what you want. They recommend.

### Take risk off the table — match the fear, not the price

Discounts don't reduce outcome fear. They make the price cheaper. If the customer is worried about *being wrong*, cheaper doesn't help — it may hurt (looks like desperation).

Match the take-risk-off move to the specific fear you diagnosed:

| Face of indecision | Take-risk-off move that fits |
|---|---|
| Outcomes fear ("what if it doesn't deliver?") | Pilots, phased rollouts, opt-outs, service guarantees tied to specific outcomes |
| Information fear ("what if I missed something?") | Deliver a written summary of *your* research on their vendor question; provide a one-page decision brief they can share internally |
| Valuation fear ("what if I pick the wrong tier?") | Opinionated recommendation + "let's take these off the table"; sometimes: bundle at a fixed price so tier choice disappears |

**The counter-intuitive move:** honesty about the downside builds more decision confidence than optimism about the upside. The vendor who names the risk gets trusted more than the one who insists there isn't one.

### Test Commercial Insight against the 4 questions

Before you take a "customer teaching" asset into a room, run Dixon's 4 tests:

1. **Would the customer pay for this insight itself, even if you sold nothing else?**
2. **Does it challenge how they currently think about their business** (not just add nuance)?
3. **Does it lead uniquely to your differentiators** (not just to "solving the problem")?
4. **Can it be taught and scaled** across the sales team without depending on one gifted rep?

If any fails, it's marketing content dressed as insight. Send it back to whoever wrote it.

### Tailor per stakeholder — never repeat one pitch across roles

The Challenger's Tailor move: same Commercial Insight, different framing for CFO vs. Head of Ops vs. IT vs. end user. Language changes, priorities change, KPIs change, incentives change.

**Test for tailoring:** if your slide deck is identical across the buying committee, you're not tailoring — you're broadcasting.

### Use JOLT proactively — don't wait for the stall

The mistake most sellers make: JOLT is deployed *after* the deal starts to slip. By then the indecision is anchored.

**Better:** ping for the three faces of indecision *by mid-cycle*, before they materialize. A well-timed *"here's what people usually worry about at this stage"* gets the anxiety on the table when there's still momentum to handle it.

## Don'ts

### Don't accept "we lost to competitor X" without checking

Cited above. The default post-mortem is wrong in most complex-B2B losses. Ask if the customer chose anyone. If not, JOLT is the diagnostic, not competitive analysis.

### Don't reach for FOMO / urgency / scarcity with a wavering buyer

Dialing up urgency backfires ~87% of the time with indecisive buyers (JOLT research). FOMU beats FOMO. The wavering buyer's fear is fear of *being wrong*, not fear of *missing out* — urgency amplifies the first, not the second.

**Author's line:** *"The industry has been telling reps the exact opposite of what the data shows."*

**Reframe when you catch yourself reaching for urgency:** the buyer isn't hesitating because they don't see the upside. They're hesitating because they can't afford to be wrong. Reduce the *cost of being wrong*, not increase the *cost of waiting*.

### Don't discount to close

Discounts don't address outcome fear. They read as desperation. They train the buyer to expect discounts on renewal. They don't move indecisive buyers materially.

**Replace with:** take-risk-off moves that match the diagnosed fear (see the T section).

Dixon exception: discounting for **timing** reasons (end of quarter, promotional pricing that was always going to happen) is different from discounting to *unstick* a stalled indecisive deal. The latter is the failure mode.

### Don't sell through Talkers

The champion who loves you but never schedules the CFO meeting is a Talker. Continuing to arm the Talker with more content is throwing good time after bad. Find the Mobilizer.

**Symptoms of selling-through-Talker:**
- Meetings are pleasant, cadence is fine, next steps keep getting rescheduled
- "Let me handle it internally" is the recurring answer to *"who else do we need to sell?"*
- Content you send them "for internal sharing" never surfaces in follow-up conversations

### Don't coach everyone to be a Challenger

The finding is about complex, multi-stakeholder, high-consideration B2B. In transactional / SMB, other profiles perform fine. Force-fitting Challenger to a transactional context is a coaching failure and demoralizes reps who don't need to change.

### Don't confuse "consultative" with "Challenger"

Consultative selling is often Relationship Builder in a costume. Both listen well; both build rapport; both bring "value." The delta: the Challenger brings a *point of view* (Commercial Insight) that reframes the customer's understanding. The consultative rep asks better questions and reflects. Not the same thing.

**Test:** did the customer leave the meeting thinking about their business differently, or did they leave feeling *heard*? Feeling-heard is Relationship Builder. Thinking-differently is Challenger.

### Don't Teach with content that hasn't been vetted against the 4 questions

Marketing decks that "add value" are not Commercial Insight unless they pass the tests. Deploying a bad Teach is worse than not Teaching — you burn the credibility to Teach later.

### Don't run JOLT on a buyer you haven't Taught

JOLT works when the buyer has a clear reason to buy something in your category *at all*. If Challenger hasn't done its work — no reframe, no Commercial Insight, no shift in how the customer sees their business — JOLT is running the closing sequence on a buyer who doesn't have the problem yet.

Sequence: Challenger earns the deal; JOLT closes the deal. Not the other way around.

### Don't confuse a Challenger who "controls the sale" with a Lone Wolf

Both are assertive. Both take control. The Lone Wolf pathology: takes control *at the customer's expense* (bulldozes, refuses to hear objections, closes hard). The Challenger: takes control *on the customer's behalf* (names objections the customer hasn't voiced, structures the process the customer needs).

If a rep is "closing hard" and the deal falls apart, ask which of the two profiles they're actually operating from.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### Diagnosing indecision correctly, prescribing wrong

Common pattern: seller correctly identifies "this deal is at risk of no-decision," then reaches for FOMO / urgency / discount anyway because JOLT feels too slow. The prescription reverts to the reflex. Discipline: once you've diagnosed indecision, *do not* reach for the tools that treat status-quo — they treat the opposite problem.

### The Mobilizer who quiets down mid-deal

Sometimes a real Mobilizer goes quiet — not because they lost interest, but because they hit an internal blocker they don't want to name. The mistake: assume they became a Talker. The move: reach out with a specific piece of Commercial Insight tailored to *the blocker you suspect*, without asking them to confirm it exists. If the blocker is real, they'll re-engage. If not, they'll tell you.

### Teaching insights that lead to a competitor's differentiator

Common failure of unvetted Commercial Insight: the reframe is genuinely useful but doesn't lead uniquely to *your* solution. You've educated the buyer to buy — from anyone. This is why test #3 exists.

### Running the 4 tests once and never again

Commercial Insight decays as the market absorbs it. What passed test #2 (challenging how they think) 18 months ago is now the industry consensus. Re-vet the Insight annually at minimum; more often in fast-moving categories.

### Overinvesting in Go-Getters, underinvesting in Skeptics

Go-Getters are easy — they're already convinced. The seller feels productive investing in them. The Skeptic is the harder win but the higher-leverage one. Reallocate.

### Confusing Take Control with rigid process

Take Control ≠ ram the deal through your process. Take Control = **you** set the process the customer's decision *actually needs*, based on what you've learned about them. If the buyer's actual process needs an unusual step (e.g., an early legal review), a Challenger surfaces and structures that step; a Lone Wolf tries to skip it because it's not "the way we do this."

### Running JOLT mechanically as J-O-L-T for every deal

JOLT is a diagnostic + a set of moves tailored to the diagnosis. Mechanical J-O-L-T on every deal wastes the diagnostic and applies the wrong prescription. If Judge says the deal is not at risk of indecision, don't run O-L-T — run the deal normally.

### The "champion" who *asks for* FOMO

Sometimes an internal Mobilizer asks the seller for urgency material *because* they're trying to move their own indecisive stakeholders. Don't reflexively refuse — but recognize that the urgency will land on people whose fear is FOMU, not FOMO. Give the Mobilizer take-risk-off material to share instead.

## Pro tips (accelerators — small devices that punch above their weight)

### The "vs. no-decision" pipeline review

At every deal-review meeting, add one standing question per opportunity: *"What's the probability this deal ends in no-decision, and what's the JOLT diagnosis?"* Doing this systematically shifts the team's default lens off "who's the competitor" and onto "what's the customer's actual friction."

### The cook's-tour honesty demo

For deals stuck in outcomes fear, offer to walk the buyer through *the honest year-1 experience with your product*, including the hard parts. Buyers who hear this from the vendor before they buy it trust the vendor more than the ones who hear only the highlight reel. Trust reduces FOMU.

### The Skeptic-first content pass

When entering an account, ask internally: *"What content would convert the toughest Skeptic in a buying committee like this?"* Build for the Skeptic. Everyone else's content becomes easier as a byproduct.

### The disconfirming data as opener

Powerful Challenger opener: lead with the data point that *contradicts* the customer's current operating assumption, sourced honestly. Not to embarrass — to earn the right to reframe. Only works if the data is real; kills trust if it's cherry-picked.

### The "two more demos is one too many" line

For information-face indecision. Explicitly cap the exploration. *"Two more demos is one too many. Let's do one, and I'll send you a summary of what would be in the second one, so we save you the meeting."* Reduces cognitive load, demonstrates confidence, shows respect for the buyer's time.

## Anti-patterns (the "bad selling" Dixon explicitly names)

### "Relationships close deals" (as sole strategy)

**What it looks like:** the rep whose Q4 forecast leans on "we have a great relationship with the CFO."
**Why it fails:** Relationship Builders are 7% of top performers in complex sales. Relationships get you the meeting, not the close.
**How to redirect:** what Commercial Insight has the rep brought to that CFO? What has the CFO been *taught*? If the answer is "we've built rapport", the deal is not de-risked.

### "Always Be Closing" (with indecisive buyers)

**What it looks like:** end of every meeting: "so, what would it take to get this done by end of quarter?"
**Why it fails:** amplifies FOMU. Triggers indecision. Loses deals the JOLT sequence would have closed.
**How to redirect:** replace with a Judge move — *"what's still an open question for you at this stage?"* — followed by tailored O-L-T.

### "Delight the customer"

**What it looks like:** WOW moments, thank-you gifts, over-service.
**Why it fails:** doesn't drive loyalty. Reducing customer *effort* does. From *The Effortless Experience* — adjacent to sales but relevant when the customer's post-sale expectations are being set by pre-sale over-promising.
**How to redirect:** in the sales conversation, don't build the customer's expectations for post-sale delight. Build their confidence in low-effort operation. Reduces FOMU on the deal.

### "Spray and pray" content dumps

**What it looks like:** the AE sending three PDFs, two videos, and four case studies after a call.
**Why it fails:** amplifies information-face indecision. Signals the seller doesn't know what matters.
**How to redirect:** send *one* asset, with a one-sentence framing of why *this specific piece* answers *this specific worry*.

### "Give them options and let them decide"

**What it looks like:** three-tier pricing where the seller stays neutral.
**Why it fails:** amplifies valuation-face indecision. The seller's neutrality reads as "I don't know either."
**How to redirect:** Offer move — opinionated recommendation, take a tier off the table.

### Post-mortem language that hides no-decision

**What it looks like:** CRM notes read "lost to Competitor X" or "no budget" for deals where the customer never chose anyone.
**Why it fails:** the pattern is invisible to the pipeline. The team never learns the frequency of no-decision losses.
**How to redirect:** add a mandatory field: *"Did the customer purchase from anyone?"* If no, tag as no-decision and require a JOLT-face diagnosis.

## Language — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts that carry method:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| "What keeps you up at night?" | Commercial Insight opener that names what *should* be keeping them up | Question-first is Relationship Builder; insight-first is Challenger |
| "We help X do Y" | Reframe the customer's business first, then position | Feature-first opens the door to a features fight |
| "What are your options looking like?" | *"If I were in your seat, here's what I'd focus on..."* | Handing indecision back vs. taking a stance |
| "Just wanted to check in" | Ping tied to a specific worry ("wondering how the internal conversation about X went") | Pings surface indecision; check-ins are noise |
| "Let me know what you'd like to do" | Opinionated recommendation | Great waiters recommend |
| "Are you worried about X?" | *"A lot of our customers at this stage worry about X. How are you thinking about it?"* | Pings, not interrogation |
| "How can we get this over the line?" (implicit urgency) | *"What's still an open question we can address?"* | Diagnostic vs. pressure |
| "Sustainable competitive advantage" (in Commercial Insight) | Specific, quantified, time-bounded reframe | Vague claims fail Dixon's 4 tests |
| "Our champion" | Named Mobilizer with observed spend of political capital | "Champion" often masks a Talker |
| "The customer went dark" | "The buying committee failed to reach consensus" | Names the actual failure mode |
| "We lost to Competitor X" | "Did the customer buy from anyone? If not, no-decision" | Enforces the diagnostic |
| "Add urgency" | "Reduce fear of being wrong" | The right treatment for FOMU-driven indecision |
