# Strategic Narrative — Applications

> When the 5-part strategic narrative fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where strategic narrative fits well

- **B2B enterprise sales deck** — the canonical use case. Zuora, Salesforce, Uberflip, Gong, OneTrust, SpotMe, Zaius all built decks around the 5-part frame.
- **CEO all-hands or external talk** — the CEO models the narrative so the org repeats it. Tien Tzuo on Mad Money is the archetype.
- **Investor pitch deck** — same 5 acts, product introduced late. The narrative that convinces buyers also convinces investors.
- **Category creation or category redefinition** — but named as *movement*, not category. See *[To Create a New Category, Name the New Game](https://medium.com/firm-narrative/to-create-a-new-category-name-the-new-game-70c2e55edc2e)*.
- **Recruiting narrative** — Promised Land becomes the North Star for hires. Hires join a movement, not a company.
- **Product launch** — the launch is an act of the narrative, not a features announcement.
- **Refreshing a stale narrative after a market shift** — the change you named 3 years ago is now common wisdom. Turing's multiple pivots are Raskin's example of iterative narrative work.
- **Diagnosing why sales, marketing, and product describe the company differently** — the narrative reveals whether strategy exists. If the org can't tell one story, they don't have one.
- **Rolling out a new deck across a sales team** — Raskin's essay on this is a rollout playbook, not just a design guide.

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Consumer products with straightforward value props.** If your buyer's decision-making is fast, price-sensitive, and doesn't involve a mindset shift, the 5-part narrative is over-engineered. Reach for StoryBrand or plain copy craft.

- **Commodity offerings competing on price.** Narrative doesn't rescue undifferentiation. If the product is genuinely a commodity, the honest strategic move is to compete on cost — a Playing to Win / Roger Martin call, not a narrative call. Raskin's method presupposes something worth making a movement out of.

- **Tactical copy work** — a landing-page headline, an email subject line, an ad. Raskin's method is CEO-level, deck-level. Use it upstream; use tactical copy craft downstream.

- **Positioning research.** If the user hasn't figured out which market they win in, which competitive alternatives they beat, or what unique attributes they have, they need *positioning* (upstream, invisible), not *narrative* (downstream, public). Reach for April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome*.

- **Internal strategic-choice cascade.** If the user needs to decide *where to play* and *how to win* — that's Roger Martin's *Playing to Win*, not Raskin. Do not do PtW at a narrative question; equally, do not do narrative at a choice question. They stack: Martin produces the choices; Raskin externalizes them.

- **Diagnosis of what's actually broken strategically.** If the user's problem is "I don't know what the crux is," reach for Rumelt's *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* first. Raskin's method presupposes you've already diagnosed the crux — his job is externalizing it as story.

- **When the user just wants a summary of the 2016 essay.** Give them the [link](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0) and stop. Don't run the 5 parts at them.

## Adjacent frameworks — when to reach for a different one

| If the user's situation is... | Reach for... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Consumer product tactical copy | StoryBrand (Donald Miller) | Consumer-scale, tactical, Hero's Journey mapped literally |
| Positioning (which market, competitive alternatives, unique attributes) | April Dunford's *Obviously Awesome* / *Sales Pitch* | Positioning is upstream and invisible; narrative is the downstream public face |
| Internal strategic choices (where to play, how to win) | Roger Martin's *Playing to Win* | Martin is internal cascade; Raskin is external narrative |
| Diagnosis of what's actually going wrong strategically | Rumelt's *Good Strategy / Bad Strategy* / *The Crux* | Rumelt diagnoses the crux; Raskin externalizes it |
| Product vision for internal alignment (3–10 years) | Marty Cagan's product vision doc | Cagan is product-org-facing; Raskin is GTM-facing |
| Understanding the buyer's job | JTBD (Christensen / Ulwick / Moesta) | JTBD is the raw material for step 1 and step 3 |
| Why-first origin story | Simon Sinek's *Start With Why* | Sinek centers company; Raskin centers buyer's world |
| Category design | Play Bigger / Al Ramadan | Category-first, movement-second (Raskin inverts this) |

## How strategic narrative composes with other frameworks

Raskin's method is downstream of some frameworks and upstream of others. Compatibility mapping:

### April Dunford (Obviously Awesome / Sales Pitch)

**Compatible. Dunford is upstream.** Dunford's positioning (market, competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value themes) is the *research artifact* that informs Raskin's narrative. Dunford's *Sales Pitch* structure (insight → alternatives → perfect world → introducing us → proof) is structurally near-identical to Raskin's 5 parts. Dunford is more analytical and market-oriented; Raskin is more emotional and movement-oriented.

**How they stack:** do Dunford positioning first (invisible). Then write Raskin narrative (public).

### JTBD (Christensen / Ulwick / Moesta)

**Compatible. JTBD is an input.** The Job the customer is trying to get done *is* the Promised Land, translated into buyer's-world language. Switch interviews (Moesta) surface the *forces of change* — which is exactly the raw material for step 1 ("name the change in the world"). Raskin doesn't cite JTBD often, but the framework consumes JTBD outputs cleanly.

**How they stack:** JTBD provides step 1 raw material and step 3 Promised Land wording.

### Donald Miller StoryBrand

**Related but different scope.** Both position the customer as hero, both use guide/promised-land beats. Divergence:
- StoryBrand's "problem" is a *personal pain point* (micro).
- Raskin's "change" is a *market/mindset shift* (macro).
- StoryBrand is tactical copy craft (landing pages, email); Raskin is org-level narrative (deck, all-hands, investor pitch).

Rule of thumb: consumer tactical copy = StoryBrand; B2B org-level narrative = Raskin.

### Hero's Journey (Campbell) / Save the Cat (Snyder)

Raskin explicitly parallels his framework to Hero's Journey — the Promised Land = Campbell's "reward"; Magic Gifts = the mentor's gift/talisman. But he flattens Campbell into 5 pragmatic acts. Don't do full Campbell at a business narrative — you'll get lost in ordeal-and-transformation architecture that doesn't fit a deck.

### Marty Cagan (product vision)

**Compatible, nested.** Cagan's product vision doc is a written narrative of the future the product enables, 3–10 years out — same DNA as Raskin's Promised Land. But Cagan is product-org-facing (aligns PMs, engineers), Raskin is GTM-facing (aligns sales, marketing, investors). A team using both would have Cagan's vision *inside* Raskin's narrative — the Promised Land in the sales deck expresses (in buyer-world language) the product vision Cagan produced (in engineer-world language).

### Richard Rumelt (Good Strategy / Bad Strategy, The Crux)

**Compatible. Rumelt is upstream and internal.** Rumelt's kernel: diagnosis → guiding policy → coherent action. Raskin's narrative *externalizes* Rumelt's diagnosis:
- Rumelt's *diagnosis* → Raskin's *change in the world* (phrased as a story the buyer can hear)
- Rumelt's *guiding policy* → Raskin's *Promised Land* (rendered as buyer's aspiration)
- Rumelt's *coherent action* → Raskin's *Magic Gifts* (rendered as buyer's tools for crossing)

**How they stack:** use Rumelt to find the crux (internal, analytical). Use Raskin to render it as a movement (external, emotional).

### Roger Martin (Playing to Win cascade)

**Compatible. Martin is upstream and internal.** Martin's cascade (winning aspiration → where to play → how to win → capabilities → management systems) is the *internal* set of strategic choices. Raskin's narrative is the *external face* of that cascade.

- Martin's *how to win* + *winning aspiration* → Raskin's *Promised Land + Magic Gifts*
- Martin's *where to play* → who the narrative is aimed at
- Martin's *capabilities + management systems* → what makes the Evidence step credible

**Martin's diagnostic that maps to Raskin:** *"if we can't tell one story about the company, we don't have one strategy"* — same failure mode Raskin diagnoses. When the internal cascade is clear, the external narrative is easy to write. When the narrative is fragmented, the cascade probably isn't coherent.

**How they stack:** use Martin to make the choices. Use Raskin to broadcast them.

**Non-negotiable for the skill:** if the user comes with a strategic-narrative question, do not run PtW at them. Different frameworks, different jobs. See `SKILL.md`.

### Simon Sinek (Start With Why)

**Related but Raskin critiques the shape.** Sinek's Golden Circle (why → how → what) centers the *company's* origin/purpose. Raskin's narrative centers the *buyer's world*. In Raskin's frame, a Why-first pitch that opens with the company's origin story is a *Chest Beater* — the whole thing is about you, not the buyer.

You can imagine a hybrid where the "why" is *the shift in the buyer's world you exist to serve* rather than *the founder's origin story* — that would compose. Sinek as usually taught doesn't compose.

### Ben Horowitz ("The company story is the company strategy")

**The axiom.** Raskin puts this line on his homepage. His entire practice is the operationalization of that Horowitz claim. Horowitz stated the axiom; Raskin built the method that turns it into practice.

## Frameworks/framings Raskin has explicitly criticized

See `heuristics.md` for detail. Summary:

- **"We help X do Y" positioning statements** — features-first, no shift.
- **"We are the leader in [category]"** — chest-beating.
- **Old Way / New Way** — invites product comparison.
- **Category creation as goal** — category is downstream of movement.
- **Storytelling as decoration** — the point isn't nicer claims; it's a different frame.
- **Positioning-as-narrative** — collapses two different jobs.
- **Long messaging documents** — *"nobody uses them."*
- **Delegating narrative to marketing or agency** — CEO must own it.

If the user invokes any of these expecting them to work, redirect with attribution.
