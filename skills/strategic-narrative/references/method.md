# Strategic Narrative — Method

> The canonical description of the 5-part strategic narrative in Andy Raskin's own terms. Fidelity is the point — the framework's power comes from doing all 5 parts in order, on the buyer's world, before introducing product. Skip a part or reorder them and you're back to features-first pitching.

## Definition

Raskin's operating claim — and Ben Horowitz's line that Raskin puts on his homepage:

> "The company story is the company strategy."
> — Ben Horowitz, quoted on [andyraskin.com](https://andyraskin.com/)

> "This story really is the strategy."
> — Andy Raskin, [Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-power-of-strategic-narrative), 2023

Strategic narrative is the single top-level story the CEO owns that shows up as the spine of the sales deck, the website, the all-hands, the investor pitch, and the recruiting story. It is not decoration on a pitch. It is the strategy, externalized.

**Test:** if sales, marketing, and product each describe the company differently, the org doesn't have a strategic narrative. The narrative *reveals* whether the strategy exists.

## The 5 parts — two labelings

Raskin has used two labelings since the 2023 refinement. Same 5 acts. The 2023 wording foregrounds that the story is about the *buyer's world*, not yours.

| # | 2016 (canonical, [Zuora essay](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0)) | 2023 ([Lenny 2023](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/the-power-of-strategic-narrative), 19:27–29:18) | What the buyer feels |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Name a Big, Relevant Change in the World | Name the Old Game (and the New Game) | "Something has shifted — this matters to me" |
| 2 | Show There Will Be Winners and Losers | Name the Stakes | "If I don't move, I lose" |
| 3 | Tease the Promised Land | Name the Objective | "That's where I want to be" |
| 4 | Introduce Features as Magic Gifts | Name the Obstacles | "I need these tools to cross" |
| 5 | Present Evidence You Can Make the Story Real | Overcome the Obstacles | "Others like me have crossed — I can too" |

The product enters at step 4 (or 5 in the 2023 wording). Everything upstream is the buyer's world.

### 1. Name a Big, Relevant Change in the World

**What it asks:** what shift is undeniably happening in your buyer's world that creates stakes and urgency?

**Not:** industry statistics. Not "The market is growing 30% YoY." Not "Digital transformation is important." Those are education, not emotion.

**Author's words:**
> "What attracts human attention is change."
> — Robert McKee, quoted in [The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever Seen](https://medium.com/the-mission/the-greatest-sales-deck-ive-ever-seen-4f4ef3391ba0)

> "Your primary goal in setting context is not educating prospects, but engaging them emotionally."
> — [Is the "Greatest Sales Deck" Great for Savvy Prospects?](https://medium.com/firm-narrative/is-the-greatest-sales-deck-great-for-savvy-prospects-d5de48fb2f94)

**Zuora's:** *subscription economy* — buyers no longer want one-time software purchases; they want recurring relationships.

**How to test:** name the change in one sentence. If a prospect wouldn't nod along ("yes, I feel that shift"), you named a fake change. The change must be one *the buyer already senses in their bones* — you're giving language to something they've already noticed.

**Raskin's redirect if you're stuck:** *"Harvest language about what's at stake directly from customers."* Use the discovery-opener: "How is this shift playing out for you?" (SpotMe pattern.)

### 2. Show There Will Be Winners and Losers

**What it asks:** who wins and who loses in the new game? Adaptation → survival + upside. Ignoring → extinction.

**Zuora's:** the Fortune 500 "mass extinction" chart — companies that failed to adapt to recurring-revenue models — set against subscription-native winners (Netflix, Salesforce) and rejuvenated incumbents.

**Why this part matters:** it activates loss aversion. Without stakes, the prospect files your message under "interesting but not urgent." Raskin's line: *"If your prospect isn't scared to stay put, they won't move."*

**Anti-pattern:** naming a competitor as the loser. That reads petty and same-shaped. The loser is *anyone who fails to adapt to the new game* — a class of company, not a rival brand.

### 3. Tease the Promised Land

**What it asks:** what is the buyer's aspirational future state — described concretely — that they can only reach by playing the new game?

**Not:** a feature list. Not your product's capabilities. The Promised Land is defined by *criteria the buyer wants to hit*, not by what your product does. If someone reads your Promised Land and says "cool product," you got it wrong. If they say "yes, that's what I want to be able to say about my business," you got it right.

**Zuora's:** *"Turn customers into subscribers."* Concrete criteria — recurring-revenue infrastructure, customer records built around subscribers, subscription analytics. All buyer-world. None product-world.

**Test:** Would a competitor's product also help the buyer reach this Promised Land? If yes, the Promised Land is honest. If no, you've secretly defined it as your feature set.

**Author's words:**
> "'Goodbye product' does not mean your product is unimportant. It does not mean you don't talk about features, speeds and feeds. It means don't start there."
> — [Goodbye Product. Hello Movement.](https://medium.com/firm-narrative/goodbye-product-hello-movement-b06df08e02b8)

### 4. Introduce Features as Magic Gifts

**What it asks:** now that the Promised Land is on the table, what capabilities does the buyer need to make the crossing? Your features are *tools*, not the story's main character.

**Raskin's borrowing from Campbell:** in Hero's Journey terms, the Magic Gifts are the mentor's gift/talisman — the thing that lets the hero (the buyer) cross terrain they otherwise couldn't. Your company is the mentor, not the hero.

**Zuora's:** customer-record model, order-to-revenue, subscription analytics — each reframed as *capabilities you need to win the new game*, not as features Zuora happens to sell.

**Test:** for each feature, ask *"what obstacle to the Promised Land does this remove?"* If you can't finish the sentence, the feature doesn't belong in the deck.

### 5. Present Evidence You Can Make the Story Real

**What it asks:** what proof exists that customers like this buyer have already reached the Promised Land using your Magic Gifts?

**Not:** a logo wall. Not "here are our customers." Customer stories that mirror the buyer — same starting point, made the crossing, describe the result in buyer-world terms.

**Zuora's:** customer proof of orgs that made the transition to recurring-revenue models. Each proof story reprises the same 5-part arc in miniature.

**Pattern-response Raskin looks for:** *"How quickly can we get started?"* From [How Great Sales Pitches Drive Urgency](https://medium.com/the-mission/how-great-sales-pitches-drive-urgency-e35aa1069de3). If the prospect asks that, the 5 parts landed. If they say "cool tool" or "let me think about it," one of the earlier parts collapsed.

## The three things strategic narrative is NOT

Raskin returns to this constantly. Untangling these three is often 80% of the value of a session.

### Not positioning

- **Positioning** = the *internal, invisible research artifact* that guides the narrative. Which market you win in, competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value. April Dunford's territory.
- **Strategic narrative** = the *external story* the org tells that operationalizes the positioning.

Positioning is upstream and invisible. Narrative is downstream and public. You need both. They are not the same thing. Raskin doesn't sell positioning; he sells narrative.

### Not messaging

- **Messaging** = downstream copy assets — taglines, benefit statements, landing-page headlines.
- **Strategic narrative** = the single story the messaging expresses.

Long messaging docs are the trap Raskin flags:
> "Nobody uses them."

Messaging without a narrative underneath is copy-editing an incoherent story.

### Not storytelling-as-decoration

The most common misconception:
> "Strategic narrative is not about 'using storytelling' to make your claims of superiority more impactful."
> — [LinkedIn, 2023](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/andyraskin_strategic-narrative-is-not-about-using-storytelling-activity-7044706892291469313-gYt6)

Adding anecdotes to a chest-beating pitch is not strategic narrative. The point is not to make your existing claims sound nicer — it's to change what the buyer thinks the story is about. Different job entirely.

## How the narrative shows up across the org

A strategic narrative is not just for the sales deck. If it's the strategy externalized, it appears everywhere the org communicates. From [Selling Your New Sales Deck to Sales](https://medium.com/firm-narrative/selling-your-new-sales-deck-to-sales-a7dc032a57bb) and the Lenny episode:

- **Sales deck** — the *core artifact*. Raskin's "true north." Anchors everything else.
- **Website & content** — same change/stakes/promised-land story, in landing-page voice.
- **CEO all-hands / external talks** — CEO models the narrative in every appearance. Canonical example: Tien Tzuo telling the *subscription economy* story on Mad Money with Jim Cramer. Same story, TV register.
- **Investor deck** — same 5 acts; product introduced late.
- **Recruiting** — the Promised Land is the org's North Star for hires. New hires join a movement, not a company.
- **Product roadmap** — features get rationalized as "capabilities the buyer needs to win the new game."
- **Sales enablement** — reps use the change slide as a *discovery opener*: *"How is this shift playing out for you?"* This generates "gold" customer language (SpotMe pattern) that in turn refines the narrative.

If the deck, the website, the all-hands, and the investor pitch tell different stories, you don't have a strategic narrative — you have four narratives. Same-story test.

## Sequence of application

Raskin does not treat the 5 parts as sequential to-dos. In practice:

1. **Find the change first.** Nothing else lands if step 1 doesn't. Talk to customers, hunt for the shift they already sense.
2. **Draft the Promised Land second.** The Promised Land constrains everything downstream. Get it in buyer-world language.
3. **Test winners-and-losers third.** Are the stakes real enough that inaction feels expensive?
4. **Reframe features as Magic Gifts fourth.** Only now.
5. **Curate evidence fifth.** Which customer stories reprise the arc?

Then iterate. If step 4 doesn't work (the features don't naturally read as "what the buyer needs to cross"), the Promised Land is probably wrong. Back up.

## What this method is NOT

Raskin is emphatic:

- **Not a copy-craft exercise.** Rewriting the deck's copy without changing the frame produces prettier features-first pitching. Same problem.
- **Not delegable to an agency or a marketing hire.** *"The CEO has to be the decider… this story really is the strategy."* If the CEO isn't in the room, don't start.
- **Not "old way / new way."** That framing pushes toward product comparison. Use "Old Game / New Game." (Post-2016 refinement — see `post-book.md`.)
- **Not a slide template.** Copying Zuora's slides literally, slide-for-slide, misses the method. The point is buyer-language discovery, not deck-layout replication.
- **Not a one-time output.** Narrative refreshes every 18–36 months as the market shifts. Static narrative = stale narrative.

**Author's words:**
> "The most powerful narratives are always 'wrong,' in that they've sacrificed completeness for brevity."
> — [dylan.tweney interview](https://dylan.tweney.com/the-art-of-strategic-narrative-a-conversation-with-andy-raskin-2/)

Being provocative beats being complete.
