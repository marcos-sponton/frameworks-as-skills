# Working Backwards — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices that separate applying the mechanisms well from doing the theater version Bill Carr spends most of his airtime warning against. Attribution is precise: this is from the 2021 book, this from the 2026 firm blog, this from Carr on Lenny 2023.

## Do's

### Start with the customer, not the capability

**How to apply:** for any product conversation, before anyone opens a laptop, force the answers to: (1) Who exactly is the customer? (2) What specific problem do they have? (3) Why does this matter to them? (4) How would we know we'd solved it?

If those four answers aren't concrete, there is no PR/FAQ to write yet — go do more customer discovery.

**Author's frame:**
> "Start with what's best for the customer and then come backward from there."
> — Bill Carr, [Lenny's Podcast](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/unpacking-amazons-unique-ways-of), Nov 2023

### Write the press release from a future launch date, in customer voice

Not "we're planning to build a thing." Write it as if it's launched, from the customer's point of view. Names, prices, quotes, "getting started." The friction of writing convincingly *as if it already existed* surfaces the parts of the idea that don't yet hold up.

**Author's structural rule:**
> "The heart of it really is that first paragraph, it's a short description, that second paragraph, that's the problem statement."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Be factual and data-rich. No hyperbole.

The press release is a factual document, not a marketing pitch. Numbers where numbers exist. Comparisons where comparisons matter. No "revolutionary." No "game-changing." No "world-class."

**Author's words:**
> "You don't want to use hyperbole. It would be very factual with numbers, data rich document too."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Invest weeks or months in the PR/FAQ before building anything

The discomfort of "we're spending three months on a document" is exactly the discomfort the mechanism is engineered to force. Time spent writing and reviewing is orders of magnitude cheaper than time spent building the wrong thing.

**Why this feels wrong outside Amazon:** because most teams have never actually paid the cost of building the wrong thing at scale. Once you have, the upfront investment feels like a bargain.

### Treat rejection of PR/FAQs as the point, not a failure

Most PR/FAQs should be rejected in review. That's the mechanism working. If your review meetings are approving 80% of what comes in, either your team is only writing safe PR/FAQs (bias-for-action broken) or your reviewers are being polite (Backbone broken).

**Author's frame:**
> "The fact that most PR/FAQs don't get approved is a feature, not a bug… preserves your company's resources to build products that will yield the highest impact for customers and your business."
> — Bryar/Carr, via Commoncog: https://commoncog.com/putting-amazons-pr-faq-to-practice/

### Force silent reading at the start of the 6-pager meeting

20 minutes. Everyone reads. No one presents. Yes, it feels weird. Do it anyway.

**Bezos's disposition (quoted in the book):** assume each sentence is wrong until proven otherwise. Challenge content, not motive.

### For a single-threaded team, name it — and mean it

An STL is: one leader, one initiative, zero competing responsibilities, dedicated cross-functional resources. If any of those four is missing, you don't have an STL — you have a labeled matrix team.

**Author's structural rule:**
> "Let's create teams that can stand alone, where there's a single leader and the cross-functional resources that they need are all either directly report to them or are dedicated to them."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Map the end-to-end customer experience, then find your input metric

Don't pick metrics from a list. Walk the customer journey — from awareness to first use to habit. Where in that journey does your team's work land? What lever, in that spot, can your team actually move this week?

**Author's rule:**
> "A sign that's a good input metric is, first of all, map your end-to-end customer experience."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Practice Disagree AND Commit as two operations, not one

**Disagree:** if you're in the room, you owe your true objection. Silence-during, complaint-after is the pattern the mechanism kills.

**AND commit:** once the decision is made, sprint hard. No slow-walk. No passive resistance. No "well, I said we shouldn't."

**Author's words:**
> "The commit part done well means that it's not just like I'm going to commit, I don't really agree."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Give the Bar Raiser real veto power

Not "the Bar Raiser will strongly recommend." The Bar Raiser can kill a hire. That authority is the mechanism. If your Bar Raiser can't veto, you have a senior interviewer with a fancy title.

**Author's frame:**
> "The Bar Raiser was there to act as a balance also on the urgency bias that every hiring manager has."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Use "slow is smooth and smooth is fast" against the shortcut instinct

When someone says "we don't have time for a PR/FAQ," the response is Carr's tagline. Skipping the mechanism to save weeks costs months later.

**Author's tagline:**
> "Slow is smooth and smooth is fast."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

## Don'ts

### Don't approach innovation skills-forward

Skills-forward = "we have this team / stack / technology, what could we build with it?" This is the failure mode the PR/FAQ is engineered to prevent. It produces products optimized for what's easy to build, not what customers need. When a team drifts into skills-forward framing during a PR/FAQ review, name it and redirect.

### Don't accept "our idea is too complex for a PR/FAQ"

This is one of the most common rationalizations for skipping the mechanism. Complex products need PR/FAQs *more*, not less. If the product is too complex to describe in a one-page press release from a customer's perspective, that's a signal — either the customer benefit isn't crisp, or the product is trying to be too many things.

Common rationalizations to watch for (from Commoncog's applied practice notes):
- "This only works for large companies."
- "It doesn't account for iteration."
- "My idea is too complex."

### Don't confuse a PRD with a PR/FAQ

A Product Requirements Document is written *for engineering* about *what to build*. A PR/FAQ is written *for review* about *whether to build*. Confusing them defeats the mechanism — you're writing an implementation spec when you should be arguing for the bet's existence.

### Don't let the 6-pager become PowerPoint dressed as prose

Bullet points sneak in. Charts get inlined. The prose degenerates into headers with sentence fragments. Each of these dilutions makes the document harder to interrogate — which is exactly what the mechanism was preventing. Enforce prose.

### Don't skip silent reading because "we're all busy"

The 20-minute silent read is the mechanism that ensures everyone encounters the full argument before anyone speaks. Skip it and the meeting collapses into the loudest person's summary. Bezos himself calls it "the weirdest meeting culture" — and does it anyway, because the alternative is worse.

### Don't name an STL who's actually 30% on the initiative

A "leader" split across three initiatives is the pre-STL state Amazon rejected. If you can't dedicate someone fully, either the initiative isn't important enough to name an STL, or you need to reallocate. What you can't do is get the benefits of single-threaded ownership from a fractionally-threaded leader.

### Don't matrix an STL's resources

If designers and engineers on the STL's team are shared with two other teams, the dependency tax eats the speed advantage. The whole point of STL is *separable* — the team can move without asking anyone else's permission.

### Don't confuse output metrics with input metrics

Revenue is an output. Active customers is an output. Share price is an output. Selection breadth, delivery speed, defect rate, price-competitiveness — those are inputs. If your team is "tracking" revenue as their metric, they're tracking an outcome they can't move directly. Force the decomposition.

### Don't use compound "fitness function" metrics

Amazon explicitly rejected one-number-that-rules-them-all metrics. They obscure cause and effect. Decompose to individual, controllable inputs — even at the cost of a longer dashboard.

### Don't put the Bar Raiser on the hiring team

The Bar Raiser has to be structurally independent of the hire's success or failure. If they're the future manager's peer, or worse, the future teammate, the mechanism collapses. Bar Raiser is outside the pod.

### Don't hard-code annual bonuses to Operating Plan targets

This is the "compensation-planning doom loop" — the post-book warning Carr sharpened in the March 2026 blog. When bonuses are hard-coded to plan targets, the planning process stops serving strategy and starts serving personal comp. Teams pick sandbagged targets they can beat rather than ambitious targets they might miss.

**Author's words:**
> "When annual bonus targets are hard-coded into Operating Plan goals, the planning process stops being about taking calculated risks that will benefit your customers and the company, and it starts being about maximizing personal compensation."
> — Bill Carr, [Ending the compensation-planning doom loop](https://workingbackwards.com/blog/ending-the-compensation-planning-doom-loop-in-leadership/), March 2026

Fix: long-term equity + holistic performance reviews + shared risk across leadership vetting of plans.

### Don't sell adoption as easy

If you're helping a user think about importing Working Backwards into their org, don't promise a frictionless transformation. Carr is explicit: *"Implementing a new process is not easy, it requires commitment and discipline to get through."* Setting the expectation honestly is part of the mechanism's success.

### Don't assume mechanisms guarantee outcomes

The Fire Phone had a PR/FAQ. It was reviewed. It got built. It failed — because the underlying hypothesis (customers wanted 3D-effect + Firefly product-recognition) was wrong. Mechanisms make bets more honest, not automatically correct.

**Author's frame:**
> "None of these things give you the answer. They are tools to help you make decisions."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

## Gotchas

### Gotcha 1 — CEO commitment is required

Carr and Bryar's consulting firm explicitly targets Series C+ and public companies. The reason: importing Working Backwards mechanisms without CEO sponsorship fails. The mechanisms are counter-cultural for most orgs (silent reading, weeks on a PR/FAQ, most PR/FAQs rejected, Bar Raisers vetoing hires) and without top-of-org insistence, they erode within a quarter.

If a mid-level user asks "how do I bring Working Backwards to my company?", the honest answer is: your leverage is running the mechanism in your own team, not converting the org. Pushing it upward without CEO air cover is a recipe for frustration.

### Gotcha 2 — Emotional resistance to the review

The PR/FAQ review is designed to interrogate. People whose ideas get interrogated experience discomfort. That's the mechanism working — but it's also why teams rationalize skipping it.

Warning signs:
- Review meetings that stay polite.
- 80%+ PR/FAQ approval rates.
- The same critic making all the challenges (everyone else deferring).

Fix: make the disposition explicit. Interrogation is respect. If you're not being challenged, you're not being reviewed.

### Gotcha 3 — Tacit knowledge that can't be templated

The PR/FAQ process develops through osmosis inside Amazon. Bryar and Carr have explicitly said outsiders adopting the format often miss the disposition. A template alone isn't the mechanism; the review discipline is.

If a user asks for "a PR/FAQ template" without any customer problem to work through, redirect: templates are downstream of the actual customer question. Start there.

### Gotcha 4 — Speed vs. upfront investment feels backwards

Every non-Amazon team's instinct: "we don't have three months to write documents, we need to ship." Working Backwards inverts this: the three months on a PR/FAQ saves nine months on the wrong build. This inversion feels wrong until you've paid the cost of building the wrong thing at scale.

Carr's counter:
> "Slow is smooth and smooth is fast."
> — Carr, Lenny 2023

### Gotcha 5 — Mechanisms depend on the Principles

Working Backwards mechanisms rest on Amazon's 14 Leadership Principles as substrate. Adopting mechanisms without a version of Customer Obsession, Ownership, Are Right A Lot, Dive Deep, Backbone, and Bias for Action makes them theater.

You don't have to adopt Amazon's Principles wholesale — but if your org's stated values pull in a different direction (e.g. "consensus-driven collaboration" clashes with Backbone/Disagree), the mechanisms will fight the culture and lose.

### Gotcha 6 — Drift toward smaller bets

The post-book warning Carr sharpened in a March 2026 blog post: organizations naturally drift toward smaller, more manageable decisions rather than significant, complex ones. Mechanisms alone don't stop this. Leadership has to actively resist the drift — by explicitly asking, in every planning cycle, "where is our next big bet?"

### Gotcha 7 — "We already have a PM writing docs" theater

A senior PM writing PRDs is not a PR/FAQ mechanism. A weekly status meeting is not a WBR. A senior engineer running debriefs is not a Bar Raiser (unless they have veto power and structural independence). If the mechanism is only the label, it isn't working. Look for the disposition, not the vocabulary.

## Pro tips (post-book refinements)

### Pro tip — Use the coordination tax framing when arguing for STL

In the 2026 blog batch, the firm sharpened the STL pitch around the *coordination tax* — the compounding cost of every dependency between teams. This is the argument that lands with modern engineering leaders: STL isn't just "one throat to choke," it's the structural minimization of coordination cost. Reach for this framing when the user is skeptical of the STL model.

### Pro tip — Find atomic customer needs, not surface preferences

The March 2026 firm blog introduces "atomic customer needs" — "needs so deeply embedded that they will persist across time periods." When drafting the "problem paragraph" of a PR/FAQ, push toward atomic needs, not stated preferences. A stated preference can flip with a trend; an atomic need is why Prime free shipping still works twenty years later.

### Pro tip — Separate innovation teams from core business teams

Another March 2026 sharpening: innovation and core-business execution require different mechanisms and different dispositions. Trying to run both under the same STL, in the same operating cadence, usually starves innovation. When the user is trying to build something new inside a mature business, ask: is this initiative structurally separated from the core, or is it competing for the same review meetings, the same metrics, the same bonus pool?

### Pro tip — Correction of Errors is not blame; it's mechanism-hunting

The COE mechanism succeeds when it identifies the *mechanism gap* that let the failure happen. It fails when it identifies the *person* who screwed up. Amazon's response to failed bets — "make that investment pay off, document, share what you've learned" — only works if the room isn't secretly hunting a scapegoat.
