# 7 Powers — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Helmer's diagnostic moves and misapplication warnings — that separate applying 7 Powers well from confusing it with generic "moat" talk. Attribution is precise: this comes from the book, this from Lenny 2024, this from the Trium Group interview with Chenyi Shi, this from Acquired's 7 Powers episode.

## Do's

### Diagnose Barrier-first

Helmer's core diagnostic move. Don't start from "which of the 7 do they have?" — start from *"what stops competitors from doing the same thing?"* The mechanism of prevention IS the Power type. Barrier type → Power type is a one-to-one mapping.

| Barrier observed | Power implied |
|---|---|
| Collateral damage to an incumbent's existing P&L | **Counter-Positioning** |
| Prohibitive share-gain cost for a smaller rival | **Scale Economies** |
| Network tipping point that has already turned | **Network Economies** |
| Customer's cost / hassle to leave | **Switching Costs** |
| Hysteresis (long, path-dependent build) — willingness-to-pay premium | **Branding** |
| Hysteresis + causal ambiguity — cost/quality of production | **Process Power** |
| Fiat (legal / physical / decreed exclusivity) | **Cornered Resource** |

**How to apply:** for any claimed advantage, ask "what stops arbitrage?" Push until you get to a *mechanism*, not a feeling. If no mechanism, no Power.

### Run the 3 S's before declaring a Power

- **Superior** — improves free cash flow vs. competitors?
- **Significant** — material, not a rounding error?
- **Sustainable** — largely immune to arbitrage?

Failing any of the three = not Power. This is Helmer's founder-claim filter.

### Test each claimed Power against each competitor individually

**Direct + functional + potential.** A single arbitrageur can destroy differential margins. Enumerate: who exactly can't or won't do this? If the answer is "well, none of the current players will get around to it", that's a lead, not Power.

### Think about Power *before* PMF is fully established, not after

**Author's move:** *"The first invention is product-market fit, but developing power is a different muscle. The second invention is getting power with product-market fit."* — Chenyi Shi, framing "Second Invention", Trium Group interview.

Getting to Power is not automatic once you have PMF. Most companies that reach PMF never develop Power. The window is stage-specific.

### Combine Powers — they compound

Powers are additive/compounding, not mutually exclusive. Look for sequences: Counter-Positioning first (Netflix vs. Blockbuster) → Scale Economies during Takeoff → Branding in Stability. When evaluating a mature business, expect multiple Powers stacked.

### Use the Value Axiom as the disciplining lens

> "Strategy has one and only one objective: maximizing potential fundamental business value."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers*

If a proposed action doesn't move fundamental value (cash flow expectations, discounted), it's out of scope. Kills purpose-language, mission-language, and vibes-based strategy talk in one move.

### Match the Power to the stage

Origination → Counter-Positioning + Cornered Resource.
Takeoff → Scale Economies + Network Economies + Switching Costs.
Stability → Branding + Process Power.

If someone is trying to build Network Economies in a market where takeoff has already been won by a competitor, tell them the door is closed and redirect.

## Don'ts

### Don't confuse benefits with Power

**Author's words:**
> "Benefits are common, and often bear little positive impact on company value, as they are generally subject to full arbitrage."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers*

Most "advantages" companies celebrate are benefits without barriers. The market will arbitrage them. Only the ones protected by a specific Barrier survive.

### Don't confuse operational excellence with Process Power

**Author's words:**
> "You're on a treadmill and if you stop running on that treadmill, you get creamed, but it's not Power. The things that drive operational excellence can be mimicked."
> — Helmer, Lenny's Podcast, May 2024

Excellence is table stakes. Process Power = excellence + hysteresis (long build) + causal ambiguity (competitors can't decompose it). Without both, it's just running harder than the other guys.

### Don't confuse network effects with Network Economies

**Author's words (via Chenyi Shi):**
> "Network effects are basically an attribute of any market... but they're very infrequently material."
> — Trium Group interview

Effects exist almost everywhere. Economies require the value increase to be *material* AND create a sharp, winner-take-most tipping. Most founder claims of "network effects" don't survive this test.

### Don't confuse brand recognition with Brand Power

Being known ≠ commanding a premium for an identical offering. If a customer wouldn't pay more for your product vs. a functionally identical unbranded one, you have brand awareness, not Brand Power.

### Don't assume data scale is Power

Common founder claim, usually fails the Barrier test. Data scale is only Power if it produces a Benefit competitors can't match — and even then, the Barrier is usually thin (data quality plateaus, competitors accumulate their own, model performance saturates).

### Don't treat speed / execution as Power

**Author's principle:** speed may boost Power once you have it, but if Power does not yet exist, you cannot rely on it. Fast execution without a Barrier is just a slightly delayed loss.

### Don't claim a leadership team as Cornered Resource

Almost always fails the sufficiency test (leadership is one input among many) and the transferability test (great teams have failed in other contexts). Helmer's caution is explicit.

### Don't extend Counter-Positioning beyond incumbents

Counter-Positioning is *partial* Power. It protects only against incumbents whose existing business creates collateral damage. It does NOT protect against fresh entrants copying the new model. This is why Counter-Positioning is stage-specific and eventually must give way to other Powers if the business is to persist.

### Don't claim Switching Costs on non-repeat categories

Switching Costs only accrue on the *next* purchase from *existing* customers. One-time-purchase categories cannot be defended this way, and neither can categories with rapid customer churn.

### Don't substitute planning for invention

**Author's words:**
> "Planning rarely creates Power."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers*

Powers arise from invention (product, business-model, or process) that lands in an available window. Planning refines execution once the invention exists — it does not summon Power into being.

### Don't retrofit — "lemons into lemonade" is rare

Trying to convert a business that lacks Power into a business with Power by re-narrating what already exists almost never works. Helmer's investor's read: rare enough that betting on it is a bad prior.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The Statics vs. Dynamics trap

Practitioners identify a Power in a mature company (Statics — easy) and then reason as if they can *design* their way to that Power (Dynamics — hard). Diagnosing Netflix's Scale Economies today tells you nothing about how to *build* Scale Economies in a new business. The two operations are different in kind, not degree.

### The "we have all seven" claim

If a founder lists more than 2–3 Powers, they're conflating benefits with Powers. Very few companies have >2 concurrent Powers. Push back with the Barrier test on each.

### The wrong-stage Power claim

Claiming Branding on a two-year-old company (fails Hysteresis). Claiming Process Power on a company that hasn't operated the process for a decade (fails Hysteresis). Claiming Network Economies before the tipping point is anywhere near reached (fails the tipping test). Match the Power claim to the age and stage of the business.

### The "network effect" over-invocation

Practitioners hear "network effects" and check the Network Economies box. Most alleged network effects are (a) not material, (b) not winner-take-most, or (c) local rather than global. Ask: is there a tipping point? Has anyone actually reached it? What happens if a rival gets there first?

### The Cornered Resource over-claim

Cornered Resource is the Power founders reach for when they can't find another one — "our team", "our IP", "our data", "our founder's insight." Run the five screens: idiosyncratic, non-arbitraged, transferable, ongoing, sufficient. Most claims fail sufficiency.

### The "brand" over-claim

Brand recognition, brand mentions, brand affinity, positive reviews — none of these are Brand Power on their own. Brand Power requires *willingness-to-pay a premium for an identical offering*. Test: at parity feature/price, would customers pay 10–30% more for the branded version? If no, no Brand Power.

### Counter-Positioning claim without the incumbent-constraint proof

If you can't articulate exactly *why* the incumbent won't respond (which existing business would be cannibalized, and how much), you don't have Counter-Positioning. The 5 stages (denial → ridicule → fear → anger → capitulation) should be observable in the incumbent's behavior over time.

## Pro tips (accelerators — small devices that punch above their weight)

### The founder-claim triage

When a founder claims a moat, three questions:
1. *What's the Benefit — in cash flow terms?*
2. *What's the Barrier — the specific mechanism that stops arbitrage?*
3. *Which of the 7 does that Barrier map to?*

If any question stalls, the claim isn't Power yet.

### The "which look-alike is this?" test

For every Power there's a look-alike that fails the test. When you're tempted to check a box, first name the look-alike:

- Scale Economies look-alike: *being bigger without a downward-sloping cost curve*.
- Network Economies look-alike: *network effects that aren't material or don't tip*.
- Counter-Positioning look-alike: *a novel business model that incumbents could copy but haven't yet*.
- Switching Costs look-alike: *hassle to switch that competitors face equally on the acquire side*.
- Branding look-alike: *brand recognition without willingness-to-pay a premium*.
- Cornered Resource look-alike: *a good team, an insight, or a patent that fails sufficiency*.
- Process Power look-alike: *operational excellence without hysteresis*.

If the look-alike fits better than the Power, you don't have the Power.

### The investor's cash-flow test

For each claimed Power, complete the sentence: *"This Power should move cash flow by ~X% relative to competitors, sustainably for ~Y years."* If you can't fill in the numbers even directionally, you're describing a story, not Power.

### The Barrier-first challenge

When someone claims Power, ask *"What stops competitors from doing the same thing?"* — and don't accept intermediate answers like "our culture" or "our speed." Push to a mechanism.

### The stage check

Before accepting any Power claim, ask which stage the company is in. Then check the stage map:

- Origination → only Counter-Positioning + Cornered Resource are on the table. Reject claims of Scale Economies, Network Economies, Switching Costs, Branding, or Process Power.
- Takeoff → Scale, Network, Switching are on the table; Branding + Process are still too early.
- Stability → Branding + Process become plausible; the earlier ones should already be locked in.

## The 5 stages of incumbent response to Counter-Positioning

Predictable, economically-rational — not a failure of vision:

1. **Denial** — "that's not a real market."
2. **Ridicule** — "no one will pay for that."
3. **Fear** — quiet task forces, competing pilots.
4. **Anger** — public defensive campaigns, price wars.
5. **Capitulation** — acquire, imitate too late, or exit.

**Diagnostic use:** if you see an incumbent in stages 1–3 while a challenger is scaling, Counter-Positioning is likely operating. If the incumbent moves directly to capitulation, the collateral damage was even worse than the framework predicted.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts that Helmer enforces:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Moat | Power (with a specific type named) | "Moat" is a metaphor; Power is definitional |
| Competitive advantage | Persistent Differential Returns | Advantages come and go; PDR is the outcome that matters |
| Sustainable advantage | Power + Barrier | Names the mechanism, not just the outcome |
| Network effects | Network Economies (if material + tipping) OR network effects (if not) | Reserve Network Economies for the Power case |
| Operational excellence | Process Power (if hysteresis) OR operational excellence (if not) | Excellence without hysteresis is table stakes |
| Brand | Brand Power (if premium at parity) OR brand recognition | Recognition ≠ Power |
| Data moat | Power via [specific Barrier] OR "we don't have Power on data yet" | Force a specific Barrier claim |
| First-mover advantage | Counter-Positioning OR Network Economies at scale OR nothing | First-mover per se is not Power |
| Speed of execution | Table stakes | Speed does not create Power |
| Vision / mission | Value Axiom + Power | Fundamental value is the disciplining lens |

## What the taxonomy does NOT cover (by design)

Helmer's scope is narrower than a general strategy framework. 7 Powers does not cover:

- **How to invent** — the framework recognizes invention when it produces Power, but doesn't tell you how to invent.
- **Mission / purpose / culture** — orthogonal to Power.
- **Org design and execution** — Helmer treats these as necessary but non-strategic.
- **Pricing mechanics** — Power creates the *option* to price differentially; the tactical decision is downstream.
- **Marketing tactics** — orthogonal to Brand Power (which is about willingness-to-pay, not campaigns).
- **M&A rationales beyond Power acquisition** — synergies without Power are usually value-destroying.

For anything in the "not covered" list, reach for the appropriate other framework (see `applications.md`).
