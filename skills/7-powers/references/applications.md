# 7 Powers — Applications

> When 7 Powers fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where 7 Powers fits well

- **Analyzing whether a specific company has a real, durable moat.** The Barrier-first diagnostic is exactly the shape of this question.
- **Stress-testing a founder's competitive-advantage claim.** The Benefit + Barrier test + 3 S's screen filters most claims quickly. Adam D'Angelo's line (cited approvingly by Helmer): *"Nine out of ten founders incorrectly assert their competitive advantages."*
- **Investment thesis for durability of returns.** The framework's investor lens is its native register; Strategy Capital is the operational proof.
- **Competitor teardowns.** Naming which of the seven each competitor has (or doesn't) clarifies competitive dynamics quickly.
- **Deciding which Power a business should aim to build at its current stage.** The three-stage sequencing (Origination / Takeoff / Stability) makes this actionable.
- **Critiquing a strategy doc that gestures at "competitive advantage."** Forces the doc to name a specific Power with a specific Barrier.
- **Reading a business after a Rumelt-style diagnosis or Martin-style cascade has surfaced "how do we win?"** 7 Powers unpacks the "how" into a specific mechanistic taxonomy.
- **Comparing two businesses on durability, not just growth.** Growth is common; Power is rare.
- **Second-invention decisions** — companies at PMF trying to decide what to build next. The "AWS-like second business" thread applies here.
- **Board / investor conversations about moat strength.** The framework gives a shared vocabulary that survives rigorous questioning.

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Pre-product/market-fit search.** Helmer is explicit that *"planning rarely creates Power"* — pre-PMF founders are searching for a market, not designing a moat. Applying 7 Powers here is premature and usually produces false confidence. Use lean discovery / continuous discovery / Rumelt's crux-finding instead.

- **Purely operational or execution questions.** "How do we ship faster?" or "how do we hire ML engineers?" are not 7 Powers questions. Helmer explicitly separates operational excellence from Process Power; ops questions don't need the taxonomy.

- **Marketing / positioning / branding tactics.** Brand Power is a *narrow* concept — willingness-to-pay a premium at parity. General marketing strategy questions (campaigns, channels, positioning-in-the-mind) live in Dunford / Raskin / Sharp territory, not Helmer's.

- **Pricing mechanics.** Power creates the *option* to price differentially; the tactical decision of how much to charge is downstream. Use monetization frameworks (Ramanujam, van Westendorp, etc.).

- **Culture, hiring, org design.** Orthogonal to Power (with one exception: Process Power can rest on culture as one of its causally-ambiguous components).

- **General strategy writing when Power isn't the crux.** If the strategic problem is really a diagnosis problem (why isn't this working?), use Rumelt. If it's really a choice-integration problem (what set of choices reinforces?), use Martin. 7 Powers is the right tool when the question is *specifically about durability of returns*.

- **When the user just wants a summary of 7 Powers.** Give them the book link and the Acquired episode. Don't run the taxonomy on their behalf.

## Adjacency to other frameworks

### vs. Porter's Five Forces

- **Porter asks:** "Which industry?" (industry attractiveness).
- **Helmer asks:** "Which company within an industry, and why does it persistently earn above-average returns?"
- Porter is diagnostic of *industry structure*. Helmer is diagnostic + prescriptive of *firm-level advantage*.
- Porter's Five Forces influence *whether* Power is available in an industry; 7 Powers identify *which* Power a specific firm can hold.
- Helmer's Barrier concept is more explicit and testable than Porter's "barriers to entry" — it's tied to a specific Power type with a specific mechanism (hysteresis, fiat, collateral damage, tipping, cost-to-switch, share-gain cost).

**Compose them:** run Porter to assess whether the industry admits Power at all; run 7 Powers to identify which Powers a specific firm can hold.

### vs. Rumelt's kernel (Good Strategy, Bad Strategy)

- **Rumelt:** diagnosis-first, kernel = diagnosis + guiding policy + coherent action, plus "the crux."
- **Helmer:** taxonomic-first — Power exists in exactly seven shapes.
- Rumelt is most useful when the problem is *what is wrong / what is the pivotal challenge*. Helmer is most useful when the problem is *what durable advantage does this business have or could plausibly build*.
- **They are complementary, not competing.** Rumelt tells you the crux; Helmer tells you which Power you're either defending or building toward.

**Compose them:** Rumelt to name the crux; Helmer to name the Power the response must produce.

### vs. Martin's Playing to Win cascade

- **Martin:** five integrated questions (winning aspiration → where to play → how to win → capabilities → management systems). Strategy-*making* cascade.
- **Helmer:** the "how to win" question, unpacked into a specific mechanistic taxonomy with strict tests.
- **Martin's cascade is a strategy-*making* process. Helmer is a strategy-*validation* framework.** Where Martin says "how to win," Helmer forces the answer to name one of seven Powers and prove Benefit + Barrier + 3 S's.
- Martin himself has written that 7 Powers *"are different flavors of 'can't/won't replicate'"* — a useful vocabulary inside the HTW box.

**Compose them:** Martin's cascade to make the strategy; Helmer's taxonomy to validate the "how to win" answer.

### vs. Buffett's "economic moats"

- Helmer explicitly maps to Buffett: *"economic castles [Benefit] protected by unreachable moats [Barrier]."*
- 7 Powers is the operational taxonomy Buffett's metaphor never gave.
- If a user is thinking in "moats," the move is to translate: name which of the seven Powers the moat corresponds to. If it doesn't correspond to any, the "moat" is probably a benefit without a barrier.

### vs. Christensen's disruption theory

- Counter-Positioning is Helmer's more precise reformulation of the "why don't incumbents respond?" question.
- Rather than psychology / innovator's dilemma alone, Helmer isolates *economically rational* self-preservation (collateral damage on existing P&L) as the primary Barrier, with cognitive bias as secondary.
- If a user is using disruption language, 7 Powers Counter-Positioning gives them a tighter mechanism.

### vs. Wardley Mapping

- Wardley is a positioning-over-time map of components in a value chain — useful for anticipating commoditization and inertia.
- 7 Powers is a durability taxonomy that assumes you know your position.
- **Compose them:** Wardley to see where the pieces are moving; 7 Powers to identify which durable Position is available at each stage.

### vs. Blue Ocean Strategy

- Blue Ocean is a search method for uncontested market space — a *how* for finding a new Where-to-Play.
- 7 Powers is silent on how to *find* the space; it tells you whether a given space produces durable returns.
- Blue Ocean often produces Counter-Positioning if the "blue ocean" involves a business model that constrains incumbents' response. Not automatic.

### vs. Jobs-to-be-Done / ODI (Ulwick)

- JTBD/ODI live in segmentation and value-proposition territory — inputs to *what* customers value.
- 7 Powers is silent on customer value discovery; it starts once you know what customers value and asks whether you can capture that value durably.
- Compose them: JTBD/ODI to identify the job; 7 Powers to identify how you'll persistently earn on it.

## Frameworks Helmer himself has NOT built against or dismissed

Unlike Martin (who kills SWOT, OKRs-as-strategy, "execution", RBV) or Rumelt (who attacks "bad strategy" at book length), Helmer does not have a hit-list of frameworks he wants to bury. His register is corrective, not confrontational. He'll note that operational excellence isn't Process Power — but he won't tell you to throw out ops management as a discipline.

The one exception in the spirit of Helmer's teaching: *do not treat "moat" as a first-class concept*. Translate it into 7 Powers vocabulary, or name it as a look-alike that fails the Barrier test.

## Common composition patterns

- **Rumelt → Helmer**: name the crux, then name the Power the response must build.
- **Martin → Helmer**: run the cascade, then validate the HTW answer against the taxonomy.
- **Porter → Helmer**: assess industry attractiveness, then identify which Powers the industry admits.
- **JTBD/ODI → Helmer**: identify the customer job, then assess whether serving it produces Power.
- **Wardley → Helmer**: map components over time, then identify Powers available at each evolution stage.
- **Buffett metaphor → Helmer**: translate "moat" into a specific Power with a specific Barrier.

## When 7 Powers is clearly the wrong tool

- **The company has no product yet.** Use lean discovery.
- **The company has no customers yet.** Use JTBD / continuous discovery.
- **The question is about *values* / *purpose*.** Use whatever framework the user prefers; Helmer explicitly excludes purpose.
- **The question is about *how to invent*.** 7 Powers recognizes invention when it produces Power; it does not tell you how to invent.
- **The question is about *how to change the org*.** Use Kotter / ADKAR / EOS depending on flavor.
- **The user wants a moral or ethical evaluation of the business.** Helmer's Value Axiom is amoral by design.

If any of these are the real question, redirect explicitly rather than forcing 7 Powers.
