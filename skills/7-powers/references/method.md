# 7 Powers — Method

> The canonical description of the 7 Powers in Hamilton Helmer's own terms. Fidelity is the point — Helmer's framework is definitional, and softening the definitions collapses the taxonomy into generic "moat" talk. This file is where the skill goes when it needs the strict version.

## Definitions first

Helmer teaches by *defining before deploying*. Three definitions anchor everything else.

### Strategy

> "A route to continuing Power in significant markets."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers* (2016)

Note what's absent: no mention of choices, no five questions, no crux. Helmer's definition is deliberately narrow. Strategy = getting to a position that produces Persistent Differential Returns, in a market big enough to matter.

### Power

> "The set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers* (2016), repeated across every interview

**Persistent Differential Returns (PDR)** — the outcome that defines Power. Not just profits, not just growth. *Persistent* differential returns — profits above the competitive baseline, sustained over time.

### The Value Axiom

> "Strategy has one and only one objective: maximizing potential fundamental business value."
> — Helmer, *7 Powers* (2016)

This is the disciplining lens. Anything that doesn't move fundamental value — measured in cash flow over time — is out of scope. Helmer's investor training is visible here: no morality, no vision language, no "purpose" — just cash flow expectations.

**Value = Market Size × Power.** Both terms matter multiplicatively. Power in a tiny market ≠ valuable strategy. A big market without Power ≠ valuable strategy.

## The mandatory two-part test — Benefit + Barrier

Every Power must simultaneously exhibit:

- **Benefit** — a material improvement in cash flow relative to competitors, via prices, costs, or investment needs.
- **Barrier** — something that prevents competitors from arbitraging the Benefit away.

**No Barrier → not Power, just a temporary advantage.**

This is the single most important sentence in Helmer's framework. Benefits are common; markets arbitrage them. Barriers are rare; that's why Power is rare. If you can't name the specific mechanism that stops competitors from copying you, you don't have Power — you have a lead.

## The 3 S's — go/no-go screen

Before declaring any Power, run the screen:

- **Superior** — does the claimed advantage improve free cash flow *relative to competitors*?
- **Significant** — is the improvement *material* (not a rounding error)?
- **Sustainable** — is it *largely immune* to competitive arbitrage?

Failing any of the three = not Power. Helmer applies this to founder claims routinely; the failure rate is high.

## The seven

Each Power has a specific Benefit and a specific Barrier. The Barriers do not overlap — that's why the taxonomy holds at exactly seven.

### 1. Scale Economies

**Benefit:** lower per-unit cost at larger scale.
**Barrier:** prohibitive share-gain cost for a smaller rival. To match the incumbent's cost, the challenger would have to buy share at a level that destroys their P&L before scale kicks in.

**Formal expression Helmer uses:** *Surplus Leader Margin* — the accounting expression of Scale Economies, capturing the gap between incumbent and challenger unit economics at prevailing prices.

**Canonical case:** Walmart. Amazon (in retail). Netflix (streaming — content spend amortized across ballooning subscribers).

**Look-alike to reject:** a company that's just bigger. Bigger ≠ Scale Economies. The cost curve has to actually slope down with size *and* the slope has to be steep enough to be prohibitive.

### 2. Network Economies

**Benefit:** the value of the offering to a customer *increases* as more customers use it.
**Barrier:** winner-take-most tipping — once a network passes the tipping point, challengers face a chicken-and-egg problem that scales against them.

**Canonical case:** LinkedIn, Facebook.

**Look-alike to reject — most important gotcha in the framework:** *Network effects ≠ Network Economies.* Effects are near-universal (almost any market has some). Economies require the value increase to be *material* AND to create a sharp, winner-take-most tipping. Helmer, via Chenyi Shi: *"Network effects are basically an attribute of any market... but they're very infrequently material."*

**Sharp strategic implication:** if Network Economies are truly present, the imperative is to *scale much faster than anyone else*. Helmer: *"If one supposes Network Economies then the strategy imperative is to scale much faster than anyone else — if another firm gets to the tipping point before you, then the game is over."*

### 3. Counter-Positioning

**Benefit:** a new, superior business model that the incumbent cannot copy without harming their own existing business.
**Barrier:** *Collateral damage* — the incumbent, acting economically-rationally, cannot copy because doing so would cannibalize their existing P&L faster than the new model can replace it.

**Canonical cases:** Vanguard vs. Fidelity (index funds cannibalize active-management fees). Dell vs. Compaq (direct-to-customer cannibalizes channel). Netflix vs. Blockbuster (streaming cannibalizes late fees + physical stores). Amazon vs. Borders. In-N-Out vs. McDonald's.

**Critical qualifier — Counter-Positioning is *partial* Power:** it protects only against incumbents constrained by their existing business. It does NOT protect against fresh entrants copying the new model. This is why Counter-Positioning is stage-specific (see Dynamics).

**The 5 stages of incumbent response** (predictable, economically-rational — not a failure of vision):
1. **Denial** — "that's not a real market."
2. **Ridicule** — "no one will pay for that."
3. **Fear** — quiet task forces, competing pilots.
4. **Anger** — public defensive campaigns, price wars.
5. **Capitulation** — either acquire, imitate too late, or exit.

Helmer's principle for challengers: *"The only bet worthwhile for a challenger is one in which even if the incumbent plays its best game, it can be taken off the board."*

### 4. Switching Costs

**Benefit:** existing customers pay a premium (or accept worse terms) rather than face the cost or hassle of switching.
**Barrier:** the same cost/hassle applies to competitors trying to poach — they must overcome it too.

**Canonical case:** Adobe (Helmer's own consulting client — Creative Suite and later Creative Cloud lock-in).

**Critical qualifier:** Switching Costs only accrue on the *next* purchase from *existing* customers. They provide no benefit with new customers, and they don't apply to non-repeat purchases. A one-time-purchase category cannot be defended with Switching Costs.

### 5. Branding

**Benefit:** willingness-to-pay a premium for an offering that is *objectively identical* to competitors'.
**Barrier:** *Hysteresis* — the premium can only be built through long, path-dependent action; the brand can't be bought, and it can't be built quickly.

**Look-alike to reject:** *Brand recognition ≠ Brand Power.* Being known is table stakes. Power requires *commanding a premium* for the same underlying good. Helmer's canonical contrast: Coca-Cola brand vs. supermarket cola — buyers pay more for a functionally identical liquid.

**Two enabling conditions Helmer flags:** (a) *affective valence* — the brand has to trigger a feeling (safety, status, belonging); (b) *uncertainty reduction* — buyers use the brand as a heuristic when they can't easily evaluate the product themselves.

### 6. Cornered Resource

**Benefit:** preferential access to a coveted resource that independently produces superior value.
**Barrier:** *Fiat* — the exclusivity is decreed (legal, physical, or structural) rather than earned through interaction.

**Canonical cases:** Pixar's "Brain Trust" as an idiosyncratic, non-arbitragable creative process. Bausch & Lomb's spin-casting soft contact lens tech. ASML for EUV lithography (applied case, not Helmer's).

**Five screens Cornered Resource must pass:**
1. **Idiosyncratic** — the resource is distinctive to this firm.
2. **Non-arbitraged** — cannot be bid away in an efficient market.
3. **Transferable** — the resource actually applies here and produces the benefit.
4. **Ongoing** — the resource keeps producing (not one-shot).
5. **Sufficient** — the resource alone is enough for the outcome.

**Most common misapplication:** claiming a **leadership team** as Cornered Resource. Leadership teams almost never pass sufficiency (a team is one input among many) and transferability (great teams have failed in different contexts). Helmer explicitly cautions against this.

### 7. Process Power

**Benefit:** superior cost or quality through embedded operations that competitors have not been able to replicate even when the process is fully visible.
**Barrier:** *Hysteresis* (long build) + *causal ambiguity* (competitors can't decompose which elements produce the outcome).

**Formal expression Helmer uses:**
> "Process Power equals operational excellence, plus hysteresis."

**Canonical case:** Toyota Production System. Documented in extreme detail, taught in business schools, published in books — and competitors have not fully replicated it. **NUMMI** (the GM–Toyota joint venture where GM had inside access to TPS and still couldn't propagate it) is the standing evidence that Process Power is Power, not just documentation.

**Look-alike to reject — the most common misapplication in the entire framework:** *Operational excellence ≠ Process Power.* Excellence without hysteresis is table stakes. Helmer, Lenny 2024:
> "You're on a treadmill and if you stop running on that treadmill, you get creamed, but it's not Power. The things that drive operational excellence can be mimicked."

## Statics vs. Dynamics — the single most misunderstood distinction

- **Statics** — how *having* Power creates value. Diagnostic. Easy in a mature company.
- **Dynamics** — how *getting* Power happens. Requires invention. Not planable in advance.

Helmer:
> "'Getting there' (Dynamics) is completely different from 'being there' (Statics)."

**Consequence:** frameworks that only describe Statics (Porter) are diagnostic-only. 7 Powers is dual — the taxonomy is for Statics; the three-stage sequencing (below) is the theory of invention for Dynamics.

**Core Dynamics claim:** *"Invention is the mother of Power."* Power arises from invention (product, business-model, or process) executed into an available window. Planning does not create Power.

## Sequencing / three-stage business phase model (post-2016 refinement)

Every Power lives in a specific business phase. Missing the window closes the door.

### Origination (pre-takeoff)

**Barrier available:** Collateral Damage (Counter-Positioning) or Fiat (Cornered Resource).
**Powers available:** Counter-Positioning + Cornered Resource.
**Investor's read:** invention is the only lever. You're building the model or securing the resource. No scale, no network, no switching, no brand, no process — those don't exist yet at meaningful volume.

### Takeoff (explosive growth)

**Barrier available:** Share Gain Cost-Benefit.
**Powers available:** Scale Economies + Network Economies + Switching Costs.
**Investor's read:** the window to lock in scale/network/switching is *now*, and it closes when the market cools. If you don't scale faster than rivals during Takeoff, the door shuts for good.

### Stability (post-explosive; typically <30–40% market growth)

**Barrier available:** Hysteresis.
**Powers available:** Branding + Process Power.
**Investor's read:** these emerge only from *sustained action over years*. Neither can be built during Takeoff — they require the slow accumulation Hysteresis names.

### Implication

If you missed the window for a stage, that Power is off the table for you. Read: don't try to build Network Economies after the takeoff has ended in someone else's favor. Don't claim Branding Power on a two-year-old company. Don't claim Process Power on a company that hasn't operated the process for a decade.

## Combining Powers

Helmer treats Powers as **additive/compounding**, not mutually exclusive. The canonical Netflix sequence, as Helmer teaches it:

- **Origination (~2007–2012):** Counter-Positioning vs. Blockbuster (streaming cannibalizes retail rentals + late fees).
- **Takeoff (~2013–2020):** Scale Economies (originals content spend amortized across a ballooning global subscriber base) + Branding beginning to emerge.
- **Stability (2020s):** Branding solidified.

Applied case (not Helmer's own): Nvidia — Cornered Resource (CUDA IP + ecosystem) + Switching Costs (developer investment) + Scale Economies (chip R&D amortization).

## Value Moment

The point at which uncertainty about market size and Power radically diminishes — a step-change in cash-flow transparency. This is what Strategy Capital's investing method hunts for. In practical terms: the moment when the Benefit + Barrier are both provable, and the market's size is no longer speculative.

## Common misapplications summary (see `heuristics.md` for the full list)

- Operational excellence ≠ Process Power.
- Network effects ≠ Network Economies.
- Brand recognition ≠ Brand Power.
- Data scale is not automatically Power.
- Speed / execution is not Power.
- Leadership team is almost never Cornered Resource.
- Counter-Positioning is *partial* Power (only vs. constrained incumbents).
- Switching Costs only accrue on the *next* purchase from *existing* customers.

## What this method is NOT

Helmer is emphatic about the negative definition. This method is NOT:

- **A "moat" metaphor.** Buffett's moat metaphor is beautiful but non-operational. 7 Powers is the operational taxonomy the metaphor never gave.
- **A prescriptive playbook.** Helmer does not tell you *how* to invent. He gives you a taxonomy for recognizing invention when it produces Power, and a stage-map for knowing which Power is even available.
- **A general strategy framework.** Helmer does not cover mission, purpose, culture, org design, execution. He covers *durable competitive economics*. Everything else is out of scope by design.
- **A ranking system.** The 7 Powers are not ordered by strength. Different Powers dominate in different contexts.
- **Static.** The framework itself has been refined (Dynamics, three-stage sequencing, Second Invention) — but the seven remain seven.
