# Good Strategy Bad Strategy — Method

> The canonical description of Rumelt's method in his own terms. Fidelity is the point — the method is opinionated, and softening any element collapses it into the "bad strategy" Rumelt spends most of his time critiquing.

## Definition

Rumelt's operating definition:

> "A strategy is a design for overcoming a high-stakes challenge. It's a mixture of policy and action designed to deal with a challenge."
> — Richard Rumelt, *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (2011), reaffirmed in *The Crux* (2022) and repeated across podcasts.

The word "challenge" is load-bearing. Strategy exists to respond to a specific challenge; without a diagnosed challenge, there is no strategy — only goals dressed up in strategic-sounding language.

## The kernel — three elements, all required

Rumelt's kernel is his central contribution. All three must be present. If any one is missing, what you have is not strategy — it is aspiration, wish, or plan.

### 1. Diagnosis

**What it is:** an understanding of the situation. What is the challenge, structurally? *Why is it hard?*

**Rumelt's own words:**
> "All three elements have to be there... if any one is missing, something's wrong. It's not really a strategy, it's something else."

**Key move — repeat "why is that hard?" until you hit bedrock.** Clients arrive with what they think is the problem. Rumelt models the diagnostic move by asking *"why is that hard?"* over and over. Most executives circle the actual pivotal issue without naming it directly. The diagnosis names it.

**What diagnosis is NOT:**
- A restatement of the problem in aspirational terms ("we need to grow faster").
- A list of symptoms without a mechanism ("our conversion is dropping and NPS is falling").
- Fluff — "our diagnosis is that we need to be more customer-centric." That's the anti-diagnosis.

**Author's words:**
> "We jumped immediately to therefore, we're going to have more people go to college... Having more people go to college doesn't solve the problem of 15 year olds not being able to do elementary math."

### 2. Guiding policy

**What it is:** the overall response to the diagnosis. A general direction for how you'll deal with the challenge. Not a goal, not a plan, not a mission statement — a **policy**.

**Rumelt's own words:**
> "How are we going to deal with it?"

**Key distinction:** guiding policy is neither the diagnosis (which is a *reading* of the situation) nor coherent action (which is *doing*). It's the connective tissue — the general approach that translates diagnosis into action.

**What guiding policy is NOT:**
- Goals ("achieve 30% market share").
- Values ("we will operate with integrity").
- A list of priorities ("focus on these 7 things").
- Ambition ("become the #1 platform").

### 3. Coherent action

**What it is:** the specific set of actions that carry out the guiding policy AND reinforce each other. Coherent, not merely non-contradictory.

**Rumelt's own words:**
> "The most important part of a strategy, the part that it's so easy to leave out because people like to think of strategy as this high level conceptual thing, is the coherent action."

**Key distinction — coherence ≠ absence of contradiction.** Coherent action means the actions *reinforce* each other, like Southwest Airlines' choices all support every other choice. Nokia's problem wasn't that its actions contradicted; it was that they were a scattered set of parallel initiatives that didn't reinforce anything.

**Author's words:**
> "You shouldn't do things that fight each other. You shouldn't say, 'Oh, we are going to burn less oil and at the same time we're going to import more oil.'"

**Southwest as the canonical coherence case:** single aircraft type + point-to-point routes + secondary airports + non-union labor + short turnarounds + unbundled service. Each choice reinforces every other. Competitors can't copy the system piecemeal — they'd have to become a different airline.

## The Crux — Rumelt's 2022 extension

The Crux (2022) added a filter *before* diagnosis: **find the crux**.

**Definition:**
> "The crux is the most important part of a set of challenges that is addressable and has a good chance of being solved by coherent action."

Three judgments in one:
1. **What matters most** — from the many things you face, which is pivotal?
2. **What is solvable** — of the pivotal things, which can you actually address?
3. **Where to concentrate** — the intersection of those two is where your energy goes.

**Rock-climbing metaphor:** the crux is the pivotal pitch of a climb — the one hard move on which the entire climb turns. Miss it and the summit is unreachable no matter how well you climb the rest.

**Why the crux matters:** a pure importance filter yields wish lists (top-10 problems, unweighted for solvability). A pure addressability filter yields busywork (easy things that don't matter). The intersection is where strategy actually gets made.

**How to find the crux:**
1. **WWHTBT test.** Run through every condition the strategy relies on. The crux is the pivotal condition you are *least* confident in.
2. **The elimination question:** *"If we solved everything else but not this, would the strategy still fail?"* If yes — you found it.
3. **The "why is that hard?" question, repeated.** The crux is structural, not emotional. Circle the paradox until you can name it directly.
4. **Listen for what people avoid.** Executives will circle the central paradox without stating it. The strategist's job is to name what everyone senses but refuses to make explicit.

**Sub-skills of the strategist** (per Rumelt on Strategy Skills Podcast Ep. 253):
> "Figuring out what is critical; constraining what is critical vs. what is controllable; focusing energy on that."

## Sequence of application

Rumelt does not prescribe a rigid sequence. In practice, his Strategy Foundry method (see `post-book.md`) unfolds this way:

1. Ground in context — what's changing, where will you be in 3-5 years without intervention.
2. Surface challenges openly — 8-10+ candidates, deferred judgment.
3. Cluster challenges — fuzzy edges OK.
4. Filter by importance × addressability — this is the crux-finding move.
5. Reframe using analogies, comparisons, explicit assumptions.
6. Ask "why is that hard?" for each remaining candidate.
7. Isolate the crux.
8. Design guiding policy + coherent actions.
9. Force one-sentence recommendations ("instant strategy").
10. Red-team by role-playing competitors.
11. Commit publicly ("swearing in").

## What this method is NOT

Rumelt is emphatic about the negative definition. This method is NOT:

- **Goal-setting.** *"Bad strategy is long on goals and short on policy or action."*
- **A vision-mission-values exercise.** Explicitly named as one of three root causes of bad strategy. Template-style thinking that "avoids the rigor of genuine analytical work and sidesteps difficult questions."
- **Financial forecasting or planning.** *"Prediction ≠ strategy."* "He who forecasts the future lies, even if he tells the truth."
- **A list of priorities.** *"The word priority means first."* A list of 12 "priorities" has zero.
- **Fluff.** *"A restatement of the obvious combined with a generous sprinkling of buzzwords that masquerade as expertise."*
- **A KPI cascade.** OKRs and BSCs are execution tools; they align *after* strategy exists, they don't create strategy.
- **A framework-fill exercise.** Running SWOT / Porter / BCG mechanically without diagnosis produces cargo-cult strategy.

**Rumelt's canonical fluff example:**
> Bank strategy: *"Our fundamental strategy is one of customer-centric intermediation."*
> Rumelt's translation: *"In other words, it is a bank."*

## The updated framing — "action agenda"

Post-Crux, Rumelt has been increasingly explicit that the word "strategy" itself is now so corroded (used for budgets, forecasts, aspirations, values) that it hurts more than it helps. He's proposed a reframe:

> "Replace the term strategy altogether and just call it an 'action agenda.'" — Lenny 2024

**Practical implication:** organizations can keep their public "Standard Narrative" (values, aspirations, long-term positioning) for stakeholders — but leadership works from a **separate, focused Action Agenda** that drives the actual week-to-week work.

**Test:** if what you wrote could be read aloud at an all-hands and no one could tell what you'd do differently on Monday, it's not an action agenda.

Whether the user calls it "strategy" or "action agenda", the same kernel applies: diagnosis + guiding policy + coherent action, aimed at the crux.
