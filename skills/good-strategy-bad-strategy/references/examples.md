## Nelson at Trafalgar — coherence as strategic asset

**Where Rumelt uses it:** Opening chapter of *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* (2011). [Thinkers50: How Nelson Did It](https://thinkers50.com/blog/how-nelson-did-it/). Referenced across podcasts.

**The situation:** Nelson was outnumbered 33 ships to 27 by the combined Franco-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar (1805).

**How Rumelt describes the kernel applied:**
- **Diagnosis:** the English had more experienced captains but were structurally at a numerical disadvantage. The standard formation (line-astern parallel to the enemy) would produce a fair fight in which numbers would win.
- **Guiding policy:** break the enemy's coherence. If the fleet fought as coordinated whole, England lost. If it fought as scattered ships, English captains' individual skill dominated.
- **Coherent action:** two columns attacking perpendicular to the enemy line, sacrificing lead ships to break the formation, then letting the melee that followed advantage individual captain skill.

**Author's words:**
> "Nelson's challenge was that he was outnumbered. His strategy was to risk his lead ships in order to break the coherence of his enemy's fleet. With coherence lost, he judged, the more experienced English captains would come out on top in the ensuing melee."

**Why the case is instructive:** the kernel is visible whole. Diagnosis names the actual mechanism (numerical vs. coherence); guiding policy specifies the leverage point (break coherence); coherent action executes it with specific choices.

## SpaceX — the audacious leap on the crux

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022), post-Crux Substack essays.

**The situation:** launch costs were the industry's binding constraint. Standard approach: better materials, better engines, incremental cost reduction.

**The crux (Rumelt's diagnosis):** the pivotal constraint was reusability, not marginal cost per component. If you couldn't reuse the vehicle, no incremental improvement could break the cost floor.

**The audacious leap:** rather than reinvent thermal-tile ablative reentry (industry orthodoxy since Apollo), use *more fuel* to slow re-entry and soft-land tail-first. Cheaper to burn extra fuel than to reengineer materials.

**Why the case is instructive:** the answer required identifying that the crux wasn't the obvious candidate (materials) — it was reusability. Once diagnosed correctly, the coherent action (fuel-based landing) was radical but obvious.

## Nvidia — kernel exemplar

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022), podcasts.

**The situation:** Nvidia was losing the graphics-performance race in the early 2000s.

**Kernel applied:**
- **Diagnosis:** "we are losing the performance race because others are improving faster."
- **Guiding policy:** release a faster chip at 3× the industry cadence.
- **Coherent actions:** 3 parallel development teams (each on a different generation), dedicated simulation facilities, coordinated driver-control system.

**Why the case is instructive:** every element is visible. Diagnosis is structural (not a wish); guiding policy specifies the leverage point (cadence); actions reinforce (three teams parallel, sim facilities, drivers).

## Netflix — crux-diagnosed response, not vision-driven

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022) — one of the book's featured cases.

**The situation (~2018):** Netflix's licensing model was becoming untenable. Studios (Disney, Warner, others) were pulling content to launch their own streaming services.

**The crux (Rumelt's diagnosis):** "Netflix could no longer count on contracting for existing good TV and studio films at reasonable prices." Not a vision problem — a supply problem.

**Coherent action:** invest heavily in original content. This is often narrated as visionary — Rumelt insists it was the *response to a diagnosed crux*, not a vision-driven bet.

**Why the case is instructive:** distinguishes "visionary leader" narratives from "correctly diagnosed challenge" reality. Rumelt uses this case to attack vision-first storytelling.

## Southwest — coherent action as reinforcing system

**Where Rumelt uses it:** Referenced across GSBS and The Crux. Also central to Michael Porter's *"What Is Strategy?"* (HBR, 1996), which Rumelt cites approvingly.

**The situation:** structurally the only US airline earning cost of capital consistently over decades.

**Coherent action as system:** non-union labor + high aircraft utilization + short turnarounds + secondary airports + unbundled service + single aircraft type (737) + point-to-point routes (not hub-and-spoke) + online-first booking. **Every element reinforces every other.**

**Why the case is instructive:** competitors cannot copy piecemeal — adopting one element doesn't work because the leverage comes from the *system*. Delta's attempts at low-cost point-to-point failed because the rest of Delta's operation couldn't be dismantled to match.

## Nokia — the 17-priorities pathology + inertia

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS + The Crux + Lenny 2024.

**The situation:** dominant global mobile-phone maker in the 2000s. Faced Apple's iPhone (2007) and Android's rise. Failed to respond.

**Why they failed (Rumelt's diagnosis):** matrix structure + hundreds of parallel initiatives + culture that dismissed bad news + no single decision-maker on smartphone response = paralysis. Nokia had the resources; it lacked the coherence.

**Why the case is instructive:** the "17 priorities" pathology at scale. Nokia is Rumelt's go-to example whenever an executive describes a long priority list as strategy.

## Salesforce — sequential cruxes

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022) + podcasts.

**The situation:** SaaS was untested when Salesforce launched (1999).

**Sequential cruxes:**
- **Crux 1:** attract developers. Response: the "No Software" campaign — build ecosystem, force-of-hand developer mindshare.
- **Crux 2 (once crux 1 solved):** get companies to adopt. Response: free tier for small users, drop the enterprise-first orthodoxy.

**Why the case is instructive:** demonstrates that strategy is often not one big move — it's the correct sequencing of cruxes. Solving one opens the next.

## Amgen 1990 — dispersion of focus

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS.

**The situation:** Amgen in 1990 was running 5,000 simultaneous research projects.

**Why it hurt:** scale advantages evaporated because no single project got enough focused resources to break out.

**Why the case is instructive:** dispersion of resources = loss of the very advantage the scale was supposed to provide.

## Vietnam War — null-set strategy

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS (Ch. 5).

**The situation:** US strategy in Vietnam had four simultaneous objectives: (a) avoid losing, (b) avoid a major war, (c) preserve US reputation, (d) preserve the South Vietnamese government.

**Why it failed:** the intersection of feasible actions satisfying all four goals was empty. Any concrete action satisfied some goals and violated others. Result: indecision, escalation-de-escalation cycles, no coherent action possible.

**Why the case is instructive:** null-set strategies are common in business too — companies with conflicting KPIs (grow AND cut costs AND raise quality AND move faster) produce the same paralysis at smaller scale.

## WeWork — engineered growth ≠ strategy

**Where Rumelt uses it:** post-Crux Substack + podcasts.

**The situation:** WeWork grew revenue explosively via massive capital raises, then imploded.

**Why the case is instructive:** growth engineered by investor money isn't strategy — it's capital allocation. Real strategic growth comes from a diagnosed capability or demand advantage. WeWork had neither; it had a real estate arbitrage plus a growth narrative.

## Space Shuttle Challenger — chain-link failure

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022) + Substack chain-link essay.

**The situation:** the 1986 Challenger disaster was caused by O-ring failure at low launch temperatures.

**Chain-link lesson:** every other component of the shuttle could be world-class, and the whole vehicle would still fail because the weakest link determined the outcome. Investing more in the strong links wouldn't have helped.

**Why the case is instructive:** dramatic version of the chain-link principle. Applies to any system where output requires coordination of parts.

## 1970s GM — chain-link in commerce

**Where Rumelt uses it:** Substack chain-link essay.

**The situation:** 1970s GM produced high-quality transmissions housed in cars with rattling dashboards, weak paint, inconsistent assembly.

**Chain-link lesson:** the customer experienced the weakest link. Quality transmissions were wasted investment because the overall product quality was determined by the worst component.

## Sprint-Nextel — the "synergies" fiction

**Where Rumelt uses it:** post-Crux Substack.

**The situation:** Sprint acquired Nextel in 2005 for $35B, sold for a $30B write-down years later.

**Why it failed:** the "synergies" that justified the price were incompatible technology (CDMA vs. iDEN), incompatible customer bases, incompatible cultures. None of the promised integration ever produced value.

**Why the case is instructive:** Rumelt uses this whenever an executive invokes "synergies" as strategic justification for M&A. His counter: request specifics; usually a contract suffices.

## Intel — anchored to yesterday's crux

**Where Rumelt uses it:** post-Crux Substack + podcasts.

**The situation:** Intel dominated the CPU era. Failed to adapt as mobile (low-power ARM) and AI (GPUs, NVIDIA) shifted the industry.

**Why it failed:** Intel's crux for 20+ years was "make faster CPUs." That solved crux became the wrong crux; the industry needed low-power and parallel computing. Intel's org, capital allocation, and identity were anchored to yesterday's diagnosis.

**Why the case is instructive:** solved cruxes create their own inertia. Rumelt's post-Crux writing emphasizes that the strategist has to re-diagnose regularly, not just execute past diagnoses well.

## CIA / WMD Iraq — confirmation bias in diagnosis

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS + The Crux.

**The situation:** US intelligence in the early 2000s concluded Iraq had WMDs. Post-invasion investigation found none.

**Why the case is instructive:** the diagnostic process was corrupted by confirmation bias — intelligence was fitted to a pre-existing conclusion. Rumelt uses this whenever discussing how diagnosis can go wrong. The lesson: the strategist must actively look for evidence *against* the current diagnosis.

## IBM's "bear hug" — using structural power

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS.

**The situation:** IBM in the 1960s-70s used its dominant position with large corporations to keep them within IBM's ecosystem — the "bear hug" strategy.

**Why the case is instructive:** an example of guiding policy using existing structural power. The policy was recognizable: "we win by making it painful to leave the IBM relationship." Actions reinforced (integrated hardware/software/services, deep account teams, high switching costs).

## Rockefeller / Standard Oil — value denial

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS.

**The situation:** Rockefeller drove kerosene prices from $1 to 10 cents, destroying competitors but delivering massive value to customers.

**Why the case is instructive:** value denial (delivering something at a price competitors can't match) as a strategic move. Fits Rumelt's category of coherent action that reinforces itself: cost efficiency → lower prices → volume → more cost efficiency.

## I.M. Pei's Louvre pyramid — focus on the actual crux

**Where Rumelt uses it:** *The Crux* (2022).

**The situation:** Pei's Louvre expansion faced years of aesthetic controversy about the glass pyramid entrance.

**Why the case is instructive:** the aesthetic debate was surface. The crux was functional — the Louvre needed a new central entrance to handle visitor flow. Pei's focus on the functional crux (regardless of the aesthetic noise) is Rumelt's exemplar of not confusing what people complain about with the actual pivotal issue.

## Lehman Brothers — counterpoint to Nelson

**Where Rumelt uses it:** occasional Substack references.

**Why the case is instructive:** Rumelt uses Lehman as the counter to Nelson — an example of an organization whose actions did NOT cohere. Excessive leverage + concentrated exposures + no coherent risk management = collapse. Structurally the opposite of Nelson's coherent-action-under-disadvantage.

---

## International Harvester — profit projections concealing the labor-relations crux

**Where Rumelt uses it:** GSBS.

**Why the case is instructive:** Harvester's boards regularly reviewed profit projections that assumed labor relations wouldn't deteriorate — even as the real crux was exactly the labor relations problem the projections skipped. Cautionary example of how quantitative frames can hide the crux.
