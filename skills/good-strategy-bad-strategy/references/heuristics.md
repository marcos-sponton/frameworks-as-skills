# Good Strategy Bad Strategy — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> Rumelt's practical devices — the operational moves that separate a real kernel of strategy from bad strategy dressed in strategic-sounding language. Attribution is precise: this comes from GSBS (2011), this from The Crux (2022), this from post-Crux Substack, this from a 2024 podcast.

## Do's

### Start with the challenge, not the goal

Financial targets drive accounting behavior, not strategy. Rumelt's opening move with any client is to reframe the conversation from *"what do we want to achieve"* to *"what challenge do we face."*

**How to apply:** if the user opens with a target ("we need to grow 40%"), redirect: *what's the challenge that makes growing 40% hard?* If they can name the challenge, you have the beginning of a diagnosis. If they can't, the target is aspirational, not strategic.

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 5) + repeated across all podcast appearances.

### Repeat "why is that hard?" until you hit bedrock

Rumelt's most-repeated operational move. Clients arrive with a stated problem. The stated problem is rarely the crux. Ask *"why is that hard?"* about the stated problem, then about the answer, then about that answer.

**Author's words:**
> "What makes it hard? Client will tell me, 'Oh, we want to open up business in Australia'... I'll say, 'Okay, so why are you and I talking about that?'"

**Source:** Lenny 2024, The Crux (2022).

### Use "what about that plan is difficult?" as an antidote to success theater

Executives default to describing their plan as if it's already working. The direct counter is to ask what's difficult about it. Silence or hand-waving is data — it usually means they haven't diagnosed.

**Source:** Rumelt Substack ("Becoming a Strategist" series).

### Force ranking — top 10, then narrow

*"Make a list of the 10 most important things you can do to improve the business."* The exercise forces the important × doable intersection to become visible. From the top 10, filter to top 2-3. The rest are noise.

**Source:** GSBS + Foundry process description.

### Write it plain

*"Good strategy almost always looks simple and obvious and does not take thick PowerPoint slides."* If it requires 60 slides to explain, it's not a strategy — it's an obfuscation.

**Source:** GSBS.

### Say no

*"Good strategy requires leaders willing to say no to a wide variety of actions and interests."* Strategy is choice, and choice is refusal.

**Source:** GSBS.

### Look for asymmetry — where you have power that competitors don't

Rumelt's canonical list of power sources: reputation, network effects, specialized knowledge, information asymmetry, position, efficiency, system management. Wherever you have asymmetric access to one of these, that's where your action agenda should concentrate.

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 8-9), *The Crux* (Ch. 3-4).

### Sequence, don't stack

Strategy is iterative. Climb one ledge, reassess, climb the next. Salesforce's evolution is the canonical case — first "attract developers" (the No Software campaign), then "get companies to adopt" (free tier for small users). Each solved crux opens the next.

**Source:** The Crux (2022) + Rumelt Substack.

### Read biographies and history

*"If you don't have access to other times and other places and other things that have happened, it's very hard to think strategically... A lot of it is based on analogy to previous human experience."* Rumelt personally researches Great Depression newspapers and military history.

**Source:** Lenny 2024.

### Build implementation coherence — actions must reinforce, not merely avoid contradicting

The Southwest test: does every action support every other action? If your actions are non-contradictory but not mutually reinforcing, you have a plan, not a strategy.

**Source:** GSBS + The Crux.

### Have one decider

Distributed strategic decisions paralyze execution. Nokia is the cautionary example — matrix structure + hundreds of parallel initiatives + no single owner of the smartphone response = paralysis.

**Source:** GSBS + Lenny 2024.

### Learn by doing — respect the operator's knowledge

The best strategists come from hands-on operational experience. Sam Walton tested the popcorn machine before executing discount retail. Kroc mastered ops before systematizing franchising. Deep operational knowledge is a prerequisite for good diagnosis.

**Author's words:**
> "The senior executives know pretty much everything you need to know to do this... The basic issues, the challenges you're facing, they know that. It's not mysterious."

**Source:** GSBS + Lenny 2024.

## Don'ts / Anti-patterns (Rumelt's "bad strategy")

### Don't confuse goals with strategy

*"Bad strategy is long on goals and short on policy or action."*

**Example Rumelt uses:** "A set of profit goals or performance goals... Ambitions are not a strategy."

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 3-4).

### Don't publish a list of "priorities"

*"The word priority means first."* A client with 12 "priorities" has none. Air-traffic-controller analogy: *"You don't want to hear the air traffic controller say they're giving priority on a runway to a list of airplanes."*

**Source:** GSBS + Lenny 2024.

### Don't produce fluff

Rumelt's canonical example: a bank whose stated strategy was *"customer-centric intermediation."* His translation: *"In other words, it is a bank."*

**Definition:**
> "Fluff is a restatement of the obvious combined with a generous sprinkling of buzzwords that masquerade as expertise."

Companion term: **word salad.**

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 4).

### Don't pursue growth AND profit as parallel objectives

They fight each other. *"Healthy growth is not engineered — it is the outcome of growing demand for special capabilities or of expanded capabilities and superior products and skills."* WeWork = engineered growth funded by investor money = not sustainable.

**Author's words:**
> "Most companies have strategic goals of increasing growth and increasing profit. Growth and profit, they battle each other... If you're going to increase growth and increase return on equity, how do you do that?"

**Source:** GSBS + Lenny 2024.

### Don't confuse OKRs with strategy

*"OKRs are useful for aligning execution once a strategy exists. Without the underlying strategic logic, OKRs are just goals with key results, and goals without diagnosis are wishes."*

**Source:** Rumelt Substack.

### Don't do vision → mission → values as your strategy template

Explicitly named by Rumelt as one of three root causes of bad strategy: template-style thinking that "avoids the rigor of genuine analytical work and sidesteps difficult questions."

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 3).

### Don't pursue null-set strategies

Bundles of ambitions that can't all be satisfied by any feasible action. Vietnam War as the historical case: avoid losing + avoid major war + preserve reputation + preserve local government → indecision, no coherent action possible because the goals themselves conflict.

**Source:** GSBS (Ch. 5).

### Don't spread resources thin

Nokia syndrome: hundreds of initiatives to keep internal factions happy = loss of competitive focus. Amgen 1990: 5,000 simultaneous research projects undermined scale advantages.

**Source:** GSBS + The Crux + Lenny 2024.

### Don't chase bright shiny objects

Managerial fashions that skip diagnosis. Every year has a new "must-do" (Six Sigma, digital transformation, AI-first, etc.). Adopting them without diagnosis is cargo-cult strategy.

**Source:** GSBS.

### Don't blame the market when the crux is inside the building

*"A lot of their problems are actually themselves."* Post-Crux, Rumelt leans harder on internal capability/politics as the true blocker of most bad strategies.

**Source:** The Crux + Rumelt Substack.

### Don't fake coherence

Non-contradictory ≠ mutually reinforcing. A plan where actions merely don't fight each other is not a strategy; a strategy is one where actions actively support each other (Southwest).

**Source:** GSBS.

### Don't let the "blob" grow

Shield new growth businesses from bureaucratic corporate structures. The core organization's overhead and process discipline will smother the new thing before it can find its own footing.

**Source:** Rumelt Substack.

### Don't fight Bertrand competition

Undifferentiated price-cutting has no strategic content. If your only move is to be cheaper, you're not doing strategy — you're doing an auction.

**Source:** GSBS.

### Don't succumb to "success theater"

Preferring good news over honest diagnosis. Meetings that manufacture the appearance of strategic activity without producing action.

**Source:** Rumelt Substack.

### Don't use frameworks mechanically

5 Forces, SWOT, BCG Matrix — all generate insight when the analyst thinks. Used mechanically they produce cargo-cult strategy. The framework is a prompt to think, not a form to fill in.

**Source:** GSBS + The Crux.

### Don't let the "90-Day Derby" override long-term strategy

Quarterly earnings tyranny. Every serious strategy takes multiple quarters to bear fruit; quarterly optimization guarantees you'll never make one.

**Source:** Rumelt Substack.

### Don't get seduced by M&A "synergies"

*"When an investment banker claims 'economies of scale' for geographically separated operations, request specifics. A contract often suffices instead of a massive merger."* Sprint-Nextel = $30B write-down = the synergies fiction.

**Source:** Rumelt Substack.

### Don't mistake current earnings for strategy

Today's profits reflect past investments. What you're doing now determines the profits 3-5 years from now, not today's P&L.

**Source:** GSBS.

### Don't change the strategy while keeping the org that failed to execute the last one

*"It takes more than the wolf at the door — often the wolf has to be in the cabin and biting legs."* Real strategic change usually requires org change too, and org change requires enough pain to make people accept the disruption.

**Source:** GSBS + Rumelt Substack.

### Don't hire a consultant when you need leadership

*"The senior executives know pretty much everything you need to know to do this... The basic issues, the challenges you're facing, they know that. It's not mysterious."* If the answer isn't emerging, it's usually a courage/politics issue, not an information issue.

**Source:** Lenny 2024.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The crux is confused with what people complain about most

The crux is structural — the condition on which the strategy actually turns. What people complain about most is often emotional or political, not structural. Diagnose past the noise.

### The diagnosis restates the problem in aspirational language

"Our diagnosis is that we need to be more customer-centric." That's not a diagnosis; it's a wish. A real diagnosis says *why* it's hard — the specific mechanism producing the problem.

### The guiding policy sounds like a goal

*"Our guiding policy is to achieve 30% market share."* That's a goal, not a policy. A guiding policy would be more like *"we will win via low-cost distribution in tier-2 cities where incumbents structurally can't compete on price."*

### Coherent action is confused with a project plan

A project plan lists tasks; coherent action is a system of choices where each choice reinforces the others. The Southwest test.

### Force ranking finds a fake top-3

Teams choose the top 3 they *feel comfortable* addressing, not the 3 that actually matter most. Rumelt's counter: the crux is usually the item people avoid discussing, not the item they gravitate toward.

### Frameworks used to justify pre-existing conclusions

Any framework — kernel included — can be run to validate a decision already made. Watch for this: if the team's answer at hour 6 of the workshop is exactly the answer someone brought in at hour 1, you didn't do strategy — you did rationalization.

## Pro tips (accelerators — small devices that punch above their weight)

### "Instant strategy" — force a one-sentence recommendation

At any point in a diagnosis, force the team to compress into one sentence: *"Given what we've said, here's what we should do..."* If they can't compress, the diagnosis isn't converged yet.

**Source:** Foundry process.

### Red-team by role-playing competitors

Have someone in the room defend the competitor's likely response. If your strategy assumes the competitor stands still, it's not a strategy — it's a wish.

**Source:** Foundry process + GSBS.

### The "swearing in" ritual

At the end of a strategy session, participants make verbal commitment to implement. Symbolic but load-bearing — it converts diagnosis into personal accountability.

**Source:** Foundry process.

### Use analogies from outside the industry

Rumelt personally reaches for military history (Nelson at Trafalgar), industrial engineering (chain-link logic), historical crises (Great Depression newspapers) to defamiliarize business problems. Analogies from inside your industry produce best-practices thinking; analogies from outside produce insight.

**Source:** GSBS + The Crux.

### The Nelson move — coherent action against a stronger opponent

Nelson at Trafalgar was outnumbered 33-27. His strategy: break his fleet into two columns and attack perpendicularly, betting his lead ships to disrupt the enemy's coherence. *"With coherence lost, he judged, the more experienced English captains would come out on top in the ensuing melee."* Use this analog whenever the user is fighting a resource-advantaged opponent.

**Source:** GSBS opening chapter + Thinkers50.

## Common misapplications (people who think they're doing good strategy but aren't)

### "Strategic plan" = 3-year rolling budget + market-share projection

Called "strategy" by convention. Does zero strategy work.

### KPI + OKR cascade without a diagnosed challenge

*"All results and no action."* You know the numbers you want but you haven't diagnosed what's stopping you from producing them.

### Growth engineered via acquisition

Not strategy — a portfolio maneuver that rarely creates value. Real growth comes from diagnosed capability or demand advantage, not from stapling companies together.

### Executive off-site producing a "vision, mission, values" deck

Rumelt calls this "success theater" — feels productive, changes nothing.

### The "17 priorities" list

Any list of priorities > ~3 has finessed the choice. Force it down.

### Framework performance

Running 5 Forces or SWOT with no honest diagnosis. Cargo cult.

### Confusing the *statement* of strategy with strategy

A well-written narrative can hide the absence of coherent action. Test: what does someone DO differently on Monday because of this document?

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts that Rumelt has made explicit:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Action agenda | The word is corroded; use one that forces concrete action |
| Priorities (list) | The crux (singular) or 2-3 proximate objectives | *"Priority means first."* |
| Vision / mission / values | Diagnosis / guiding policy / coherent action | Kernel is testable; VMV is fluff |
| Execution | Coherent action | Coherent action IS the thing, not a separate downstream phase |
| Best practices | Diagnosis-driven action | Best practices = what everyone does = non-strategic |
| Strategic planning | Action agenda work / Foundry | Planning enumerates spending; strategy diagnoses and responds |
| Growth AND profit | Choose which is the strategic outcome and which follows | They fight each other as parallel objectives |
| We need a bold vision | What's our diagnosed challenge? | Vision without diagnosis is rhetoric |
| Bright shiny object | Ask "does this address our crux?" | Fashion isn't strategy |
| Ambition | Addressable challenge | Ambition is fine as motivation, not as strategy |
| KPIs as strategy | KPIs as measures OF strategy | KPIs align execution to a strategy that already exists |
| Prediction | Diagnosis | *"He who forecasts the future lies, even if he tells the truth."* |
| Customer-centric | Concrete customer choice + capability to serve it | Fluff — everyone claims customer-centricity |
| Strategic HR / strategic sourcing / strategic X | Just X, or state the actual strategy | The prefix "strategic" usually signals no strategy content |
