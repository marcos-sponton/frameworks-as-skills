# Playing to Win — Method

> The canonical description of the 5 questions in Roger Martin's own terms. Fidelity is the point — Martin's framework is opinionated, and softening any of the questions collapses the method into generic strategic-planning language.

## Definition

Martin's operating definition of strategy:

> "Strategy is an integrated set of choices that compels desired customer action."
> — Roger Martin, *Playing to Win*, 2013 (and repeated in every subsequent interview)

Two words in that definition are non-negotiable:

- **"Integrated"** — the components have to reinforce each other. Answering the questions in isolation is a strategic-planning exercise, not strategy.
- **"Compels"** — customers have to be *moved* to act. If your strategy doesn't change customer behavior in your favor, it's not a strategy. As Martin puts it: *"If you say, we're the most innovative company in our industry, and customers look at the two products and say, I could flip a coin on this one, you are not effectively playing to win."*

## The five questions

Martin insists the cascade is not a sequential to-do list. You draft answers to all five, then iterate — a change in one answer forces changes in the others. The integration is the method.

### 1. What is our winning aspiration?

**What it asks:** What are we trying to accomplish? For whom? Why does it matter? What is the *purpose* the choices below serve?

**Not:** a mission statement, a values slogan, a growth target, or "we want to be #1". Aspirations are directional context, not the strategy itself.

**Author's words:**
> "You have to have answers to five questions. What's your winning aspiration?" — Martin on Lenny's podcast, 2024

**Key distinction from goals:** aspirations frame what winning looks like at a level a customer would recognize, not what winning looks like on a spreadsheet.

### 2. Where will we play?

**What it asks:** Which of these — pick specifically:
- **Geography:** global, national, regional, one city
- **Product category and segment:** which slice of the market
- **Channel:** how customers reach us
- **Customer type:** consumer, enterprise, SMB, specific persona
- **Value chain stage:** vertical integration up or down

**Martin's move:** the where-to-play answer must be *specific enough that you could point to which customers are in and which are out*. Vagueness here ("we serve customers who value quality") is the sign of a bad where-to-play.

**Anti-move Martin warns against:** entering a market because "it's big, we could get some of that". His words:
> "Most entries by foreign companies into China, they get their faces shot off. And the reason is the rationale is, it's big, we could get some of that."

The valid reason to enter: **customers there are bereft** — they lack something you can provide.

### 3. How will we win there?

**What it asks:** Given the where, what is your route to compelling customers to choose you over the alternatives? Martin insists there are two archetypes:

- **Differentiation** — customers pay a premium because you offer something they value that competitors don't.
- **Low cost** — you have a structural cost position that competitors can't match at scale.

**No middle ground.** Being "sort of differentiated and sort of cheap" is what Michael Porter called "stuck in the middle" and Martin calls "playing to play, not to win":

> "You cannot bully [competitors]. There's no way to protect yourself if you're not one of those two [differentiated or low cost]."

**Test for "how will we win":** would a competitor structurally struggle to copy this? If they could just match your feature or price tomorrow, you don't have a how-to-win — you have a temporary advantage.

### 4. What capabilities must we have?

**What it asks:** What is the reinforcing set of activities and competencies we must be uniquely good at to win *this way, in this place*?

**The word "reinforcing" matters.** Individual capabilities are easy to copy. A *system* of capabilities that mutually reinforce is what Martin calls a real moat. Four Seasons doesn't win because they train staff well — they win because hiring practices + training + empowerment + brand + facilities + processes all lock together. Any one of those, isolated, a competitor could match.

**Martin's words:**
> "What capabilities do you have to have that your competitors don't, that would enable you to win that way?"

**Pro tip Martin repeats:** the more *nuanced and multifaceted* your capabilities, the more competitors say "life's too short" and give up trying to replicate.

### 5. What management systems do we need?

**What it asks:** What structures, measures, hiring, incentives, processes, and rituals do we need to build and maintain the capabilities above over years?

**Why this question is often skipped:** it's the least glamorous. But without management systems, capabilities decay within a management cycle. Martin cites P&G's brand-management system as an example of a management system so well-designed that P&G still exports CEOs to 10% of the S&P 500 decades later — the *system* keeps producing strategic operators.

**Author's words:**
> "What management systems do you have to put in place to make sure you build and maintain those must have capabilities?"

## Integration

Martin returns to this constantly:

> "You have to do them together, and that is the essence of producing a strategy that compels desired customer action... It answers a bunch of questions that have to fit together and reinforce one another."

Concretely, integration means:

- Your **winning aspiration** should make sense only if you play in your chosen **where** — if it would apply equally to any market, it's too generic.
- Your **how-to-win** should be impossible without the **capabilities** you name — if the capabilities are generic, the how-to-win is not defensible.
- Your **management systems** should exist specifically to build the capabilities — if they're generic HR/ops systems, the capabilities won't compound.

If you can swap out one answer without disturbing the others, they're not integrated — they're a list.

## Sequence of application

Martin does not prescribe a strict order. In practice:

1. Draft all five answers roughly and fast — treat the first pass as hypothesis.
2. Test each answer against the others. Where they fail to reinforce, the strategy has a hole.
3. Rework the weakest answer, then re-check integration.
4. Iterate until the five answers form a system that a rival with the same information couldn't easily replicate.

Martin's own advice, from Lenny 2024:
> "Betterment is making that gap go away. And guess what happens if you make that gap go away? You can turn your attention to the next gap, and the next gap."

Don't aim for a perfect first draft. Aim for the next-better version.

## What this method is NOT

Martin is emphatic about the negative definition. This method is NOT:

- **A goal-setting exercise.** Goals are outputs of strategy, not strategy.
- **A vision or mission workshop.** Those set direction, they don't compel customer action.
- **"Strategic planning"** in the corporate-annual-cycle sense. Planning enumerates what you'll spend money on; strategy makes choices about how you'll win. Martin's most-repeated critique is that strategic planning has replaced strategy in most large companies, and produces neither.
- **A one-time offsite output.** Strategy is a living set of choices; when the market changes, the cascade needs re-testing.
- **Only for CEOs.** Martin cites P&G's model where brand managers "four levels down in the organization" make strategic choices that determine brand success. The cascade applies at every level where choices about winning are being made.

**Author's words on this:**
> "For the most part, in the leading business schools, it's illegal to teach [my approach]... I couldn't teach my approach at my own business school."

The critique is that academia teaches the "resource-based view of the firm" and other theories that Martin considers useless in practice. Playing to Win is his answer.
