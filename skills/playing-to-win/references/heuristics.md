# Playing to Win — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Martin's "small operational tricks" — that separate applying the cascade well from doing the version he spends most of his airtime critiquing. Attribution is precise: this comes from the book, this from a 2024 podcast, this from the 2025 Practitioner Insights essay where he updated a position.

## Do's

### Answer the five questions as a matched set, not sequentially

Martin's most-repeated correction. Practitioners break the cascade by treating Where-to-Play and How-to-Win as separate boxes on the diagram — pick WTP first, then figure out HTW. That's the diagram creating a trap, and it produces weak strategies.

**How to apply:** for any WTP you're considering, immediately ask *"is there a HTW that would make this a winning combination?"* If not, discard the WTP entirely. Don't collect it "for later."

**Author's words:**
> "WTP and HTW are an inseparably matched pair — and are the heart of strategy. Any decoupling of them will make your strategy weaker."

**Source:** [On the Inseparability of Where-to-Play and How-to-Win](https://rogermartin.medium.com/on-the-inseparability-of-where-to-play-and-how-to-win-181c2ea5c463)

### Ask "what would have to be true?" (WWHTBT) instead of "what is true?"

Invented in 1994 at Inmet Mining, now the single most valuable device in Martin's toolkit. Reframes strategy debate from data-fighting to logic-agreeing.

**How to apply:**
1. For each option on the table, list what would have to be true for it to be the right choice.
2. Team agrees on the *logic* first — no data yet.
3. Identify the items the team feels are least likely to be true — those are your **Barriers to Choice (BTC).** Test those first.
4. **Do WWHTBT for the status quo too.** This is the correction Martin now emphasizes most: without it, the status quo implicitly feels riskless. It's not.
5. Every morning, ask: *"Is our WWHTBT still true?"* If yes, keep going. If no, retool now.

**Source:** [What Would Have to be True?](https://rogermartin.medium.com/what-would-have-to-be-true-83dac5bd2189)

### Start with betterment, not perfection

You already have a strategy — whatever the org is actually doing. Don't rebuild from zero. Pareto-chart the biggest gaps between wished-for and actual outcomes, and improve the top three. That's 80/20 strategy.

**Author's words:**
> "Betterment is making that gap go away. And guess what happens if you make that gap go away? You can turn your attention to the next gap, and the next gap."

**Source:** [Where to Start with Strategy — Focus on Betterment](https://rogermartin.medium.com/where-to-start-with-strategy-bae40506304c)

### Reverse-engineer a respected competitor as your icebreaker

Martin's go-to opening move for any strategy offsite. Less threatening than starting with the company itself; teaches the cascade experientially; teams get absorbed.

**How to run it:**
1. Pick a *respected* competitor — not average. *"A choice is not strategic unless the opposite is not stupid on its face."* Average competitors haven't made real choices.
2. Follow the cascade starting from WTP (most observable from outside).
3. Focus on **differences from the industry norm, not similarities**. Similarities are industry operating imperatives — non-strategic. Differences ARE the strategy.
4. Study **actions** (calendars, ad spend, acquisitions, hiring patterns), not statements (investor decks, mission statements).
5. Aim for 80/20 accuracy — external inference is never complete.
6. **Convergence trap warning:** the point of studying the competitor is to *inoculate against copying them*. Their outcomes come from distinctiveness — copy the distinctiveness and you erase it.

**Source:** [The Best Strategy Icebreaker](https://rogermartin.medium.com/the-best-strategy-icebreaker-0c9b6ae58461)

### Use "can't or won't" as the test for a real How-to-Win

Not just "different from competitors" — the difference has to be one that competitors **can't** or **won't** replicate.

- **Can't** — competitors lack the Must-Have Capabilities. Example: Westlaw's 100-year keyword-searchable US legal database — a competitor can't build that overnight.
- **Won't** — competitors have the capability but refuse to use it. Example: Tesla — legacy automakers had EV capability but wouldn't cannibalize combustion margins.

If your HTW passes neither test, it's a temporary Value Proposition, not a Competitive Advantage. Different things.

**Source:** [Untangling Value Proposition & Competitive Advantage](https://rogermartin.medium.com/untangling-value-proposition-competitive-advantage-c3ab3ff91151)

### When generating HTW options, search along three vectors, not one

**Vector 1 — Analogies.** Every problem has been solved somewhere else. Broad, non-literal search. P&G applied *chemotherapy's targeted attack* to stain bleaching. Dyson applied *sawmill cyclone tech* to vacuums. Warning: avoid literal benchmarking — the analogy has to be similar-but-not-exact.

**Vector 2 — Tradeoffs (Integrative Thinking).** When the problem shows up as either/or, attack the tradeoff:
- **Hidden Gem** — combine opposing elements. Four Seasons = small-hotel intimacy + large-hotel amenities.
- **Double Down** — intensify one dimension to transcend the tradeoff.
- **Decomposition** — split into components that get different solutions. P&G Connect & Develop separated invention from commercialization.

**Vector 3 — Anomalies.** Ignore averages, hunt outliers. 1970s hackers foreshadowed PCs. A small segment of young women doing full skincare regimens foreshadowed Olay's opportunity.

**Source:** [Generating Creative Strategy Possibilities](https://rogermartin.medium.com/generating-creative-strategy-possibilities-cb5545206e85)

### Charter — don't delegate

When cascading strategy downward, don't just delegate the outcome. **Charter** the choice: explain the *why*, name the necessary features the subordinate's choice must satisfy, offer real help via the Responsibility Ladder, signal willingness to revisit your own choice if theirs reveals it flawed.

**Author's words:**
> "There is no 'execution.' There are only strategy choices made at various levels."

**Source:** [A Primer on Strategy Choice Chartering](https://rogermartin.medium.com/a-primer-on-strategy-choice-chartering-0fae9b42422b)

## Don'ts

### Don't do SWOT

Martin's most vocal deletion. *"Never do a SWOT analysis."* Structural problem: a strength is only a strength in the context of a specific WTP/HTW choice — and if you haven't chosen yet, you cannot assess. Ask any audience for a blinding insight they got from a SWOT — no hands. Replace with: define the problem → "how might we" → possibilities → WWHTBT → test critical assumptions.

**Author's words:**
> "It's time to toss SWOT analysis into the ashbin of strategy history."

**Source:** [Toss SWOT into the Ashbin](https://rogermartin.medium.com/its-time-to-toss-swot-analysis-into-the-ashbin-of-strategy-history-733a0dce4a80)

### Don't confuse OKRs with strategy

OKRs jump from Objectives to Key Results and skip the middle step — WTP/HTW. *"Desire (as with hope) is simply not a strategy."* Google's success came from search-based ads (>90% of causality); OKRs were a rounding error.

**Author's words:**
> "Stop letting OKRs masquerade as strategy."

Use OKRs as an Enabling Management System that *bookends* strategy — not as a substitute.

**Source:** [Stop Letting OKRs Masquerade as Strategy](https://rogermartin.medium.com/stop-letting-okrs-masquerade-as-strategy-a57fc2cea915)

### Don't invoke VUCA to justify no strategy

Emergent-strategy nihilism is Martin's other big deletion.

**Author's words:**
> "Complete strategy nihilism is a recipe for at best mediocrity, and at worst, abject failure."

CEOs who cite VUCA to avoid making a testable choice are usually control freaks avoiding accountability. Mintzberg did not mean this.

**Source:** [Strategic Nihilism](https://rogermartin.medium.com/strategic-nihilism-1ee7cba80049)

### Don't use the word "execution"

*"There is no 'execution.' There are only strategy choices made at various levels."* Blaming "execution failure" for strategic failure is misdiagnosis. Replace with **distributed strategy choices** and use Chartering (see above).

### Don't pursue growth AND profit as goals of equal weight

They fight each other. Choosing one over the other is a real strategic choice; refusing to choose is what makes most corporate strategies bad.

### Don't try to be both differentiated and low-cost

Michael Porter called this "stuck in the middle." Martin agrees: attempting both weakens both. Competition eventually forces the choice; forcing it early is strategy, deferring it is failure.

**Author's words:**
> "Cost leadership *or* differentiation, not both."

### Don't accept "we're too fast-moving for strategy" as an argument

Fast-moving businesses need strategy MORE, not less. The excuse usually masks discomfort with committing to a choice.

### Don't chase catch-up

Matching a leader's moves guarantees you stay behind. Leapfrog, don't emulate.

### Don't pivot because things aren't working *yet*

Pivot when WWHTBT is proven invalid — when customers demonstrably aren't behaving as your theory predicted. Not because you're impatient.

**Author's words:**
> "Changing direction simply because what you are doing now doesn't appear to be working well is a recipe for never giving your strategy a chance to work."

**Source:** [Playing to Win & Pivoting](https://rogermartin.medium.com/playing-to-win-pivoting-b464957e02c3)

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The WTP-alone lockdown

Practitioners email Martin stuck on HTW after picking WTP. His response: if you're down to a single WTP with no plausible HTW, the WTP itself is probably wrong. Analogy: aspiring to the NBA without the height or skills. Back up.

### Value Proposition mistaken for Competitive Advantage

A clever value prop competitors can copy tomorrow is a value prop, not a competitive advantage. This is one of the most common misapplications — teams celebrate winning-sounding language that has no defensive structure behind it.

### Capabilities listed individually instead of as a reinforcing system

Individual capabilities are easy for competitors to match. What creates a moat is the *system* — hiring + training + brand + facilities + processes all locking together (Four Seasons, Westlaw). If you can list your capabilities in a bulleted list without arrows connecting them, they don't reinforce.

### The "portrait" vs. "pixels" trap

Bad HTW = a list of activities that each look sensible individually. Good HTW = a portrait where links matter as much as nodes. Draw the connecting lines. If the story doesn't hold together as narrative, the pixels are empty calories.

**Author's words:**
> "Hang together to tell a happy story of winning."

**Source:** [Developing a Powerful How-to-Win](https://rogermartin.medium.com/developing-a-powerful-how-to-win-b28227a184fd)

### Market-share denominator inflation

Broad market definitions inflate low share. Always ask *"share of what?"* The chosen denominator often reveals whether the WTP is honest.

### The comfort trap

*"If you are entirely comfortable with your strategy, there's a strong chance it isn't very good."* A good strategy places a bet — bets are uncomfortable. If everyone left the offsite feeling calm and safe, that's a signal.

**Source:** [Big Lie of Strategic Planning (HBR)](https://hbr.org/2014/01/the-big-lie-of-strategic-planning)

## Pro tips (accelerators — small devices that punch above their weight)

### The Opposite Choice Test

For any statement you're considering as a strategic choice, ask: *"Is the opposite of this stupid on its face?"* If yes, it's not a strategic choice — it's an operating imperative. "Be customer-centric" fails the test. "Serve only Fortune 500 buyers via a direct sales team" passes.

### Reverse-engineer your own calendar

*"Strategy is what you do, not what you say."* Look at what your calendar allocates time to. That's your revealed strategy. If it doesn't match your stated WTP/HTW, one of them is wrong. Usually the stated one.

### Design roles around talented people, not templates

Organize around timebound projects rather than static positions. Get your best people on your biggest strategic bets, not on whatever the org chart says they're supposed to do.

### Segments are probabilistic, not deterministic

A segment is a distribution, not a fixed persona. Don't over-narrow.

### If you're in your industry's top quintile, don't hire the big consultants

They'll help your competitors more than you. They export your playbook.

### Small-to-medium customers > obsession with large accounts

Big accounts often destroy value net-of-servicing-cost. Review the economics carefully before organizing the company around them.

## Anti-patterns (the "bad strategy" Martin explicitly names)

### "Playing to play" instead of playing to win

**What it looks like:** language like "we're the most innovative company in our industry" or "we deliver quality" without proof customers can distinguish you.
**Why it fails:** if customers "look at the two products and say, I could flip a coin," you're playing to play.
**Author's words:**
> "If you say, we're the most innovative company in our industry, and customers look at the two products and say, I could flip a coin on this one, you are not effectively playing to win."

**How to redirect:** run the how-to-win against the can't/won't test. What can competitors literally not do or refuse to do?

### "It's a big market, we could get some of that"

**What it looks like:** market entry decisions justified by market size.
**Why it fails:** *"Most entries by foreign companies into China, they get their faces shot off."*
**How to redirect:** rewrite the entry rationale as "customers there are bereft of X, and we can provide X because Y." If you can't finish the sentence, don't enter.

### 17 (or 8, or 12) strategic priorities

**What it looks like:** the executive team lists "our strategic priorities" and it's a long list.
**Why it fails:** a list of priorities isn't strategy — it's the opposite. Strategy chooses.
**How to redirect:** force the list to 2-3 by applying the Opposite Choice Test to each item.

### Strategy delayed for "operational issues"

**What it looks like:** *"We have too many fires right now — we'll do strategy next quarter."*
**Why it fails:** without strategy, operational fires don't shrink; they multiply.
**Author's words:**
> "People say, 'well, I've got all these operational concerns right now, and then I'll get to strategy later.' You'll never amount to anything. Nobody who says that ever amounts to anything."

**How to redirect:** name the biggest gap (Pareto) and start there. 80/20 strategy is better than deferred strategy.

### Stuck in the middle (Porter, endorsed by Martin)

**What it looks like:** trying to be somewhat differentiated and somewhat cheap.
**Why it fails:** both positions weaken. A differentiated competitor beats you on premium; a cost competitor beats you on price.
**How to redirect:** pick differentiation OR low cost. Commit.

### "Buy-in" to strategy generated by strategists

**What it looks like:** strategy team develops the answer, then socializes it with line management for buy-in.
**Why it fails:** *"Everyone who needs to decide to agree with a strategy conclusion also needs to be part of generating that conclusion."*
**How to redirect:** the line generates the choice; strategists facilitate the process.

## Common misapplications (people saying they're doing Playing to Win but aren't)

### Filling in the boxes as a workshop deliverable

The cascade becomes a template someone fills out at an offsite, walls a printout, then goes back to running the company the way they were. This is planning theater with new vocabulary.

**Test for the real thing:** are any decisions actually being made differently after the exercise? If not, no strategy was produced.

### Treating the five questions as sequential

You can't answer them in isolation. If your team is fighting over "let's finish WA before touching WTP," you're on the wrong track. Draft all five fast, then iterate.

### Using PtW to justify the current path

People love to reverse-engineer their existing strategy and declare it validated. If the exercise produces no discomfort and no changes, either you're already brilliant (rare) or you've defanged the method.

### Stopping at HTW without capabilities and management systems

The book's cascade has 5 boxes. Most implementations honor the first 3 and hand-wave the last 2. Martin has published *more* about capabilities and management systems since the book than about the first three questions.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts that Martin has made explicit since 2013:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy | Integrated set of choices | Emphasizes integration, not artifact |
| Sustainable competitive advantage | Compels desired customer action | New 2025 definition; puts customer where control isn't |
| Execution | Distributed strategy choices / chartered choices | *"There is no 'execution.'"* |
| Buy-in | Commitment (via co-generation) | Buy-in comes from being in the room |
| Options | Possibilities | Options connotes planning rigor; possibilities invite creativity |
| Issues | Problems | Issues understates the pain of the gap being addressed |
| Testing & transformation | Assessment | Avoids confusion with corporate transformation programs |
| Strategic planning | Placing a bet | Names the discomfort strategy actually requires |
| Data analytics as strategy | Logic + then data | *"Strategy is primarily about logic — logical arguments about the future."* |
| Emergent strategy (as excuse) | Practice + iteration | Mintzberg didn't mean "no explicit choice" |
| Getting to #1 | Winning aspiration + how-to-win | Rank isn't strategy; how you win is |
| Best practices | Distinctiveness | Best practices = what everyone does = non-strategic |
