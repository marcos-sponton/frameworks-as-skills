## P&G / Olay — repositioning + capabilities-as-system

**Where Martin uses it:** *Playing to Win* (book, 2013), Lenny's podcast (2024), many Practitioner Insights essays.

**The situation:** Oil of Olay was a fading prestige-competitor brand for older women. Under Lafley and Martin's cascade, P&G repositioned it as premium mass-market skincare — capturing customers Clinique/Estée Lauder couldn't follow (they'd cannibalize their prestige portfolios) and demolishing the "we're a mass brand" ceiling.

**How Martin describes the cascade applied:**
- **Winning aspiration:** to lead the premium mass skincare category, not just participate in it.
- **Where to play:** mass channels (drugstore, big-box), but at the top of that shelf — not fighting Clinique in department stores.
- **How to win:** premium price in mass channel + full skincare regimen (borrowed analogically from Shiseido's Japanese multi-step routine) + specific bio-active ingredients + branding that credibly bridged mass and prestige.
- **Capabilities:** package design, ingredient sourcing, retail relationships, marketing to a new segment.
- **Management systems:** the P&G brand-management model that let brand managers "four levels down" make strategic choices with real authority.

**Why the case is instructive:** it shows the "won't" moat — Clinique/Estée Lauder had capabilities to enter mass but structurally wouldn't (cannibalization) — and it shows analogy as HTW-vector (Shiseido's regimen was the analogy source).

**Quotable moment:**
> "P&G's Lafley refused to boycott Amazon despite retailer pressure. Acknowledged: 'If customers want to shop there, we just can't not be where our customers want to shop.'" — following the customer tide.

## Vanguard — low-cost differentiation + following the tide

**Where Martin uses it:** book, Lenny 2024, multiple Practitioner Insights essays.

**The situation:** ~$9 trillion AUM. Differentiates via lowest-cost AUM model at scale.

**How the cascade applied:**
- **WA:** democratize investing by driving cost out of ownership.
- **WTP:** individual investors seeking passive exposure, then institutional.
- **HTW:** structural low-cost via scale + mutual-ownership structure (Vanguard is owned by its funds) that removes profit extraction.
- **Capabilities:** operational efficiency, distribution, brand as fiduciary.
- **Management systems:** the mutual structure itself is a management system.

**Instructive move:** Jack Bogle eventually adopted ETFs despite personally disliking them. Following the customer tide even when it hurts. Martin uses this to teach that "water finds its own level" — resisting customer flow is a losing bet.

## Southwest Airlines — the coherent activity system

**Where Martin uses it:** book, Practitioner Insights, endless podcast references. (Also central to Michael Porter's 1996 *What Is Strategy?* essay, which Martin cites approvingly.)

**The situation:** only US airline earning cost of capital consistently over 50 years.

**How the cascade applied:**
- **WA:** make air travel affordable and reliable enough that people drive less.
- **WTP:** point-to-point short-hop routes (not hub-and-spoke), secondary airports (avoid hub congestion costs), no international.
- **HTW:** structural low-cost.
- **Capabilities (reinforcing system):** single aircraft type (737) → lower training/maintenance costs → higher aircraft utilization → shorter turnarounds → more flights per plane per day → lower cost per seat-mile. Plus: flexible labor relations, online-first booking, no assigned seats, unbundled services.
- **Management systems:** hiring for attitude, empowerment, profit-sharing.

**Why the case is instructive:** every element reinforces every other. Competitors can't dismantle their entire business model to copy this piecemeal. Delta trying to run point-to-point with a mixed fleet is worse than either strategy done cleanly.

**Quotable moment (Rumelt version, cited by Martin approvingly):**
> Every action taken by Southwest supported every other action.

## Four Seasons — differentiation via reinforcing capabilities

**Where Martin uses it:** book, Lenny 2024, Practitioner Insights ("Fixing Strategy").

**The situation:** defined luxury as compensation for leaving home/office, not grand architecture. 45+ years of market leadership.

**How the cascade applied:**
- **WA:** be the hotel of choice for demanding business travelers who value being taken care of over ostentation.
- **WTP:** business-traveler-heavy urban and resort destinations.
- **HTW:** differentiation via service excellence — the "hidden gem" tradeoff-resolution move: small-hotel intimacy + large-hotel amenities.
- **Capabilities:** hiring practices (10% turnover vs. 80% industry average), training depth, empowerment (front-desk can spend to solve guest problems), brand.
- **Management systems:** the hiring and training system institutionalizes service standards. Founder Isadore Sharp expanded iteratively — one hotel at a time — never overextending.

**Why the case is instructive:** competitors can't replicate without remaking their entire staff model. Any single element (empowerment, hiring, training) is copyable; the *system* is not.

## Westlaw — the "can't" moat

**Where Martin uses it:** Practitioner Insights ("Untangling Value Proposition & Competitive Advantage").

**The situation:** Thomson Reuters' Westlaw is the dominant legal research platform. 100+ years of lawyer-written case headnotes with searchable keywords.

**HTW moat:** competitors literally can't build this overnight. 1,500 full-time lawyers maintain the system continuously. That's 150,000+ lawyer-years of work. Plus law-school training that entrenches the vocabulary and interface.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetypal "can't" moat — the capability system took a century to build and would take a century to replicate. Competitors can offer alternatives (LexisNexis, and now AI-based tools) but the switching cost and legacy asset make Westlaw structurally hard to unseat.

## Tesla — the "won't" moat

**Where Martin uses it:** Practitioner Insights ("Untangling Value Proposition & Competitive Advantage"), Lenny 2024.

**The situation:** 10-year EV head start. Legacy automakers had EV capability but wouldn't cannibalize combustion margins.

**HTW moat:** competitors *could* have entered EVs but *wouldn't* — the internal politics and short-term profitability incentives kept them out. By the time they entered seriously (2020s), Tesla had built brand association ("electric vehicle = Tesla"), Supercharger network, manufacturing learning curve.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetypal "won't" moat. The moat wasn't technical — the technology was available — it was political/organizational.

## Lego — iterative bets

**Where Martin uses it:** Practitioner Insights ("Fixing Strategy").

**The situation:** CEO Jørgen Vig Knudstorp greenlit *one* Lego Movie, not a slate.

**Why the case is instructive:** Martin uses Lego (and Isadore Sharp's one-hotel-at-a-time approach) to teach iteration as strategic principle: *"You don't bet the farm. But you aren't too terrified to bet."* One test at a time, learn, then commit or kill.

## Dyson — analogy vector

**Where Martin uses it:** Practitioner Insights ("Generating Creative Strategy Possibilities").

**The situation:** James Dyson borrowed cyclone technology from industrial sawmills (used for extracting dust from air) and applied it to vacuum cleaner suction — bypassing the bag-filter design orthodoxy that had dominated for decades.

**Why the case is instructive:** the analogy has to be similar-but-not-exact. Sawmill cyclones aren't vacuum cleaners; the mechanism is transferable across the surface differences.

## Rotman School (Martin's own deanship)

**Where Martin uses it:** Chartering essays, personal 4-step strategy process essays, "Being Too Busy" personal-strategy essay.

**The situation:** Martin was dean of the Rotman School of Management (University of Toronto) 1998–2013. During that period he applied Playing to Win to the school itself and used chartering with senior staff.

**Concrete examples he cites:**
- **EMBA director Beatrix Dart:** chartered with "get us to #1 in GTA; features: major innovation, deep student research, anything except outsourcing credibility." She invented the 13-month compressed EMBA — a possibility Martin himself wouldn't have suggested.
- **CAO Mary-Ellen Yeomans:** chartered with "build a financial framework that gives us multi-year revenue certainty and benefits the university; anything else negotiable." She invented "resource center management" that produced $130M revenue growth.
- **Executive Assistant Suzanne Spragge:** institutionalized time allocation (Martin's personal Enabling Management System).

**Why the cases are instructive:** they show chartering in action — Martin as strategist made *his* choice (be #1 in the GTA), specified constraints, delegated the *how* fully, and got possibilities he wouldn't have generated himself.

## Herman Miller — pivoting when WWHTBT breaks

**Where Martin uses it:** Practitioner Insights ("Playing to Win & Pivoting").

**The situation:** Herman Miller sold expensive furniture with heavy design-service integration. Their WWHTBT included: customers value integrated design consulting bundled with product.

**When WWHTBT broke:** market shifted; customers no longer wanted (or would pay for) the bundled service.

**How they pivoted:** segmented offerings — customers who still wanted full service got it at a premium; customers who wanted just the product got simplified offerings at lower price.

**Why the case is instructive:** contrast with Nokia (didn't pivot when WWHTBT broke) and Yamamoto at Midway (pivoted too fast on inadequate signal). Herman Miller pivoted on real data, on the right dimension.

## Cases from Martin's own books (not covered above but worth knowing)

Martin has written 5 books beyond *Playing to Win*. Each contains cases:

- *The Opposable Mind* (2007) — Martha Graham, Charles Schwab, integrative-thinking cases.
- *The Design of Business* (2009) — Procter & Gamble again, plus design-thinking cases.
- *Creating Great Choices* (2017, with Jennifer Riel) — Integrative Thinking applied to strategy choice.
- *When More Is Not Better* (2020) — post-Great-Recession critique of shareholder capitalism.
- *A New Way to Think* (2022) — 14 mental models that get strategy wrong.

If the user asks for cases outside the recurring roster, these books are the source of truth.
