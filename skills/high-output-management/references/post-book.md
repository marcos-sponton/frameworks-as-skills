# High Output Management — Material posterior al libro

> **This is the differential of this skill.** The 1983 book *High Output Management* laid down the manager's operating system. Since then:
>
> - Grove himself published **three more books** (*Only the Paranoid Survive* 1996, *Swimming Across* 2001, *Strategy Is Destiny* 2001) and HBR articles
> - Grove died on 2016-03-21 — his own corpus is now closed
> - **A named roster of carriers** publicly extends his method: Ben Horowitz (a16z, foreword to the 2015 reprint, two books), John Doerr (OKR canonization), Julie Zhuo (first-time manager translation), Claire Hughes Johnson (Stripe-era operational playbook), Robert Burgelman (Stanford academic account)
> - The **2015 Vintage reprint with Horowitz's foreword** is now the canonical edition — most modern readers enter Grove through it
>
> Most assistant responses about *HOM* pull from a competent paraphrase of the 1983 book alone. This file captures the 40+ years of extension — where Grove himself refined his positions, and where the carriers have applied and adapted the method into 2026 startup and tech contexts. Organized so you can pull the specific piece you need.

## Grove's own extensions (1983 → 2001)

### *Only the Paranoid Survive* (Currency, 1996) — the strategy book

*HOM* is about running the machine. *OTPS* is about noticing when the machine you've optimized is about to become obsolete. Grove wrote it after leading Intel through the memory-to-microprocessor pivot (1985–86) and made the pivot itself the load-bearing case study.

**What *OTPS* adds to *HOM*:**

**Strategic Inflection Points (SIP)** — *"A time in the life of a business when its fundamentals are about to change."* The metaphor is calculus — the point on a curve where the direction of bending reverses, never to reverse back.

**The 10X test** — a change in one of the six competitive forces (Porter's Five + complementors) that is roughly ten times stronger than what the business is built to handle. Grove is strict about the "10X" — most industry shifts aren't SIPs, and calling everything an SIP produces false-positive pivots.

**Helpful Cassandras** — the middle managers, salespeople, and frontline engineers who see the shift before the executives. Grove's point: they will be dismissed by the insulated top, but their weak signal is the strongest early data you have.

**The "outside CEO" thought experiment** — *"If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?"* — the retold moment between Grove and Moore that unblocked the memory exit. The mechanic creates psychological distance from the sunk cost.

**Three phases of SIP response** — acknowledge emotional resistance → enable bottom-up input → implement top-down direction with consistent messaging.

**The famous line:** *"Success breeds complacency. Complacency breeds failure. Only the paranoid survive."*

**Source:** [*Only the Paranoid Survive* — publisher page](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/only-the-paranoid-survive-andrew-s-grove); Commoncog analysis [Only the Paranoid Survive](https://commoncog.com/only-the-paranoid-survive/).

### *Strategy Is Destiny* (with Robert Burgelman, Free Press, 2001)

Grove co-taught the Strategic Management course at Stanford GSB with Robert Burgelman for ~25 years. *Strategy Is Destiny* is Burgelman's academic account of Intel's strategy-making process, with Grove's direct collaboration. Case-based, dense, more academic in register than *HOM* or *OTPS*.

**What it adds:** the theoretical framing of Intel's strategy as an *emergent* process rather than a designed one — strategy as the result of thousands of resource-allocation decisions made by middle managers, which the CEO then rationalizes into a coherent direction. This is Grove's most-explicit account of how his operating system (managerial leverage, middle-manager empowerment, constructive confrontation) *produces* strategy from the bottom up.

**Source:** [Simon & Schuster — Strategy Is Destiny](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Strategy-Is-Destiny/Robert-A-Burgelman/9780743215688).

### *Swimming Across: A Memoir* (Warner Books, 2001)

The Hungarian refugee story through age 20. Not a management text, but foundational for the voice. Read this if you want to understand *why* Grove writes the way he writes — the directness, the refusal to sentimentalize, the engineering register applied to human experience.

**Source:** [Hachette — Swimming Across](https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/andrew-s-grove/swimming-across/9780446679701/).

### HBR — *Inside Intel* (Nov 1996)

Grove's companion HBR article to *OTPS*. Applies the SIP + 10X framework to Intel's specific pivot. The most-accessible primary-Grove source that isn't a book.

**Source:** [HBR — Inside Intel (1996)](https://hbr.org/1996/11/inside-intel).

## The 2015 Vintage reprint of *HOM* — the Horowitz foreword

The single most consequential piece of Grove-adjacent writing since 1996. Vintage reprinted *HOM* in 2015 with an original foreword by **Ben Horowitz**. The foreword is publicly reproduced on Medium: [Andy — Introduction to *High Output Management*](https://medium.com/software-is-eating-the-world/andy-37e10d4780bc).

**Why it matters:** the foreword is where most modern tech-industry readers actually enter Grove. It is also the piece that positions Grove as the intellectual grandparent of Silicon Valley management practice. Horowitz identifies "three core aspects to [Grove's] genius":

1. Grove "lucidly explains concepts that require entire books from lesser writers" in minimal space.
2. Grove "consistently uncovers brand-new management ideas or finds new insights in old standards."
3. Unlike most management books teaching basic competency, Grove teaches "how to be great."

**Key Grove concepts Horowitz elevates in the foreword:**
- The manager's output equation
- The "can't or won't" diagnostic for underperformance
- Task-relevant maturity
- Training as the manager's core responsibility (*"managers have only two levers: motivation and training"*)
- One-on-ones as *"perhaps the best source for organizational knowledge that a manager can get."*

**Horowitz's key line on Grove:** *"Probably for my money the best CEO we ever had out here."*

## The carriers — modern extensions of Grove's method

Because Grove is dead, the *live* development of his ideas happens through named carriers. This section indexes each carrier's contribution, so the skill can pull the specific extension when the user's context needs it.

### Ben Horowitz — the direct successor

**Position:** a16z co-founder. Wrote the foreword to the 2015 *HOM* reprint. Explicitly builds on Grove throughout his own work.

**Books:**
- [*The Hard Thing About Hard Things*](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things-ben-horowitz) (HarperBusiness, 2014) — the CEO-under-fire book. Grove's task-relevant maturity, one-on-ones, and performance-review directness are load-bearing throughout.
- [*What You Do Is Who You Are*](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/what-you-do-is-who-you-are-ben-horowitz) (HarperBusiness, 2019) — the culture book. Applies Grove's structural view (culture = the specific mechanics you enforce) rather than the vibes view.

**Key Horowitz extensions of Grove's method:**

**Task-Relevant Maturity applied to executive hires.** Horowitz's most-cited extension. New executives — even the very senior ones — have low TRM in *your* market, *your* technology, *your* company. Micromanaging new executives for a limited period accelerates their onboarding; skipping this because they're senior is the mistake. *"Even — and maybe especially — executives are also immature in certain tasks."* Source: [Driverless Crocodile summary](https://www.driverlesscrocodile.com/books-and-recommendations/ben-horowitz-on-task-relevant-maturity-and-micromanagement/).

**One-on-ones as fireable expectation.** Horowitz has publicly said he would fire a manager for not doing one-on-ones. That is the leverage they represent in his view.

**Performance-review directness, extended.** Horowitz's version of *"level with your report, listen, leave yourself out"* is his rule that the CEO's honesty about a company's problems is the load-bearing element of trust. *"There is only one thing worse than lying — believing your own lies."* (paraphrase, *THT*).

**Public accountability as a cultural mechanism.** The Grove story Horowitz retells most: Grove, in a public setting, to a habitually-late subordinate: *"All I have in this world is time and you are wasting my time."* Horowitz's read: Grove used public accountability to reinforce cultural norms about respect for others' time.

**Source (audio, dense):** [Tim Ferriss Show ep. 392 — Ben Horowitz transcript](https://tim.blog/2019/11/12/ben-horowitz-transcript/).

### John Doerr — the OKR canonizer

**Position:** joined Intel in 1974 (age 23); took Grove's iMBO course in 1975; joined Kleiner Perkins; introduced OKRs to Google in 1999; wrote the book.

**Book:** [*Measure What Matters*](https://www.measurewhatmatters.com/) (Portfolio, 2018) — the OKR canon. Explicitly credits Grove throughout as the "Father of OKRs" and calls him *"the greatest manager of his or any other era."*

**Platform:** [What Matters](https://www.whatmatters.com/) — Doerr's ongoing OKR content site. The [Origin Story](https://www.whatmatters.com/articles/the-origin-story) and [Operation Crush case](https://www.whatmatters.com/okrs-explained/john-doerr-operation-crush) are the most Grove-forward pieces.

**Key Doerr extensions of Grove's method:**

**Naming and canonizing OKRs.** Grove called his version *iMBO* (Intel Management by Objectives). Doerr renamed it *OKRs* (Objectives and Key Results). Under Doerr, OKRs picked up specific mechanics that Grove had left implicit — public visibility of all OKRs across the company, quarterly cadence + annual, the practice of scoring 0.0–1.0 with 0.7 as ambitious target.

**Cascading OKRs at scale.** Grove's original design was for Intel-scale (~10K employees at the time). Doerr's book extends the cascade to organizations of ~100K+ people (Google post-2010) and to non-profit / policy contexts (Gates Foundation, ONE, MyFitnessPal).

**The Google case studies.** *Measure What Matters* is the closest thing to a modern manual for OKR practice, with detailed case studies of how OKRs actually get set, cascaded, and graded.

**For OKR-native practice** — see [[radical-focus]] (Christina Wodtke), which is the startup-scale practical manual downstream of Grove/Doerr.

### Julie Zhuo — the first-time-manager translation

**Position:** ex-Facebook VP Design (was employee #~150 in the design org; managed teams from single-digit to hundreds). Now co-founder of Sundial.

**Book:** [*The Making of a Manager*](https://www.juliezhuo.com/book/manager.html) (Portfolio, 2019) — reviewers routinely call it *"HOM* for the modern first-time manager." Zhuo's explicit debt to Grove is throughout.

**Platform:** [Substack "The Looking Glass"](https://lg.substack.com/) — ongoing management essays that carry Grove's operational register into first-time-manager territory.

**Key Zhuo extensions of Grove's method:**

**Task-relevant maturity applied to the IC-to-manager transition.** The single hardest TRM moment: a senior IC promoted into management has high TRM in the craft, low TRM in managing. Zhuo's book is essentially a full manual for the low-TRM management task.

**One-on-ones translated to the design/product context.** Zhuo's chapters on giving feedback, running one-on-ones, and dealing with reports who are struggling are the Grove mechanics applied to a context Grove never wrote about (creative work, subjective quality, taste).

**The manager as coach vs. player.** Zhuo's most-cited move: the transition from "playing the game" (IC) to "coaching the game" (manager) is a *category shift*, not a skill upgrade. This is Grove's output-equation move restated for the first-time manager who still identifies as an IC.

### Claire Hughes Johnson — the modern operational playbook

**Position:** ex-Stripe COO (2014–2021, took Stripe from ~200 to 7,000+ employees). Previously at Google in operating roles.

**Book:** [*Scaling People*](https://press.stripe.com/scaling-people) (Stripe Press, 2023) — 500+ pages of playbook, templates, and cadence design. Reviewers: *"Claire's book captures much of the best from its predecessors, such as *The Effective Executive* and *High Output Management*."*

**Key Hughes Johnson extensions of Grove's method:**

**Grove's cadences as living templates.** Where Grove wrote general principles for one-on-ones and staff meetings, Hughes Johnson writes explicit templates — with a level of operational specificity Grove left implicit. The book is closer to an "operating system installer" than a management philosophy.

**The performance-review cycle at scale.** Hughes Johnson's chapters on running performance reviews at high-growth-startup scale (where the org is doubling in size each year) extend Grove's *HOM* Ch. 13 into a very different operating context.

**Values → behaviors → mechanisms.** Hughes Johnson's translation of Grove's *"values are transmitted by doing"* into a specific practice: name the value, name the observable behavior that embodies it, name the mechanism that reinforces it (a promotion, a piece of feedback, a specific ritual).

**Self-awareness and mutual awareness.** A Grove-adjacent addition Hughes Johnson emphasizes: managers need explicit tools to understand their own working style and communicate it, so reports can calibrate. Not in *HOM*, but consistent with Grove's structural view of management (the relational dimension needs its own instrument).

### Robert Burgelman — the academic carrier

**Position:** Stanford GSB professor of management. Co-taught the Strategic Management course with Grove for ~25 years (~1991–2016). Co-authored *Strategy Is Destiny* (2001).

**Post-Grove-death piece:** [HBR — Remembering Andy Grove, the Teacher](https://hbr.org/2016/03/remembering-andy-grove-the-teacher) (March 2016).

**Key Burgelman extensions/observations of Grove's method:**

**Grove's teaching method.** Case-based, adversarial, ruthless with hand-waving. If a student made a general claim, Grove would ask *"How would you know?"* and let the silence sit. Burgelman: Grove preferred concrete examples pulled from the student's actual situation, and would turn the class into live consulting on the student's problem.

**Strategy as an emergent process.** The academic frame Burgelman gives to Grove's operating system: Intel's strategy was not designed in a corner office; it *emerged* from thousands of resource-allocation decisions made by middle managers with high TRM. The CEO's job was to rationalize the emergent direction after the fact and to accelerate what was working. This is a strong theoretical claim about how Grove's operating system produces strategy from the bottom up.

**Source (canonical academic text on Intel):** [*Strategy Is Destiny*](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/Strategy-Is-Destiny/Robert-A-Burgelman/9780743215688).

## The Silicon Valley canon effect — endorsers who keep *HOM* in circulation

Not strict carriers (they don't publish extensions) but loud enough that their endorsements keep the book on every founder's shelf:

- **Marc Andreessen** (a16z co-founder): after Grove's death, *"The best company builder Silicon Valley has ever seen, and likely will ever seen."*
- **Mark Zuckerberg** (Meta): reportedly makes *HOM* required reading for new managers.
- **Brian Chesky** (Airbnb): endorses publicly.
- **Drew Houston** (Dropbox): endorses publicly.
- **Evan Williams** (Twitter/Medium): endorses publicly.
- **Patrick Collison** (Stripe): endorses publicly; also commissioned *Scaling People* through Stripe Press.
- **Sundar Pichai** (Google): OKR-native leader; direct descendant of the Grove → Doerr → Google lineage.
- **Marc Benioff** (Salesforce): endorses publicly.
- **Dominic Cummings** (UK political strategist): recommended *HOM* to government advisors following the 2019 UK election.
- **Clayton Christensen** (Harvard, deceased 2020): peer, admirer, and one of Grove's most rigorous theoretical readers. HBR tribute: *"I am easily a foot taller than Andy Grove. But whenever I was with him, I felt that he was the giant."* Source: [HBR — What I'll Miss About Andy Grove](https://hbr.org/2016/03/clayton-christensen-what-ill-miss-about-andy-grove).

## Biographical context — Michael S. Malone

The definitive triple biography of the Intel founding trinity: [*The Intel Trinity: How Robert Noyce, Gordon Moore, and Andy Grove Built the World's Most Important Company*](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/the-intel-trinity-michael-s-malone) (HarperBusiness, 2014). Malone had unprecedented access to Intel archives. Necessary if you want to understand why *HOM* has the voice it does — the register comes from a specific man, in a specific place, at a specific time, and Malone documents that.

## What the extensions haven't touched (open ground for future carriers)

Grove's method has been extended into:
- Modern startup CEO context (Horowitz)
- OKRs at scale (Doerr)
- First-time managers (Zhuo)
- High-growth operations (Hughes Johnson)

It has *not* been comprehensively extended into:
- **Remote / distributed teams.** Grove wrote for co-located Intel. The one-on-one, staff meeting, and operation review mechanics translate but with friction; no carrier has written a full extension.
- **AI-agent management.** The output equation for a manager whose "team" includes AI agents is an open question. No carrier has written this.
- **Async communication.** Grove's meeting taxonomy assumes synchronous. Extending it to async is open ground.

When the user is in one of these contexts, the skill should acknowledge Grove didn't address it, then reason from the underlying principles (output equation, leverage, TRM) rather than pretend an extension exists.
