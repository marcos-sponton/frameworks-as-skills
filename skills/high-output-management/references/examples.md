# High Output Management — Examples

> Worked cases that Grove used publicly, plus canonical cases from the carriers who apply his method. Each case is tagged with source so the assistant can cite it, and with the specific Grove mechanic it illustrates.

## From Grove's own writing

### The Breakfast Factory (*HOM* Ch. 1)

**Grove mechanic illustrated:** the production frame — every organizational activity is a production process.

Grove opens *HOM* with a café making breakfast — soft-boiled egg, buttered toast, coffee. Not a real company; the didactic case that anchors the whole book.

The lesson: **identify the limiting step** (the egg — longest cook time). Everything else has to be timed against the egg's throughput. Same principle applies to a strategy meeting (the limiting step is the decision), to hiring (the limiting step is the reference check), to a launch (the limiting step is the customer's first successful use).

**Extends to:** any "our team is slow" diagnosis. First question: *where is the limiting step?* Adding capacity at any other step doesn't help until the limiting step is addressed.

**Source:** *HOM* Ch. 1.

### Intel's memory-to-microprocessor pivot (1985–1986)

**Grove mechanics illustrated:** strategic inflection point, 10X change, Helpful Cassandras, the "outside CEO" thought experiment, three-phase SIP response.

The canonical *OTPS* case. Intel was a memory-chip company throughout the 1970s and early 1980s — it was the founding business, the identity, the profit engine. By mid-1985, Japanese competitors (NEC, Hitachi, Toshiba, Fujitsu) had turned memory chips into a 10X competitive force through superior manufacturing quality and cost. Intel's profits collapsed from $198M to below break-even.

The pivotal moment: mid-1985, Grove to Gordon Moore:

> **"If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?"**

Moore, immediately: **"He would get us out of memories."**

Grove: **"Why shouldn't you and I walk out the door, come back, and do it ourselves?"**

Over the next year, Intel exited memory chips — closed plants, laid off a third of the workforce, and redirected all resources to microprocessors. By 1986, the 8086 microprocessor had recaptured 85% of the 16-bit market, and the 8088 variant was already inside the IBM PC.

**What made this a canonical SIP case:**
- **10X test passed** — Japanese manufacturing was ~10x better on the axes that mattered (yield, cost, quality).
- **Helpful Cassandras were active** — Intel salespeople had been reporting for months that customers were switching to Japanese DRAM. Executives had rationalized the signal.
- **The outside-CEO thought experiment unblocked the decision** — Grove and Moore had been paralyzed by identity (Intel = memory) and sunk cost. Reframing as an outside decision-maker cleared it.
- **The three-phase response happened** — emotional acknowledgment first (the layoffs were the hardest thing Grove ever did), then bottom-up input (product teams designed the pivot), then top-down consistent messaging (the microprocessor story became the new Intel identity).

**Source:** *OTPS* Ch. 5; [Immunity Networks — Grove pivot](https://blog.immunitynetworks.com/andrew-grove-intel-microprocessor-pivot-paranoid-survive/); Michael Malone's *Intel Trinity*.

### Operation Crush (1979)

**Grove mechanics illustrated:** OKRs in action, cascaded objectives, speed of response, cultural safety enabling Helpful Cassandras.

The canonical OKR-in-action case, retold by John Doerr in *Measure What Matters*.

Late 1979: a field manager sent Intel HQ a desperate eight-page telex — Motorola's 68000 microprocessor was winning designs against Intel's 8086. Motorola engineers were being called "superior"; Intel was losing.

Grove's response, in **two weeks**: reorganized company priorities around a single OKR:

**Objective:** *"Establish the 8086 as the highest performance 16-bit microprocessor family."*

**Key results:**
1. Develop and publish five benchmarks showing superior 8086 family performance.
2. Repackage the entire 8086 family of products.
3. Get the 500 series into production.
4. Win **2,000 design wins** in a year.

The OKR cascaded — every Intel manager knew what "design win" meant, what their contribution to the 2,000 target was, and what specific action their week produced.

**Outcome:** they beat the target by 500 (2,500 design wins). One of those design wins was IBM, which chose the 8088 (a variant of the 8086) for the IBM PC. That decision structured the entire PC industry for the next 25 years.

**Grove's operating insight from this case:** *"Bad companies are destroyed by crisis. Good companies survive them. Great companies are improved by them."* Operation Crush was Intel improved by crisis, structurally, via the OKR mechanic.

**Cultural precondition:** the field manager felt safe enough to send the telex. Grove had built the culture (constructive confrontation, Helpful Cassandras) that made the weak signal audible.

**Source:** [What Matters — Operation Crush case](https://www.whatmatters.com/okrs-explained/john-doerr-operation-crush); Doerr's *Measure What Matters*.

### Grove's public rebuke of the habitually-late employee

**Grove mechanic illustrated:** promotions and public accountability as cultural signals.

Retold by Ben Horowitz on Tim Ferriss (2019). Grove, in a public setting (staff meeting) to a subordinate who arrived late:

> **"All I have in this world is time and you are wasting my time."**

Horowitz's analysis: Grove used the *public setting deliberately*. If he'd said it privately, only two people would have felt the norm. Said publicly, the whole meeting understood: respect for others' time is not negotiable.

This is Grove's *"Values and behavioral norms are simply not transmitted easily by talk or memo, but are conveyed very effectively by doing and doing visibly"* in action.

**Extends to:** any culture drift where a manager tolerates a small violation privately, hoping it corrects. Grove's move — public correction of a norm violation, in the moment — is the mechanic that actually reinforces the norm.

**Source:** [Tim Ferriss Show ep. 392, Ben Horowitz transcript](https://tim.blog/2019/11/12/ben-horowitz-transcript/).

### Intel's constructive confrontation culture

**Grove mechanic illustrated:** attack problems not people; disagree and commit; structural directness.

Recurring case in *HOM* and *OTPS*. Intel's engineering culture was built on structured, high-intensity debate — engineers ferociously arguing with one another, then walking out of the meeting as friends. The pattern was intentional; a consultant observing Intel in the early 1970s coined the term "constructive confrontation" to describe it.

**Illustrative moment (from the Grovian Culture Substack retelling):** a vice president proposed a statistical model to a group of plant managers. Plant managers pushed back hard — the model missed practical constraints on the factory floor. The VP debated, listened, and then — publicly — accepted the plant managers' better solution. No hierarchy protection; no face-saving.

**The four disciplines Grove required for the pattern:**
- Attack problems, not people
- Speed matters — don't let disagreement calcify
- Assume positive intent
- Listen with the same intensity you argue with

**Source:** *HOM* Ch. 12; [Grovian Culture Substack — Intel's Constructive Confrontation](https://groveianculture.substack.com/p/intels-constructive-confrontation).

## From the carriers — modern applications

### Google adopting OKRs (1999)

**Carrier:** John Doerr.
**Grove mechanic illustrated:** OKR lineage; cascaded objectives at organizational scale.

Doerr, as Kleiner Perkins investor in Google, introduced OKRs to Larry Page and Sergey Brin in 1999 when Google had ~30 employees. Doerr's pitch was Grove's iMBO framework directly — the same course he'd taken at Intel in 1975.

Google adopted OKRs across all employees. As the company scaled to 100,000+ employees, OKRs became the operating cadence — quarterly + annual, public (any Google employee can see any other's OKRs), graded 0.0–1.0 with 0.7 as ambitious target.

**Why this case matters:** it is the proof-of-scale for Grove's OKR mechanic. Grove designed for Intel-scale (~10K); Doerr and Google extended to 100K+ without losing the operating discipline.

**Source:** [What Matters — Origin Story](https://www.whatmatters.com/articles/the-origin-story); Doerr's *Measure What Matters*.

### Ben Horowitz managing at Loudcloud / Opsware (2000–2007)

**Carrier:** Ben Horowitz.
**Grove mechanics illustrated:** task-relevant maturity applied to executive hires; one-on-ones as diagnostic instrument under corporate near-death; performance-review directness.

*The Hard Thing About Hard Things* is largely a case book of Grove's mechanics applied under crisis. A few illustrative moments:

**TRM applied to new-executive onboarding.** Horowitz hired multiple senior executives (VP-level) during Loudcloud's high-growth phase. His extension of Grove: even senior hires have low TRM in *your* market, *your* technology, *your* company. Micromanaging them for the first 90 days accelerated their learning; treating them as instantly high-TRM because of their resume was the mistake.

**One-on-ones as the load-bearing diagnostic during the Loudcloud-to-Opsware pivot.** When Loudcloud was hemorrhaging and Horowitz was deciding whether to pivot to software (Opsware), the one-on-ones with his senior team were where the weak signals surfaced — customer behavior, competitor moves, internal morale. The formal meetings couldn't produce that data; the one-on-ones did.

**Firing directness.** Horowitz's chapter on firing an executive is Grove's *"level with your report, listen, leave yourself out"* under maximum stakes. His rule: never let a performance issue go unnamed in the review. *"You've got to put in the review. It's bad that you didn't tell him, but it's worse to not tell him now."*

**Source:** [*The Hard Thing About Hard Things*](https://www.harpercollins.com/products/the-hard-thing-about-hard-things-ben-horowitz).

### Claire Hughes Johnson operationalizing Stripe (2014–2021)

**Carrier:** Claire Hughes Johnson.
**Grove mechanics illustrated:** one-on-ones, staff meetings, cascaded goals, performance reviews at high-growth-startup scale.

Hughes Johnson joined Stripe as COO when the company was ~200 people. When she left in 2021, Stripe was 7,000+. *Scaling People* is the operating-playbook document that came out of that scaling exercise — and reviewers universally read it as "*HOM* for a modern high-growth startup."

**Illustrative extensions:**

- **One-on-ones with templates.** Where Grove wrote general principles, Hughes Johnson provides literal templates — a Notion-page format for the report to fill in before the meeting, standard agenda items, cadence calibrated to role.
- **Performance-review cycles at scale.** How to run twice-yearly reviews across 5,000 people without losing the individual signal.
- **Values → behaviors → mechanisms.** Hughes Johnson's translation of Grove's structural culture claim into a concrete practice: for each Stripe value, name the observable behavior and the specific mechanism (a hiring bar, a promotion criterion, a piece of feedback) that reinforces it.

**Source:** [Stripe Press — Scaling People](https://press.stripe.com/scaling-people).

### Julie Zhuo managing at Facebook (2006–2018)

**Carrier:** Julie Zhuo.
**Grove mechanic illustrated:** IC-to-manager transition (the classic low-TRM moment); manager-as-coach translation for the design/product context.

Zhuo was Facebook employee #~150 in the design org. Managed teams from single-digit to hundreds over 12 years. *The Making of a Manager* is her extension of Grove's method into the first-time-manager onboarding problem, in a specific context Grove didn't write about (creative work, subjective quality, taste).

**Illustrative extensions:**

- **The IC-to-manager identity shift.** Zhuo names it as a *category shift* (from "playing the game" to "coaching the game"), not a skill upgrade. This is Grove's output-equation move applied to the first-time manager who still identifies as an IC and produces "individual work" as their output.
- **Feedback on subjective craft.** Grove's "level with your report" translated for a design manager giving feedback on a mockup where the "right" answer is taste-based, not measurable. The mechanic: name the specific observation + the specific principle + the specific alternative.
- **Managing small teams (5–15 people) at high-growth scale.** Grove wrote for middle managers with dozens of reports. Zhuo extends to the small-team lead in a fast-growing product organization.

**Source:** [Julie Zhuo — The Making of a Manager](https://www.juliezhuo.com/book/manager.html).

## Third-party applications (useful, but tag them as applied Grove, not Grove himself)

### Amazon's "Two-Pizza Team" rule

Not from Grove, but a Grove-adjacent operating pattern. The two-pizza rule (team small enough to be fed by two pizzas) is a specific instrument for the Grove principle *"managerial leverage falls when the manager can't calibrate involvement to each report's TRM"* — beyond a certain team size, the manager can't run one-on-ones at Grove's cadence, and the leverage collapses.

**How to cite in the skill:** applied Grove pattern, not Grove himself.

### Netflix's Freedom & Responsibility culture

Grove-adjacent. Netflix's "context, not control" is a version of Grove's high-TRM management style — objective-setting + minimal involvement. But Netflix pushes further than Grove would (skipping the low-TRM calibration entirely for senior hires), which produces both the famous Netflix wins and the famous Netflix cultural bruises.

**How to cite in the skill:** applied Grove pattern, extended past Grove's own boundaries. Useful as a contrast case — Grove would probably have said Netflix skips the TRM-low phase for new hires and pays the cost in mistakes.

### The AWS "Andy Jassy 6-page memo" practice

Not from Grove, but consistent with his production-frame instinct. Amazon replaces PowerPoint with narrative memos + silent reading + structured discussion. This is Grove's "detect problems at the lowest-value stage possible" applied to the strategy meeting — the writing forces the argument to be complete before the meeting, so the meeting is only for the decision.

**How to cite in the skill:** applied Grove principle, extended into a specific mechanic Amazon invented.
