# High Output Management — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Grove's specific mechanics — that separate applying *HOM* well from doing the paraphrased version most managers cite. Attribution is precise: this comes from *HOM* Ch. X, this from *OTPS*, this from the Horowitz foreword to the 2015 reprint, this from the Doerr/Zhuo/Hughes Johnson extensions.

## Do's

### Do define output as team output, not individual work

Grove's opening move. The moment you're managing, your output equation is:

> **A manager's output = the output of his organization + the output of the neighboring organizations under his influence.** — *HOM*, Ch. 3

Your own tasks are inputs to that equation, not the output. A manager who spends the week head-down producing individual work is a manager who is failing at the manager job — by Grove's definition, not by soft opinion.

**How to apply:** at the end of any week, ask *"how did my team's output move, and how did the neighboring teams' output move?"* If the answer is *"I don't know, but I produced X, Y, Z myself"* — the leverage is missing.

### Do delegate what you know best

The single most-counterintuitive Grove move.

> **"Because it is easier to monitor something with which you are familiar, if you have a choice you should delegate those activities you know best."** — *HOM*, Ch. 5

**Why:** you can only monitor delegation at the granularity your own knowledge supports. Delegating your unknowns *feels* like empowerment but is actually abdication with plausible deniability.

**How to apply:** when picking what to delegate, don't pick the tasks you find boring. Pick the tasks you're most competent at (and would enjoy keeping). Those are the ones you can meaningfully monitor.

### Do run one-on-ones as the subordinate's meeting

> **"A one-on-one should be regarded as the subordinate's meeting, with its agenda and tone set by him."** — *HOM*, on one-on-ones

**Mechanics that Grove is explicit about:**
- **Cadence:** weekly for reports with low task-relevant maturity; less often (every 2–4 weeks) for veterans. New assignment → increase frequency.
- **Duration:** **one hour minimum.** Shorter meetings let people avoid difficult topics; the hard conversations surface around minute 20–30.
- **Agenda:** the subordinate builds it. The manager brings questions in reserve, not the agenda.
- **Notes:** mandatory. *"Equally important is what 'writing it down' symbolizes... the act implies a commitment, like a handshake, that something will be done."*
- **Grove's Principle of Didactic Management:** *"When the supervisor thinks the subordinate has said all he wants about a subject, he should ask another question."* End the meeting when *the topic* has bottomed out, not when the subordinate has stopped talking.

**Horowitz's extension (2015 foreword):** the one-on-one is *"perhaps the best source for organizational knowledge that a manager can get."* Horowitz has reportedly fired managers for not doing one-on-ones — that is the leverage they represent.

### Do use the six-question audit before calling any decision meeting

> Before the meeting starts, answer:
> 1. What decision needs to be made?
> 2. When does it have to be made?
> 3. Who will decide?
> 4. Who will need to be consulted prior to making the decision?
> 5. Who will ratify or veto the decision?
> 6. Who will need to be informed of the decision?
> — *HOM*, Ch. 4

If you cannot answer 1–6, cancel the meeting. You don't have a decision-making meeting — you have a chat. The modal failure of decision meetings is #3 (who decides), and everyone leaves the room ambiguous.

### Do let the most junior person speak first

Grove's guard against groupthink. When the senior person speaks first, everyone else anchors to that opinion. When the junior speaks first, the room hears their unbiased read + gets a training moment.

### Do run performance reviews to change future behavior, not to justify past pay

> **"Level with your report. Listen. Leave yourself out."** — *HOM*, Ch. 13

**Length discipline:** *"Less is often more."* The point is behavior change, and behavior change requires the report to absorb and act — not to be handed a laundry list.

**Horowitz's extension (Tim Ferriss, 2019, ep. 392):** *"You've got to put in the review. It's bad that you didn't tell him, but it's worse to not tell him now."* — Grove, quoted by Horowitz on the failure to name hard truths in reviews.

### Do treat promotions as your loudest cultural signal

Grove: promotions communicate values more powerfully than any speech or memo. Every promotion tells the org what "good" looks like. *"Values and behavioral norms are simply not transmitted easily by talk or memo, but are conveyed very effectively by doing and doing visibly."* (*HOM*)

**Grove's own practice:** promoted the person who exemplified the culture, even if the technically best candidate had a different profile. The cultural signal is worth more than the local optimization.

### Do build a dual ladder

Not every senior IC should be a manager. If your comp/promotion system forces the choice, you break both tracks — you get bad managers *and* you lose your best ICs.

Grove pioneered this at Intel. It is now standard in tech; the practice comes from Grove.

### Do listen to Helpful Cassandras (*OTPS*, Ch. 5)

At an inflection point, the middle managers, salespeople, and frontline engineers see the shift before the executives. They are the Helpful Cassandras — the weak-signal detectors.

**Apply:** if a middle manager is telling you customer behavior has changed, and you find yourself explaining why they're wrong, pause. Grove's frame is that the insulation at the top is the enemy of early recognition.

### Do run the "outside CEO" thought experiment when the fundamentals feel off

> **"If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?"** — Grove to Moore, mid-1985, retold in *OTPS*, Ch. 5.

The mechanic creates psychological distance from the sunk cost. Answer the question honestly and you often see the pivot you've been avoiding.

### Do practice constructive confrontation

- **Attack problems, not people.**
- **Speed matters** — don't let disagreement sit.
- **Assume positive intent** — reduces relational cost of directness.
- **Listen with the same intensity you argue with** — reciprocal accountability.
- **After decision: disagree and commit.**

Grove's version of directness is unusual because it is *structural*, not just cultural. The meeting mechanics (six-question audit, junior speaks first, agenda-set-by-subordinate) are what make the confrontation productive rather than personal.

## Don'ts

### Don't confuse TRM with general competence

> **"Don't confuse people's Task-Relevant Maturity with their general competence."** — *HOM*, Ch. 12

**The single most-cited Grove pitfall.** A senior engineer with 15 years of experience has zero TRM in leading their first team. Manage them like a rookie in the new task, even while respecting them like a veteran in the old one.

**Horowitz's extension** (via Driverless Crocodile summary of *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*): *"Even — and maybe especially — executives are also immature in certain tasks."* New executive hires are typically *"immature in their understanding of your market, your technology, and your company."* Micromanaging new executives for a limited period accelerates learning; skipping this because they're senior is the mistake.

### Don't delegate without monitoring

Delegation ≠ abdication. Monitoring frequency maps to TRM.

- Low TRM → check work at every stage
- High TRM → verify against objectives at milestones

Not monitoring at all is *not* trust; it is negligence dressed as trust.

### Don't run meetings without the six-question audit

Especially: *who will decide?* Missing this is the modal failure of decision meetings. Everyone leaves the room without knowing, then the decision drifts back to the manager anyway three days later.

### Don't meddle

Once you've delegated at high TRM, stop touching the work. Managerial meddling has **negative** leverage — it reduces the subordinate's initiative and depresses net output.

The paradox: the manager who "just wants to help" by tweaking a report's high-TRM work is producing less team output than the manager who does nothing.

### Don't stop training your team

> **"If you are not training, then you are basically neglecting half the job."** — *HOM*

Grove's math: 12 hours training 10 people yields ~200 additional hours of team output over a year. Training is not L&D; the manager does it personally.

### Don't treat one-on-ones as status updates

If the whole hour was "here's what I did this week," you wasted the single most-leveraged tool you have. Push for problems, worries, half-formed ideas, potential problems.

**Grove's Principle of Didactic Management applied:** when the report has finished the status update, ask *"what else is on your mind?"* three times. The third answer is usually the real one.

### Don't confuse activity with output

> **"Measure a salesman by the orders he gets (output), not by the calls he makes (activity)."** — *HOM*

Applies at every level. Engineers by shipped features, not commits. PMs by outcomes, not launches. Managers by team output, not meetings held.

### Don't ignore the emotional phase of a strategic inflection point

Denial precedes acceptance. Grove insists the CEO's job at an SIP is to acknowledge the emotional weight before demanding the decision. Skipping this produces resistance later — the pivot happens on paper but not in the org.

### Don't declare an inflection point on a whim

Every quarter someone declares AI, crypto, remote work, or LLMs an SIP. Grove's 10X test is strict: the force must be **actually** ten times stronger than what the business is built to handle, not just novel. Most industry shifts are not SIPs.

### Don't skip the emotional phase of a performance issue

Grove's sequence for a poor performer: **denial → active denial → acknowledgment → responsibility → solution.** The manager's job is to move the report through the sequence, not to skip to solution and be surprised when nothing changes.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong when you think you're doing it right)

### One-on-ones become a status meeting

The manager takes over the agenda. The subordinate stops bringing problems. The output collapses to what the manager already knew.

**Fix:** return the agenda to the subordinate. Ask *"what else?"* three times. If the subordinate has nothing to bring after a month of this, that itself is a signal (they're not thinking about problems, or they don't feel safe naming them).

### OKRs become a performance-management artifact

Grove designed them as an operating cadence. When comp gets attached to OKR grades, **sandbagging** appears — reports set OKRs they know they'll hit rather than ambitious ones. The OKRs stop instrumenting strategy.

**Fix:** decouple OKR grades from compensation. Use OKRs for direction and cadence; use performance reviews for individual assessment. Roger Martin's critique — OKRs as strategy — applies exactly here. See [[radical-focus]] for OKR-native practice.

### Task-relevant maturity used to justify never delegating

The manager decides everyone has low TRM and remains a bottleneck forever.

**Fix:** TRM is task-specific *and dynamic*. If a report has been doing the task for a year with good results, their TRM is high; if you're still meddling, you're the problem, not their maturity.

### Task-relevant maturity used to justify never involving

The mirror-image mistake. The manager reads "high-TRM reports need minimal involvement" and stops one-on-ones entirely. The report goes off the rails on a new task the manager didn't notice was new.

**Fix:** TRM is per-task, not per-person. A veteran engineer starting a new architecture has *low TRM in that architecture even though high TRM in engineering*. Grove: *"Don't confuse people's Task-Relevant Maturity with their general competence."*

### Constructive confrontation becomes personal

Without the "attack problems, not people" discipline, the culture turns into pointed cruelty that then defends itself as "Grove-like directness."

**Horowitz's *What You Do Is Who You Are* (2019) is explicit about this drift.** The culture pattern requires the discipline; the discipline without the structure produces bullying.

### Strategic inflection points get called too early

Every quarter, someone calls an SIP. Grove's 10X test is strict. If you're not sure whether the force is 10X, it isn't yet — SIPs are unmistakable when they arrive.

### The 2015 revival makes people read the book without the bridge

*HOM* was written in 1983 for a middle manager at a chip factory. Some passages (indicators for raw-material inventory) don't translate literally to software. The **frameworks** translate cleanly; the **examples** sometimes need reinterpretation.

**Fix:** when the user is in a modern context, translate through the carriers — Horowitz on managing at Loudcloud, Hughes Johnson on cadences at Stripe, Zhuo on first-time manager onboarding at Facebook. See `references/post-book.md`.

### "Only the Paranoid Survive" read as a permanent operating mode

Grove meant strategic vigilance at inflection points. Reading it as *"always be paranoid, always be at 10X-alert"* produces an exhausting culture and false-positive strategic pivots.

**Fix:** paranoia is diagnostic, not identity. Use it when the fundamentals feel off; return to normal operating mode otherwise.

## Common misapplications (people saying they're doing HOM but aren't)

### "Grove says micromanage new employees" — half the sentence

Grove says *manage tightly for tasks with low TRM*. That's calibrated involvement, not micromanagement as an identity. Once TRM rises, involvement falls. Reading only the first half produces a chronic micromanager who cites Grove.

### "Grove says weekly one-on-ones" — task-relevant

Grove says **weekly for low-TRM reports, less often for veterans**. Making every one-on-one weekly forever, regardless of TRM, wastes leverage on veterans and communicates distrust.

### OKRs as a substitute for strategy

See [[radical-focus]] and Roger Martin's [Stop Letting OKRs Masquerade as Strategy](https://rogermartin.medium.com/stop-letting-okrs-masquerade-as-strategy-a57fc2cea915). OKRs are an operating cadence; strategy has to come from somewhere else. Grove himself in *HOM* Ch. 7 is clear that OKRs cascade *from* strategic decisions, not instead of them.

### "Constructive confrontation" as license to be a jerk

The pattern requires the four disciplines (attack problems, speed, positive intent, listen-as-hard-as-you-argue) *and* the structural instruments (six-question audit, junior speaks first). Take the confrontation without the discipline and the structure and you get an unpleasant culture that then blames Grove.

### One-on-ones "as needed" rather than on cadence

The whole point is that the subordinate knows the meeting is coming and can save problems for it. Ad-hoc one-on-ones ("my door is open") do not produce the same behavior — the subordinate doesn't build the habit of bringing problems.

### Filling out an OKR template at the offsite, then going back to running the company the way you were

The most common OKR failure. The document exists; nothing changes. Grove would name this immediately.

**Test for the real thing:** are any decisions actually being made differently after the OKR cycle started? If not, no OKRs were produced — you produced a document.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts that keep the method honest:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| My work | My team's output | Grove's equation puts the team, not the individual, as the output |
| Delegating | Delegating with monitoring | Delegation without monitoring is abdication |
| Micromanagement (as an insult) | Calibrating involvement to TRM | Sometimes the report has low TRM and needs it |
| Empowerment | Delegation with monitoring at the right cadence | "Empowerment" often masks abdication |
| Doing more one-on-ones | Calibrating one-on-one cadence to TRM | More is not better; matched is better |
| OKRs are our strategy | OKRs cascade from our strategy | Grove's *HOM* Ch. 7 is explicit on this |
| We need to be more paranoid | Are we at an inflection point? Apply the 10X test | Grove meant diagnostic vigilance, not permanent anxiety |
| Execution vs. strategy | Distributed strategic choices at every level | See Roger Martin's chartering essays for the extension |
| My direct report is bad at their job | Is this a can't or a won't? — *HOM*, Ch. 13 | Grove's diagnostic; the fix differs |
| The team is stuck | Where is the limiting step? | Grove's production frame applied to organizational output |
| Culture problem | Which specific mechanic is missing? | Grove's fix for culture is structural, not vibes |
