# High Output Management — Applications

> Where Grove's method fits, where it doesn't, and which adjacent frameworks to reach for instead when the user's actual problem is a different shape than *HOM* was designed to solve.

## Where Grove fits (use this skill)

### The core: any manager-of-anyone with 3+ reports

*HOM* was written for the middle manager at a chip factory. The frameworks — output equation, managerial leverage, TRM, one-on-ones, staff meetings, six-question decision audit, cascaded OKRs, performance reviews — apply cleanly to any manager whose job includes:

- Getting other people to produce output
- Making decisions with team input
- Building and calibrating a meeting cadence
- Delegating and monitoring
- Reviewing performance
- Hiring and promoting

Below is a scenario-by-scenario map of where the skill is the right tool.

### Building or fixing a one-on-one cadence

The single most-common trigger. Users say *"my 1:1s aren't working,"* *"they're just status updates,"* *"my report doesn't bring anything to them."* The skill's specific answer:

- **Cadence calibrated to TRM.** Weekly for low-TRM reports; less often for high-TRM veterans.
- **One hour minimum.** Not 30 minutes.
- **Subordinate sets the agenda.** Not the manager.
- **"Ask one more question."** End the meeting when the topic bottoms out, not when the report stops talking.

See `references/method.md` § *One-on-ones* and `references/heuristics.md` § *Do run one-on-ones as the subordinate's meeting*.

### Auditing the meeting stack

The user has too many meetings and can't tell which to cut. Grove's meeting taxonomy (process-oriented vs. mission-oriented) is the diagnostic:

- **Process-oriented** (one-on-ones, staff, operation reviews): recurring, predictable, structured. These are the leverage — cut cautiously.
- **Mission-oriented** (decision-forcing, ad-hoc): should be <25% of manager meeting time. If more, something is broken about the process-oriented cadence.

For every mission-oriented meeting: run the **six-question audit**. If you can't answer 1–6, cancel the meeting.

### Running a decision meeting that keeps drifting

The classic Grove case. The meeting happened, everyone talked, nothing decided — or a decision was made and then quietly reversed by the manager three days later. **Fix: the six-question audit before the meeting.** Especially question 3 — *who will decide?* — which is the modal miss.

### Cascading OKRs

Grove is the origin of OKRs (via Doerr). This skill is the right tool for the *manager's job around OKRs*: how to write them, how to cascade them, how to grade them, how to keep them from becoming a performance-management artifact.

For OKR-native operational practice at startup scale — mechanics of the OKR cycle itself — prefer [[radical-focus]].

For the strategy question OKRs are meant to serve — *what to bet on, how to differentiate, where to win* — prefer [[playing-to-win]], [[good-strategy-bad-strategy]], or [[7-powers]]. OKRs are an operating cadence; strategy has to come from somewhere else.

### Delegating and worrying about micromanagement

The TRM diagnostic. The user is either:
- **Over-involved on a high-TRM report's task** — meddling with negative leverage. Fix: step back to milestone-monitoring.
- **Under-involved on a low-TRM task** — abdication dressed as trust. Fix: increase monitoring frequency; don't apologize for it.

Grove's counterintuitive move: **delegate what you know best**, because you can only monitor what you understand.

### Giving a hard performance review or dealing with an underperformer

Grove's diagnostic: *can't or won't?*

- **Can't** — capability gap. Fix: training, coaching, or moving to a role where their TRM matches (Grove's "recycle" move; better than firing).
- **Won't** — motivation gap. Fix: name the specific behavior + the specific expectation + the specific consequence. *"Level with your report. Listen. Leave yourself out."*

For the emotional / interpersonal dimension of the hard conversation — *how* to say it — prefer [[radical-candor]] (Kim Scott's care + challenge framing). Grove assumes the care and focuses on the mechanics.

### Hiring — designing the interview loop

Grove's rules: applicant talks 80%; go deep on failures ("what are your most significant failures and what did you learn?"); no tricks; the point is to give the applicant runway to reveal how they think.

For interview loops specifically — question banks, calibration, panel structure — Hughes Johnson's *Scaling People* extends Grove into modern startup-scale practice.

### Transitioning from IC to manager (or manager to manager-of-managers)

The classic low-TRM management moment. A senior IC promoted into management has high TRM in the craft, low TRM in managing. Grove's approach: manage them like a rookie in the management task, respectfully. Julie Zhuo's *The Making of a Manager* is the modern extension explicitly for this transition.

### Suspecting a strategic inflection point

The *OTPS* frame. Diagnostic questions:
1. Is one of the six competitive forces (Porter's Five + complementors) shifting by ~10X?
2. Are Helpful Cassandras (middle managers, salespeople, frontline engineers) signaling something the executives are dismissing?
3. Run the outside-CEO thought experiment: *"If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what would they do?"*

If yes to all three — likely an SIP. Response: acknowledge emotional resistance → enable bottom-up input → implement top-down direction with consistent messaging.

### Rebuilding culture around directness / constructive confrontation

Grove is the source of "attack problems, not people" + "disagree and commit." The skill is the right tool for a manager building this pattern, especially if the culture is drifting into either avoidance (no confrontation) or cruelty (personal attacks).

Grove's key insight: the culture pattern requires the structural mechanics (six-question audit, junior speaks first, agenda-set-by-subordinate). Without the mechanics, the confrontation turns personal. Horowitz's *What You Do Is Who You Are* extends this into a full culture-building framework.

## Where Grove does NOT fit (don't use this skill; use another)

### Pre-PMF founder deciding what to build

Grove is about running the machine, not searching for the machine. A pre-PMF founder using *HOM* is running a factory with no product-market fit — the leverage math is moot. Prefer discovery frameworks; work backwards from customer job.

### Strategy questions

*"Where should we play?" "How do we differentiate?" "What's our unfair advantage?"* — these are strategy questions Grove's operations frame does not answer. Prefer:

- **[[playing-to-win]]** — Martin's 5-question integrated cascade
- **[[good-strategy-bad-strategy]]** — Rumelt's kernel (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action)
- **[[7-powers]]** — Helmer's Power taxonomy for durable competitive advantage

Grove's *OTPS* touches strategy but only at the specific moment of the strategic inflection point — it is not a general strategy tool.

### Small (<5-person) all-IC team without formal management

The Grove mechanics assume a manager whose job is to produce team output through people. On a small all-IC team where everyone is producing individual work, formal one-on-ones, staff meetings, and OKR cascades add more overhead than leverage. Grove's principles (output equation, leverage) still apply informally, but the specific instrumentation is over-engineered.

### The relational / interpersonal dimension of management

Grove assumes the relational dimension. He is not going to teach you how to build trust with a struggling report, how to have an emotionally-charged conversation, or how to hold care and challenge in the same sentence. For that dimension prefer:

- **[[radical-candor]]** — Kim Scott's care personally + challenge directly framework
- Modern operator books that spend more airtime on the relational (Zhuo, Hughes Johnson)

### OKR mechanics at startup scale

Grove originated OKRs, but he wrote for Intel-scale (~10K employees). For a founder implementing OKRs on a 20-person team who has never done it before, prefer [[radical-focus]] (Christina Wodtke). Same intellectual DNA; format optimized for the smaller context.

### Culture-first / values-first company building

Grove's culture claim is structural: *"Values and behavioral norms are simply not transmitted easily by talk or memo, but are conveyed very effectively by doing and doing visibly."* But he doesn't write a whole framework for company-culture design. For that: Horowitz's *What You Do Is Who You Are*, Ben Casnocha / Reid Hoffman on Alliance-style culture, or PatriotSong / Andy Grove's own values-as-behaviors approach as extended by Hughes Johnson.

### Deep interpersonal coaching / therapy-adjacent work

Grove is a management operator. If the user needs coaching-style depth — around identity, purpose, values, life direction — Grove is the wrong tool. Prefer coaching frameworks; or in the executive-coaching adjacent space, some of the more recent operator-writers.

## Composing Grove with other frameworks

### Grove + [[playing-to-win]] (Roger Martin)

The two most natural composers in this repo. Martin answers *what to do*; Grove answers *how to do it well*.

- Use Martin to set the strategy: winning aspiration, where to play, how to win, capabilities, management systems.
- Use Grove to instrument the last two: *management systems* is where Grove takes over. Cascaded OKRs, one-on-ones, staff meetings, performance reviews — these are Grove's territory and Martin doesn't dwell on them.

**Martin's explicit warning about OKRs** — [Stop Letting OKRs Masquerade as Strategy](https://rogermartin.medium.com/stop-letting-okrs-masquerade-as-strategy-a57fc2cea915) — is the exact caveat Grove would have endorsed. OKRs cascade *from* strategy; they don't replace it.

### Grove + [[good-strategy-bad-strategy]] (Richard Rumelt)

Rumelt: diagnose, guiding policy, coherent action, the crux. Grove: instrument the coherent action once you've named it.

Use Rumelt when the user's problem is *what is actually wrong* and *what is the pivotal challenge*. Use Grove once the diagnosis is done and the question shifts to *how do we run this coherent action reliably*.

### Grove + [[radical-candor]] (Kim Scott)

Structural + relational. Grove: level with your report. Scott: care personally AND challenge directly. Both authors would recognize each other; Scott spends more airtime on the "care" dimension Grove assumed.

**Composed:** Grove's structural mechanics (one-on-ones on cadence, six-question audit, TRM-calibrated involvement) + Scott's relational framework (how to actually deliver the direct feedback without dropping the care). The manager who runs both is doing Grove's instrumentation with Scott's relational skill.

### Grove + [[radical-focus]] (Christina Wodtke)

Grove is the OKR origin. Wodtke is the OKR practice manual for post-Grove startups. Compose:

- **Grove:** OKRs cascade, one manager's key results become their reports' objectives, the operating cadence sits inside the manager's job.
- **Wodtke:** the specific mechanics of OKR-setting at 5–50 person scale, the weekly Monday commitments + Friday celebrations, avoiding the trap of OKRs-as-tasks.

Together: Grove gives the theory and the cascade; Wodtke gives the practice.

### Grove + [[7-powers]] (Hamilton Helmer)

Different levels. Helmer: does the business have durable Power (Benefit + Barrier)? Grove: are you running the machine well?

Compose when the user is diagnosing why the business is stuck. Helmer diagnoses *strategic* stuck-ness (no Power, or the wrong Power for the stage). Grove diagnoses *operational* stuck-ness (no leverage, meddling, decisions not sticking, OKRs disconnected from work).

### Grove + [[obviously-awesome]] (April Dunford)

Different domains, occasional composition. Dunford does positioning; Grove does operations. Compose when the user is deciding *"why isn't our sales team closing?"* and the answer might be either positioning (Dunford) or operational — sales manager not running one-on-ones, no OKRs, no clear who-decides on discounts, etc. (Grove).

## Modern operational context — where the carriers help

Grove wrote in 1983. Some modern operating contexts he didn't address; the carriers have partially extended:

- **Distributed / remote teams** — no strict carrier extension yet. Grove's mechanics translate but with friction. Reason from principles.
- **Async communication** — same. Grove's meetings taxonomy assumes synchronous. Async is open ground.
- **AI-agent management** — completely new context. The output equation for a manager whose "team" includes AI agents is an open question.
- **High-growth startup operations (10x/year headcount)** — Hughes Johnson's *Scaling People* is the modern carrier.
- **First-time-manager onboarding at scale** — Zhuo's *The Making of a Manager* is the modern carrier.
- **New-executive TRM** — Horowitz's extension in *The Hard Thing About Hard Things*.

When the user is in one of these contexts, the skill should acknowledge Grove didn't address it directly, then reason from the underlying principles (output equation, leverage, TRM) — or point to the specific carrier who has partial coverage.

## Quick decision tree

Ask the user's situation:

1. **"Is this an operations question or a strategy question?"**
   - Operations → this skill.
   - Strategy → [[playing-to-win]], [[good-strategy-bad-strategy]], [[7-powers]].

2. **"Is this about the manager's job (leverage, meetings, decisions, delegation, reviews) or the individual work itself?"**
   - Manager's job → this skill.
   - Individual work → this is not a management question; different frame entirely.

3. **"Is the mechanic clear and the failure is relational, or is the relational fine and the mechanic missing?"**
   - Mechanic missing → this skill.
   - Relational failure → [[radical-candor]] (compose with this skill for the structural piece).

4. **"Is this OKR mechanics at startup scale?"**
   - Yes → [[radical-focus]] first; this skill for the surrounding manager job.

5. **"Is this a strategic inflection point?"**
   - Yes → this skill (*OTPS* chapter of it).
   - Not sure → this skill can help you apply the 10X test to decide.
