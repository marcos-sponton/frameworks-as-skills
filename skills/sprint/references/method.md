# Sprint — Method

> The five-day structure in Jake Knapp's own terms — each day's exercises, roles, outputs, and the mechanics that make them work. From the 2016 book *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days* (Simon & Schuster), co-authored with John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz, plus post-book adaptations (remote sprints, 4-day format). Attribution is precise: this is from the book unless noted otherwise.

## The frame

Everything below rests on one premise: **you can fast-forward into the future to see how customers react before you invest all the time and expense of building the real thing.**

A sprint replaces months of debate, planning, prototyping, and testing with a single intense week. The output is not a product — it's validated learning about whether the direction is right.

Knapp's origin principle, from the book's introduction:

> "Instead of waiting to launch a minimal product to understand if an idea is any good, you'll get clear data from a realistic prototype."
> — *Sprint*, Introduction.

And the operational disposition:

> "The sprint gives our startups a superpower: they can fast-forward into the future to see their finished product and customer reactions, before making any expensive commitments."
> — *Sprint*, Introduction.

## Before the sprint — setup

### The right challenge

Not every problem deserves a sprint. The right challenge is:

- **High stakes.** The answer matters — revenue, product direction, strategic bet.
- **Not enough time.** Urgency creates focus. "We need to figure this out" problems.
- **Stuck.** The team has been debating without progress, or multiple options look equally plausible.

Knapp's rule: "Sprints are most useful when the stakes are high, when there's not enough time, or when you're just plain stuck." — *Sprint*, Ch. 1.

### The team (7 or fewer)

The ideal sprint team has **7 or fewer people.** More than 7 slows the exercises and dilutes focus.

**Required roles:**
- **The Decider** — the person with authority to make the final call. CEO, VP of Product, founder — someone who can say "we're doing this" and mean it. Without a Decider, Wednesday stalls. This is the single most important role.
- **The Facilitator** — runs the exercises, manages time, keeps the group on track. Often (but not always) the person who knows the sprint process best. Not necessarily the most senior person.
- **Finance expert** — someone who understands the business model and constraints.
- **Marketing expert** — someone who understands the customer, messaging, and channel.
- **Customer expert** — someone who talks to customers regularly (support, sales, research).
- **Tech/logistics expert** — someone who understands what's buildable and what's not.
- **Design expert** — someone who understands the product's design and user experience.

Knapp's rule on the Decider: "Without a Decider, decisions won't stick. We've tried sprints without a Decider, and they just don't work as well." — *Sprint*, Ch. 2.

### Logistics

- **Dedicated room for the full week.** Two whiteboards minimum. Big table. No one leaves for other meetings.
- **No devices.** Laptops and phones stay outside (or in a pile). This is non-negotiable. Attention is the scarcest resource.
- **Schedule: 10 AM to 5 PM, Monday through Friday.** Mornings before 10 are for email and logistics. Afternoons end at 5 sharp — sprint fatigue is real.
- **Supplies:** whiteboards, whiteboard markers, sticky notes (3x5), dot stickers (small, two colors), paper, Sharpies, masking tape, a Time Timer (visible countdown).
- **Snacks and lunch in the room.** Don't break flow for a restaurant trip.

## Day 1 — Monday: Map and Target

**Goal:** By end of Monday, the team has agreed on a target — a specific customer and a specific moment in their journey — that the sprint will focus on.

### Exercise 1: Set a long-term goal

"If everything goes perfectly, what will our product/project look like in two years?" Write the aspirational goal on the whiteboard. This is the North Star for the week.

**Example:** "Become the #1 online store for specialty coffee equipment."

### Exercise 2: List the Sprint Questions

Sprint Questions are the risks and unknowns that could prevent the long-term goal. Phrased as "Can we...?" or "Will customers...?" questions.

**Format:** "What questions do we want to answer this week?"

**Examples:**
- "Will first-time customers understand our value proposition?"
- "Can customers complete the checkout process without help?"
- "Will doctors trust the clinical trial matching results?"

Sprint Questions frame the entire sprint. They are what Friday's test will answer.

### Exercise 3: Make a Map

Draw a simple diagram of how customers interact with the product — from discovery to the key action. Left side = actors (customers, partners). Right side = the end goal. Middle = the steps.

**Rules:**
- Keep it simple. 5-15 steps maximum.
- It's a functional map, not a service blueprint. Show what happens, not every backend process.
- The map is deliberately incomplete — it will be refined through expert interviews.

### Exercise 4: Ask the Experts

Invite 2-4 domain experts (not on the sprint team) to give 15-30 minute talks about the problem space. The sprint team listens and takes How Might We notes.

**Expert types:** customer support lead, analytics/data person, industry expert, prior effort lead.

### Exercise 5: How Might We (HMW) notes

During expert talks and discussion, each team member writes HMW notes on sticky notes — reframing problems and insights as opportunities.

**Format:** "How might we [opportunity]?"

**Examples:**
- "HMW make the first visit feel personalized?"
- "HMW reduce the number of steps in checkout?"
- "HMW help doctors trust the AI recommendation?"

**Rules:** One idea per sticky note. No discussion yet — just capture.

### Exercise 6: Organize and vote on HMW notes

1. Stick all HMW notes on the wall.
2. Silently sort them into themes (affinity mapping, but fast — ~10 min).
3. Each team member gets **2 dot votes.** Place dots on the HMW notes that feel most important. Silent voting.
4. The Decider reviews the top-voted HMW notes and picks the ones that matter most. Move them to the Map where they belong.

### Exercise 7: Pick a target

The Decider picks **one target customer** and **one target event on the Map** that the sprint will focus on. This is the moment the team will prototype and test.

**Knapp's rule:** "You can't do everything in one sprint. Pick one target — one customer, one moment — and go deep." — *Sprint*, Ch. 5.

**Monday output:** A Map on the whiteboard with a circled target, Sprint Questions on the wall, and prioritized HMW notes attached to the Map.

## Day 2 — Tuesday: Sketch

**Goal:** By end of Tuesday, each team member has created a detailed Solution Sketch — a concrete proposal for how to solve the problem at the target.

### Exercise 1: Lightning Demos (inspiration gathering)

Each team member spends the morning looking at existing solutions — not competitors, but **inspiring examples from any industry.** Then each person gives a **3-minute tour** of 1-3 great solutions they found.

**Rules:**
- Big ideas from anywhere. Not just competitors.
- 3 minutes per person, strict.
- The Facilitator captures the best ideas on a whiteboard as a shared reference ("Big Ideas" board).

**Knapp's frame:** Lightning Demos "are a fast way for the team to collect a catalog of inspiring solutions. It's important to look for great solutions outside your own industry." — *Sprint*, Ch. 8.

### Exercise 2: The 4-Step Sketch

The afternoon is individual work. No collaboration. Each person works through four steps alone:

**Step 1 — Notes (20 min).** Walk around the room. Review the goal, Sprint Questions, Map, HMW notes, Lightning Demos board. Take personal notes.

**Step 2 — Ideas (20 min).** Rough ideas. Doodles, flows, wireframes — anything. Quantity over quality. Private.

**Step 3 — Crazy 8s (8 min).** Fold a sheet of paper into 8 panels. Sketch 8 variations of your best idea — one per panel, one minute each. Forces rapid divergent thinking. Most people hate this exercise; it's the most productive one.

**Step 4 — Solution Sketch (30-90 min).** A detailed, 3-panel (or more) sketch of your best solution idea. This is the artifact that will be evaluated on Wednesday.

**Rules for the Solution Sketch:**
- **Self-explanatory.** It must make sense without the author presenting it. On Wednesday, the author stays silent.
- **Anonymous.** No names on sketches. Solutions are evaluated on merit, not authorship.
- **Detailed enough to prototype.** Not a vague concept — a step-by-step flow showing what the customer sees and does.
- **Words matter.** Include real headlines, real button text, real copy. Not "Lorem ipsum."

**Knapp's frame on individual work:** "We believe the best results come from individual work — not group brainstorming. There's nothing wrong with building on one another's ideas, but we find the best solutions come from individual effort." — *Sprint*, Ch. 7.

**Tuesday output:** One Solution Sketch per team member, anonymous, taped to the wall face-down (revealed Wednesday).

## Day 3 — Wednesday: Decide

**Goal:** By end of Wednesday, the team has chosen one solution (or a combination) and created a step-by-step Storyboard that will guide Thursday's prototype.

### Exercise 1: Art Museum

Tape all Solution Sketches to the wall. The team walks around silently, reviewing each sketch. No talking. Just reading and absorbing.

### Exercise 2: Heat Map

Each team member gets dot stickers. Place dots on parts of the sketches you find interesting, compelling, or important. Cluster dots = hot spots.

**Rules:** Silent. No discussion. Just dots. Place as many as you want — wherever you see something good.

### Exercise 3: Speed Critique

The Facilitator leads a structured 3-minute discussion of each sketch:

1. **Narrator** (the Facilitator) describes the sketch to the group.
2. **Team** calls out standout ideas (captured on sticky notes above the sketch).
3. **Author** stays silent — reveals themselves only at the end to clarify if needed.

3 minutes per sketch, strict.

### Exercise 4: Straw Poll

Each team member gets **one vote** (a large dot sticker, different color from Heat Map dots). Place your vote on the sketch (or part of a sketch) you think should be prototyped.

Write a short note next to your dot explaining why. This is a non-binding signal — it informs the Decider but doesn't decide.

### Exercise 5: The Supervote

**The Decider gets 3 Supervotes** (extra-large dots, distinct color). The Decider places their votes on the sketch(es) that will be prototyped. The Decider's vote is final.

This is where the Decider mechanism earns its keep. Without it, the team either deadlocks or compromises — both bad outcomes.

### Exercise 6: Rumble or All-in-One?

If the Supervotes land on a single sketch: **All-in-One.** Prototype that solution.

If the Supervotes are split across 2 sketches: the Decider chooses — **Rumble** (prototype both and test head-to-head on Friday) or **All-in-One** (pick one). Rumble is powerful when two genuinely different approaches exist.

**Knapp's rule:** "If you can test two approaches in one day, do it. You'll learn twice as much." — *Sprint*, Ch. 11.

### Exercise 7: Storyboard

The team creates a **step-by-step storyboard** — typically 10-15 frames on a whiteboard — showing the customer's journey through the prototype. This is Thursday's building plan.

**Rules:**
- Start with an **opening scene** — how does the customer discover/encounter this? (Google search, email, app store, friend's recommendation.)
- Each frame = one screen or one interaction step.
- Include real copy, real button text, real data.
- Detailed enough that Thursday's prototype team can build from it without guessing.

**Wednesday output:** A Storyboard on the whiteboard. This is Thursday's blueprint.

## Day 4 — Thursday: Prototype

**Goal:** By end of Thursday, the team has a realistic prototype that looks real enough to provoke honest reactions from Friday's test customers.

### The Goldilocks quality principle

The prototype must be **"just right"** — not too polished (wastes time, makes the team attached), not too rough (customers can't engage with it honestly).

Knapp's term: **"Goldilocks quality."** "The sweet spot between too detailed and too rough. It should look real, but you should be able to build it in a day." — *Sprint*, Ch. 12.

### What "prototype" means in Sprint

A Sprint prototype is a **facade.** It looks like a real product but has no real backend, no real data, no real functionality. It's a movie set — convincing from the front, empty from the back.

**Common prototype tools:**
- **Keynote / PowerPoint / Google Slides** — for clickable screen flows. Knapp's preferred tool. Fast, flexible, real enough.
- **Figma / Sketch / InVision** — for higher-fidelity screen prototypes.
- **Paper and physical materials** — for physical product concepts.
- **Video / motion graphics** — for concepts that are hard to fake interactively.
- **A modified existing product** — use an existing live product with modified content/branding.

### Prototype team roles

Divide the sprint team into roles for Thursday:

- **Maker(s)** (2-3 people) — build the prototype screens/flows. The core builders.
- **Stitcher** (1 person) — assembles the pieces into a coherent flow. Ensures transitions work and the story holds.
- **Writer** (1 person) — writes all the text: headlines, body copy, button labels, error messages. Real words, not lorem ipsum.
- **Asset Collector** (1 person) — gathers images, icons, logos, data for the prototype. Finds or creates realistic content.
- **Interviewer** (1 person) — prepares Friday's interview script and test logistics. Reviews the prototype with fresh eyes. Does NOT build — stays objective.

### The trial run

At 3-4 PM Thursday, the Interviewer does a **trial run** — walks through the prototype as if they were a customer. The team watches. This surfaces broken flows, missing screens, confusing copy. Fix issues before Friday.

**Thursday output:** A clickable, realistic-looking prototype and a tested interview script.

## Day 5 — Friday: Test

**Goal:** By end of Friday, the team has tested the prototype with 5 real customers, identified patterns, and answered the Sprint Questions.

### Why 5 customers

Knapp draws on Jakob Nielsen's research: **5 users surface approximately 85% of usability problems.** After 5, diminishing returns set in fast. More than 5 rarely reveals fundamentally new patterns — it just confirms what you already learned.

> "After just five interviews, big patterns will emerge."
> — *Sprint*, Ch. 14.

### Recruiting

Recruit **5 customers** who match the target customer profile. Ideally recruited the previous week (Monday at the latest). Offer a gift card or similar incentive. Schedule 60-minute slots, staggered through the day (9 AM, 10:30, noon, 1:30, 3).

### The 5-Act Interview

Each customer session follows a structured **5-act script:**

**Act 1 — Friendly welcome (5 min).** Build rapport. Explain the session format. "There are no right or wrong answers." Ask the customer to think aloud.

**Act 2 — Context questions (10 min).** Open-ended questions about the customer's life/work relevant to the product. "Tell me about the last time you..." Understand their current world before showing the prototype.

**Act 3 — Introduction to the prototype (3 min).** "This is an early concept. Some things work, some don't. I didn't design this, so you won't hurt my feelings." Frame it as a test of the prototype, not the person.

**Act 4 — Tasks and observation (30 min).** Ask the customer to complete specific tasks using the prototype. Observe. Ask "What are you thinking?" and "What do you expect to happen?" Do not guide, correct, or explain. Let the customer struggle when they struggle — that's data.

**Act 5 — Debrief (5 min).** Quick reactions. "What stood out to you?" "How does this compare to [current solution]?" "Would you use this?"

### The observation room

While the Interviewer conducts the session in one room, the rest of the sprint team watches on a **live video feed** in another room. They take notes on sticky notes — one observation per note.

**Color coding:**
- Green sticky = positive reaction / understanding.
- Red/pink sticky = negative reaction / confusion / frustration.
- Yellow = neutral observation.

### Finding patterns

After all 5 interviews, the team reviews the sticky notes, looking for **patterns across customers:**

- Something 3+ customers reacted to the same way = strong pattern.
- Something 2 customers flagged = notable but inconclusive.
- Something only 1 customer noticed = worth noting but not actionable yet.

### Answering Sprint Questions

Return to Monday's Sprint Questions. For each one: **did the test answer it? What did we learn?** Some questions will have clear answers. Some will be "we need more data." Some will surprise you.

**Friday output:** Patterns documented, Sprint Questions answered, and a clear sense of what to do next — build it, iterate it, pivot, or kill it.

## Key mechanisms (cross-cutting)

### Note-and-Vote (silent decision-making)

Used at every decision point in the sprint. The pattern:

1. **Write.** Individuals write ideas/votes silently on sticky notes or with dot stickers.
2. **Vote.** Simultaneously reveal. Dot stickers for priorities.
3. **Discuss.** Only after voting. The vote exposes the group's raw thinking before social dynamics distort it.

**Why:** Prevents the loudest voice (usually the most senior) from anchoring the group. Silent first, then discuss. This is the anti-brainstorming mechanism.

### The Decider

One person with real authority. Not a proxy, not a committee. Typically the CEO, founder, VP of Product, or whoever is actually accountable for the product.

**Where the Decider acts:**
- Monday: picks the target.
- Wednesday: casts the Supervote. Decides Rumble vs. All-in-One.
- Any time: breaks ties.

**Without a Decider:** The sprint team debates endlessly on Wednesday, compromises to avoid conflict, and produces a watered-down prototype nobody believes in.

### Sprint Questions (the framing device)

Sprint Questions turn anxiety into testable hypotheses. They are the bridge between Monday's Map and Friday's test.

**Good Sprint Questions:**
- Specific: "Will first-time customers understand what 'Pour Over Kit' means?"
- Testable: You can observe whether customers understand it in a 60-minute session.
- Important: The answer changes what you build.

**Bad Sprint Questions:**
- Vague: "Will customers like it?"
- Untestable in a day: "Will this increase retention over 6 months?"
- Already answered: "Do customers want faster delivery?" (They always do.)

### How Might We (HMW)

A reframing tool — not a brainstorming prompt. Takes a problem or insight and turns it into an opportunity statement.

**The mechanic:** When you hear a problem, write "How might we..." on a sticky note and reframe it.

- Heard: "Customers abandon checkout because of surprise shipping costs."
- HMW: "How might we show shipping costs early?"
- HMW: "How might we make shipping feel like a value-add?"

**Rules:** One per sticky. Write fast. Don't evaluate. Evaluation happens later via dot voting.

### Crazy 8s

8 sketches in 8 minutes. One per panel on a folded sheet of paper. Forces rapid divergent thinking before the brain locks into its first idea.

**Why it works:** Most people's first idea is their most obvious idea. Crazy 8s burns through the obvious fast, forcing the brain into less-traveled territory by sketch 5, 6, 7, 8.

**Why people hate it:** It feels rushed and messy. That's the point. Comfort produces mediocrity; mild panic produces creative range.
