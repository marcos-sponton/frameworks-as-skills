# Sprint — Applications

> Where Sprint fits, where it doesn't, and adjacent frameworks to reach for instead. Sprint is a specific, time-boxed intervention — a week to answer a big question — not an ongoing operating methodology. Knowing when to sprint (and when not to) is half the skill.

## When Sprint fits

**Challenge type:** Big, uncertain questions where the stakes are high and the team is stuck or misaligned. "Should we build this?" "Will customers understand this?" "Which direction should we take?"

**Team stage:** Any stage — pre-product, post-launch, growth, turnaround. Sprint works whenever there's a testable question and access to 5 target customers for Friday.

**Team size:** 7 or fewer for the sprint team. Larger organizations run sprints with a small cross-functional team while the rest of the org continues normal work.

**Time pressure:** Sprint is designed for urgency. If the team has months of leisurely exploration ahead, a sprint may be premature. If they need to decide in the next 2 weeks, a sprint is the tool.

**Classic triggers:**
- **New product or feature.** "We have an idea but don't know if customers want it."
- **Team misalignment.** "Everyone has a different vision. Let's test one."
- **Stuck.** "We've been debating this for months. Let's prototype and test."
- **High-stakes bet.** "We're about to commit 6 months of engineering. Should we?"
- **New market entry.** "We're entering a market we don't know. Let's test our assumptions."
- **Redesign.** "Our product needs a major overhaul. Where do we start?"

## When Sprint doesn't fit — use something else

### Ongoing product development — use Shape Up, Scrum, or Continuous Discovery

Sprint answers one question in one week. It's a one-time intervention, not a permanent operating rhythm. If the user needs a methodology for ongoing feature development, Sprint is the wrong tool.

**Reach for:** Shape Up ([[shape-up]]) for shaping and shipping feature bets in 6-week cycles. Scrum for teams that need short feedback loops with tight ceremony. Continuous Discovery Habits ([[continuous-discovery-habits]]) for ongoing weekly customer contact.

### The problem is already well-understood — just build it

Sprint is for questions, not for execution. If the team already knows what to build and why, running a sprint wastes a week confirming what they already know.

**Signal:** If the Decider says "I already know the answer, I just need the team to build it" — don't sprint. Just build.

### No access to real customers on Friday

Without Friday's test, the sprint is a workshop — ideas and decisions without validation. If you can't get 5 target customers for Friday, don't run the sprint. Recruit first, then schedule.

### No Decider available

Without a Decider, Wednesday's decision process collapses into consensus-seeking or paralysis. If the person with real authority can't commit to at least Monday and Wednesday, postpone the sprint until they can.

### The problem is too small

Sprint is expensive — a full week of 7 people's time. If the question can be answered with a quick A/B test, a customer call, or a 2-hour design review, use those lighter tools.

### Corporate strategy — use Playing to Win or Good Strategy Bad Strategy

Sprint operates at the product/feature level, not the corporate strategy level. "Which market should we enter?" or "How do we win against competitor X?" are strategy questions, not sprint questions.

**Reach for:** Roger Martin's [[playing-to-win]] cascade. Richard Rumelt's [[good-strategy-bad-strategy]] kernel.

### Early-stage idea validation (pre-solution) — use the Foundation Sprint

If the team hasn't validated that the *idea* is differentiated enough to matter — if the question is "should we build this at all?" rather than "how should we build this?" — use Knapp's Foundation Sprint (*Click*, 2025) upstream of the Design Sprint. The Foundation Sprint is a 2-day process focused on differentiation.

**Reach for:** Foundation Sprint for differentiation testing. Lean Startup ([[lean-startup]]) for market validation with MVPs.

## Adjacent frameworks — how Sprint composes (or doesn't)

### vs. Design Thinking (IDEO / Stanford d.school)

- **Design Thinking:** Open-ended process — Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. No fixed timeline. Flexible, creative, expansive.
- **Sprint:** Structured 5-day process with specific exercises in a specific order. Prescriptive. Time-boxed.

**Sprint is an opinionated distillation of design thinking.** Knapp took the principles (human-centered design, prototyping, testing) and compressed them into a repeatable recipe. The philosophical roots are shared; the execution model is completely different.

**Where Design Thinking is the better tool:** Long-term research programs. Systemic design challenges. When the problem space itself is poorly understood and needs open-ended exploration before solution work begins. When the team has the luxury of time.

**Where Sprint is the better tool:** When time is short, the question is specific, and the team needs a result by Friday. When the team is new to human-centered design and needs structure. When an open-ended process would produce debate without progress.

**They compose:** Use design thinking for broad exploration, then Sprint to test the leading candidate quickly.

### vs. Lean Startup (Eric Ries) — see [[lean-startup]]

- **Lean Startup:** Build-Measure-Learn loop. MVP experiments. Pivot or persevere decisions. Business-model validation.
- **Sprint:** Prototype and test with customers in one week. Solution validation.

**Different altitudes.** Lean Startup operates at the business-model level — "is this business viable?" Sprint operates at the solution level — "does this specific thing work for customers?"

**They compose well:** Use Lean Startup's hypothesis framing to identify the riskiest assumption. Then Sprint to test that assumption with a prototype. Knapp and Ries are mutual fans — Ries endorsed the Sprint book.

**When Lean Startup is the better tool:** When the business model itself is uncertain. When you need multiple rounds of market validation, not a single solution test.

**When Sprint is the better tool:** When you have a specific solution idea and need to test it with customers before committing to build. When the business model is viable but the product design is uncertain.

### vs. Shape Up (Ryan Singer) — see [[shape-up]]

- **Shape Up:** Ongoing product development methodology. Shaping → Betting → Building → Cool-down. 6-week cycles. For post-PMF feature work.
- **Sprint:** One-time 5-day intervention. For answering a specific question with a prototype test.

**Different problems entirely.** Sprint asks "does this solution work?" Shape Up asks "how do we ship features reliably?" Sprint is a diagnostic tool; Shape Up is an operating system.

**They compose:** Use a Sprint to validate a major bet before committing a 6-week Shape Up cycle to it. The Sprint prototype test can inform the shaped Package that goes to the Betting Table.

**When Shape Up is the better tool:** Ongoing feature development for a post-PMF product. When the team needs a sustainable cadence, not a one-time intervention.

**When Sprint is the better tool:** When the team needs to answer a big question fast. When they're considering a major new direction and want customer signal before committing.

### vs. Scrum

- **Scrum:** Ongoing development cadence. 2-week sprints, backlog, daily standups, sprint reviews, retros.
- **Sprint (Knapp):** One-time 5-day process. Completely different from a Scrum sprint, despite the shared name.

**Do not confuse these.** The naming collision is unfortunate. A Knapp Sprint is a design-and-test intervention. A Scrum sprint is a development cycle. They solve different problems.

**When Scrum is the better tool:** Ongoing development work with a known backlog. Teams that need the discipline of short cycles and regular ceremony.

**When Sprint is the better tool:** Before committing to a Scrum backlog full of features — "should we build any of this?" Or during a Scrum project when the team is stuck — "let's take a week to prototype and test the thing we've been debating."

### vs. Continuous Discovery Habits (Teresa Torres) — see [[continuous-discovery-habits]]

- **Torres:** Weekly customer interviews. Opportunity-Solution Trees. Small experiments. Ongoing discovery.
- **Sprint:** Intensive 5-day customer testing sprint. One big prototype test.

**Different cadences.** Torres is continuous and embedded in the weekly rhythm. Sprint is a one-time intervention that produces concentrated learning.

**They compose:** Torres-style continuous discovery can identify the opportunity. Sprint can test the leading solution deeply with a prototype. Then Torres-style experiments can refine the solution after the sprint.

**When Torres is the better tool:** Teams that need ongoing learning and small iterative experiments. Product teams already in steady-state development.

**When Sprint is the better tool:** When the team needs a big, focused push to answer a specific question. When they haven't done any customer testing and need a catalyst.

### vs. Jobs to be Done (JTBD)

- **JTBD:** A theory of *why* customers hire products (demand-side motivation).
- **Sprint:** A process for *testing* whether a specific solution works.

**Complementary, not competing.** JTBD can inform the Sprint Questions and the customer recruitment criteria. Sprint can test whether a product designed around a Job actually delivers.

### vs. Foundation Sprint (Click, Knapp 2025)

- **Design Sprint (2016):** "Does this solution work for customers?"
- **Foundation Sprint (2025):** "Is this idea differentiated enough to build?"

**Same author, different tools.** The Foundation Sprint sits upstream of the Design Sprint. Run a Foundation Sprint to validate differentiation. Run a Design Sprint to validate the solution. Most startups need the Foundation Sprint first.
