# Sprint — Worked Examples

> Cases Jake Knapp uses publicly to teach the Sprint method. The 2016 book grounds itself in real sprints run at Google Ventures portfolio companies — each chapter tells the story of a specific sprint with a specific company facing a specific challenge.

## Blue Bottle Coffee — the online store sprint

**Source:** *Sprint* (2016), featured prominently in the book's opening chapters.

**Company:** Blue Bottle Coffee, a specialty coffee roaster known for its in-store experience. At the time, a GV portfolio company.

**The challenge:** Blue Bottle's physical stores were magical — the ritual of hand-poured coffee, the barista relationship, the sensory experience. Their online store was... a website selling beans. The question: **how do you translate the Blue Bottle in-store experience to an online shopping experience?**

**What the sprint tested:** A prototype online store that led with the coffee experience — the origin story of each bean, the brewing method, the flavor profile — rather than a standard e-commerce grid of products.

**What they learned:** Customers who saw the story-led prototype spent more time, understood the premium pricing, and were more likely to say they'd buy. The standard e-commerce layout produced "just another coffee website" reactions.

**Why Knapp uses this case:** It illustrates the Sprint's power to test a bold direction (story-led vs. product-grid) without building either one. In a week, they had customer data to inform a decision that would otherwise have taken months of internal debate.

## Savioke — the robot delivery sprint

**Source:** *Sprint* (2016), one of the book's most memorable stories.

**Company:** Savioke, a robotics startup building autonomous delivery robots for hotels. A GV portfolio company.

**The challenge:** Savioke had built a robot that could navigate hotel hallways and deliver items to guest rooms. The technology worked. But the question wasn't technical — it was emotional: **how would hotel guests react to a robot showing up at their door?** Would they find it creepy? Delightful? Confusing?

**What the sprint tested:** Different "personality" options for the robot's behavior — how it approached the door, whether it "looked up" at the guest, how it communicated the delivery was complete. They prototyped interactions with real hotel guests.

**What they learned:** Guests loved the robot — but specific behaviors mattered enormously. A robot that "looked up" at the guest felt friendlier. A robot that made a small sound (not speech) felt less creepy than one that talked. The details of the interaction were the product.

**Why Knapp uses this case:** It's the perfect example of a question that can't be answered in a meeting room. No amount of internal debate would have predicted which robot behaviors would delight vs. creep out guests. The sprint got real emotional reactions from real people in real hotel rooms.

## Slack — the explanation sprint

**Source:** *Sprint* (2016).

**Company:** Slack, the team communication platform. At the time, still early-stage.

**The challenge:** Slack had a product people loved once they used it, but a value proposition that was hard to explain to non-users. The question: **how do you explain Slack to someone who's never used it?**

**What the sprint tested:** Different explanations and onboarding approaches. How do you describe a product that replaces email, chat, and file sharing without sounding like "just another chat app"?

**What they learned:** The most effective explanation wasn't a feature list or a comparison to email — it was showing a team using Slack and the benefits emerging naturally. The "try it and see" approach worked better than any marketing explanation.

**Why Knapp uses this case:** It illustrates that Sprint works for communication and positioning challenges, not just product design. The question "how do we explain what we are?" is testable with a prototype.

## Flatiron Health — the clinical trials sprint

**Source:** *Sprint* (2016).

**Company:** Flatiron Health, a healthcare technology company focused on oncology data. A GV portfolio company (later acquired by Roche).

**The challenge:** Getting cancer patients matched to appropriate clinical trials is a complex, high-stakes process. Oncologists don't have time to search through hundreds of trials. Patients don't know what's available. The question: **can we build a tool that helps match patients to trials efficiently?**

**What the sprint tested:** A prototype matching interface that an oncologist could use during a patient visit to quickly surface relevant clinical trials based on the patient's cancer type, stage, and history.

**What they learned:** Oncologists were willing to use the tool if (and only if) it fit into their existing workflow. A separate app that required a login and data entry was a non-starter. An interface that integrated into their existing medical record system was viable.

**Why Knapp uses this case:** It illustrates Sprint in a high-stakes, regulated domain. The prototype didn't need to be a real medical device — it just needed to look real enough for oncologists to react honestly to the workflow.

## Foundation Medicine — the report sprint

**Source:** *Sprint* (2016).

**Company:** Foundation Medicine, a genomic profiling company for cancer patients. A GV portfolio company.

**The challenge:** Foundation Medicine's reports — detailed genomic analyses — were technically accurate but difficult for oncologists to interpret quickly during patient consultations. The question: **how do you present complex genomic data in a way that's actionable during a 15-minute patient visit?**

**What they learned:** The report needed to lead with actionable information (treatment options) rather than raw data (genomic mutations). Oncologists wanted "what do I do with this patient?" not "here's every gene we sequenced."

## Post-book sprint cases

### Character Capital portfolio (2017 → present)

Since founding Character Capital, Knapp and Zeratsky have run sprints (both Design Sprints and Foundation Sprints) with 60+ portfolio companies across AI, healthcare, commerce, and industry. Specific companies mentioned in *Click* (2025) and in podcast appearances include cases from Nike, Google, and Slack (revisited), plus stories from AI research frontiers.

### The "hundreds of sprints" at GV

Knapp has referenced running 100+ sprints during his time at GV (2012-2017). Portfolio companies mentioned in various interviews beyond the book cases include:

- **Medium** (Ev Williams's publishing platform)
- **Nest** (smart home devices)
- **23andMe** (genetic testing)
- **One Medical** (primary care — later IPO)
- **RetailMeNot** (digital coupons — later IPO)

These cases are mentioned in passing in talks and interviews, not developed as full sprint stories like the book cases.

### The Three-Hour Brand Sprint

**Source:** Knapp's blog post on jakeknapp.com/Medium.

A compressed sprint format specifically for brand and positioning work. Not a full 5-day sprint — a 3-hour workshop that uses Sprint mechanics (Note-and-Vote, Lightning Demos) to rapidly align a team on brand positioning.

This is Knapp's most popular non-book sprint format, widely adopted by startups and agencies.

## Using cases in a session

**When the user is deciding whether to sprint:** Use Blue Bottle ("we had a big question and tested two approaches in a week") and Savioke ("some questions can only be answered by watching real people react").

**When the user is worried about prototyping something complex:** Use Flatiron Health ("we tested a medical workflow with a Keynote prototype — it just needed to look real enough") and Foundation Medicine ("complex data, simple prototype that tested the right question").

**When the user's challenge is communication/positioning:** Use Slack ("how do we explain what we are?" is a sprintable question).

**When the user doubts Sprint works outside tech:** Use Savioke (physical robots, real hotel guests) and the broader GV portfolio (healthcare, commerce, finance).
