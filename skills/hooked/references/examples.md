# Hooked — Worked Examples

> Cases Nir Eyal has used publicly to illustrate the Hook Model. Each case names the source so users can verify.

## Instagram

**Where Eyal used it:** *Hooked*, multiple chapters; various talks and podcast appearances.

**The situation:** Instagram became one of the most habit-forming apps in history within a few years of launch. Users open the app dozens of times per day without conscious decision.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — fear of missing a moment (FOMO), boredom, desire for social connection. External — push notifications for likes, comments, new followers, stories from close friends.
- **Action:** Open the app and scroll. Extremely simple — one tap, immediate content. Low friction, high ability.
- **Variable reward:** All three types present. **Tribe** — likes, comments, new followers (social validation). **Hunt** — the feed shows different content each time (what will appear next?). **Self** — the satisfaction of posting a well-received photo, building a curated gallery.
- **Investment:** Photos uploaded, captions written, followers accumulated, hashtags used, stories posted. Each piece of content loads the next trigger (comments, likes, shares). The more you post, the more feedback you get, the more reasons you have to come back.

**Why the case is instructive:** Instagram demonstrates all four hook phases working in concert, with all three variable reward types present simultaneously. It also illustrates the transition from external triggers (notifications) to internal triggers (the automatic urge to check).

## Pinterest

**Where Eyal used it:** *Hooked*, Chapter 4 (Variable Reward); multiple talks.

**The situation:** Pinterest created a habit around visual discovery — users return to browse, pin, and organize content that no one told them to look for.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — desire for inspiration, planning a project (wedding, home renovation, recipe collection), curiosity about visual ideas. External — email digests ("pins you might like"), push notifications.
- **Action:** Scroll through a visually rich feed. The infinite scroll design makes the action nearly frictionless — no pages to click, no decisions to make before seeing content.
- **Variable reward:** Primarily **Rewards of the Hunt** — the user is searching for the next beautiful, useful, inspiring image. Each scroll reveals something unpredictable. Also **Rewards of the Self** — the satisfaction of curating a well-organized board.
- **Investment:** Pins saved, boards created, collections organized. Each pin improves future recommendations (algorithmic learning) and builds a personal library the user doesn't want to lose. The investment loads the next trigger: a well-curated board invites return visits to add more.

**Why the case is instructive:** Pinterest is Eyal's canonical example of Rewards of the Hunt. The product's entire engagement model is built on the thrill of discovery — "what will I find next?" The infinite scroll design makes the action phase nearly frictionless, and the board system creates a powerful investment loop.

**Quotable moment:**
> "Pinterest is essentially a slot machine for ideas. You pull the lever — or in this case, scroll — and something unexpected appears." — Nir Eyal, paraphrased from talks and *Hooked*, Chapter 4

## The Bible App (YouVersion)

**Where Eyal used it:** Post-*Hooked* talks and blog posts; frequently cited as a surprising example of habit-forming design outside tech.

**The situation:** YouVersion became one of the most-used apps in the world — a Bible reading app with over 500 million installs. Not a typical "tech product" but one of the most habit-forming apps ever built.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — desire for spiritual connection, daily devotional habit, seeking guidance or comfort. External — daily verse notification (a masterful owned trigger that fires once per day at a time the user sets).
- **Action:** Read one verse. The daily verse is delivered in the notification — the user barely has to do anything. Maximum simplicity.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Tribe** — community comments, verse sharing, social features. **Rewards of the Self** — reading streaks, reading plan completion, growing biblical knowledge. **Rewards of the Hunt** — "what verse will appear today?" provides daily variability.
- **Investment:** Reading streaks (users don't want to break them), bookmarks, highlights, notes, completed reading plans, friends added. Each investment deepens commitment and loads the next trigger.

**Why the case is instructive:** The Bible App demolishes the assumption that hook design is only for consumer tech or social media. It shows the Hook Model applied to spiritual practice — a domain most people assume is "above" behavioral design. It's also a textbook Facilitator on the Manipulation Matrix: the builder uses it, and it genuinely improves users' lives (for those who value spiritual practice).

## Email

**Where Eyal used it:** *Hooked*, Chapter 2 (as the 5 Whys example); *Indistractable*, throughout.

**The situation:** Email is one of the oldest and most persistent digital habits. Users check email dozens of times per day, often compulsively.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — uncertainty ("what if someone sent me something important?"), FOMO, anxiety about being out of the loop. Eyal's 5 Whys example traces surface behavior ("I want to send messages") to root emotion ("fear of being out of the loop").
- **Action:** Open inbox, scan for new messages. Near-zero friction — the app is always running, always accessible.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Hunt** — "what's in my inbox?" provides genuine uncertainty. Each refresh might contain something important, something interesting, or nothing at all. The variability is what makes checking compulsive.
- **Investment:** Emails sent (which invite replies — loading the next trigger), contacts accumulated, filters and folders organized, threads participated in.

**Why the case is instructive:** Email is Eyal's go-to example for the 5 Whys trigger discovery technique. It also demonstrates that habit-forming products don't require sophisticated design — email's hook is structurally simple but incredibly persistent. In *Indistractable*, email becomes the example of a hook to manage: "hack back" the external trigger (turn off notifications) and schedule traction time for processing email.

## Slack

**Where Eyal used it:** Various podcast appearances (2018–2024); frequently cited as a workplace hook.

**The situation:** Slack became the default real-time communication tool for tech teams, with users checking it constantly throughout the workday.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — uncertainty about what colleagues are saying or doing, fear of missing important information, desire for social connection with coworkers. External — notifications for messages, mentions, reactions.
- **Action:** Open Slack, read new messages. The app sits in the dock/taskbar; opening it is frictionless.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Tribe** — emoji reactions, thread replies, feeling included in team conversations. **Rewards of the Hunt** — new messages and channels contain unpredictable content.
- **Investment:** Channels joined, integrations configured, message history accumulated, custom emoji created, status patterns established. Heavy investment creates high switching costs.

**Why the case is instructive:** Slack shows the Hook Model operating in B2B — a domain people assume is immune to habit design. The internal trigger (workplace uncertainty and social belonging) is just as powerful as consumer triggers. It also illustrates a double-edged sword: Slack's hook is so effective that it can become a distraction trap — making it a case study for *Indistractable* as well.

## Video Games (general pattern)

**Where Eyal used it:** *Hooked*, Chapter 4 (Variable Reward — Rewards of the Self); multiple talks.

**The situation:** Video games are among the most habit-forming products ever designed, with engagement patterns that predate digital product design by decades.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — desire for mastery, escapism, social connection, boredom. External — game notifications, friend invitations, tournament announcements.
- **Action:** Start playing. Modern games minimize friction to the first action (fast loading, immediate gameplay, low initial difficulty).
- **Variable reward:** All three types. **Self** — leveling up, completing quests, unlocking achievements (mastery and competence). **Tribe** — multiplayer competition and cooperation, leaderboards, team play. **Hunt** — loot drops, random rewards, discovering new content.
- **Investment:** Character progression, items collected, skills developed, social connections built, time invested (sunk cost). Each session makes the next session more valuable because the character/account is more developed.

**Why the case is instructive:** Games are Eyal's canonical example of Rewards of the Self. They also demonstrate infinite variability (procedurally generated content, multiplayer dynamics) vs. finite variability (a game with fixed levels that eventually runs out of content). Games that maintain engagement for years have infinite variability; those that spike and die have finite variability.

## Fitbod (fitness app)

**Where Eyal used it:** nirandfar.com; cited as an example of a Facilitator on the Manipulation Matrix.

**The situation:** Fitbod is a strength training app that generates personalized workout plans based on the user's history, available equipment, and recovery state.

**How Eyal applied the framework:**
- **Trigger:** Internal — desire to maintain a fitness routine, guilt about skipping workouts, desire for progress. External — workout reminders, progress notifications.
- **Action:** Open the app to see today's workout. One tap to start.
- **Variable reward:** **Rewards of the Self** — completing a workout, seeing progress charts, hitting new personal records. The workout itself varies each session based on what the algorithm recommends — built-in variability.
- **Investment:** Workout history, strength progression data, equipment settings, preference data. The more workouts logged, the better the algorithm's recommendations become — classic stored value.

**Why the case is instructive:** Fitbod is Eyal's go-to example of ethical hook design. The builder uses the product (Eyal has said he uses Fitbod himself) and it genuinely improves users' health. Facilitator quadrant, clean. It demonstrates that the Hook Model is not inherently exploitative — it's a design tool that can serve users' genuine interests.
