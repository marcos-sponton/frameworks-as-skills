## Definition

Eyal defines the Hook Model as a four-step process embedded into products to bring users back repeatedly — without relying on costly advertising or aggressive messaging:

> "A hook is an experience designed to connect the user's problem to a solution frequently enough to form a habit." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Introduction

The goal is not a single transaction but a **loop** — a cycle that repeats, deepens, and becomes increasingly automatic. Products that successfully create hooks change user behavior by creating **associations** between internal emotional states and the product's solution.

> "The Hook Model describes an experience designed to connect the user's problem to a company's solution with enough frequency to form a habit." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 1

## The Hook Model — 4 Phases

### 1. Trigger

The trigger is the actuator of behavior — the spark plug in the engine. Triggers come in two types: **external** and **internal**.

**External triggers** are environmental cues that tell the user what to do next. They include:
- **Paid triggers** — ads, SEM, paid social. Effective for acquisition but expensive to sustain.
- **Earned triggers** — press, viral videos, featured app store placement. Hard to sustain.
- **Relationship triggers** — word of mouth, referrals, invitations from friends. Viral loops.
- **Owned triggers** — app icons on the home screen, email newsletters, push notifications. The trigger the company controls.

**Internal triggers** are emotional states and situations that become associated with the product through repeated use. They are the ultimate goal:
- Boredom → scroll Instagram
- Loneliness → open Facebook
- Uncertainty → Google it
- Anxiety about missing something → check email

**Key distinction:** The entire Hook cycle exists to move users from external triggers to internal triggers. External triggers get the user to the product; internal triggers bring them back automatically. A product that still depends on external triggers to drive engagement has not yet formed a habit.

**Author's own words:**
> "When users start to automatically cue their next action, the new habit becomes part of their everyday routine. Over time, users begin to associate the product with the internal trigger, and the external trigger is no longer needed." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 2

**How to identify internal triggers:** Eyal recommends the "5 Whys" technique — starting with a surface behavior and asking "why?" five times to get to the emotional root. Example from *Hooked*:
1. Why would he use email? To send and receive messages.
2. Why would he want to do that? To share and find information quickly.
3. Why would he want to do that? To know what's going on in the lives of coworkers, friends, family.
4. Why would he need to know that? To know if someone needs him.
5. Why would that matter? **Fear of being out of the loop.**

The internal trigger is fear — not "sending email."

### 2. Action

The action is the **simplest behavior done in anticipation of reward**. Eyal builds on BJ Fogg's Behavior Model:

**B = MAT** (Behavior = Motivation + Ability + Trigger)

For the action to occur, three things must be present simultaneously:
1. **Motivation** — sufficient desire to act. Fogg identifies six core motivators organized in three pairs: seek pleasure / avoid pain; seek hope / avoid fear; seek social acceptance / avoid social rejection.
2. **Ability** — the capacity to perform the action. Measured by how simple or complex the action is.
3. **Trigger** — the cue (external or internal) described in Phase 1.

**Key distinction:** Increasing ability (reducing friction) is almost always more effective than increasing motivation. Motivation is expensive and unreliable; simplicity is designable.

Eyal identifies **six factors of simplicity** (from Fogg):
1. **Time** — how long does the action take?
2. **Money** — what is the financial cost?
3. **Physical effort** — how much exertion is required?
4. **Brain cycles** — how much thinking is required?
5. **Social deviance** — does it go against social norms?
6. **Non-routine** — how much does it disrupt existing habits?

The product designer's job: identify the simplest action the user can take in anticipation of reward, and eliminate friction from that action.

**Author's own words:**
> "To increase the likelihood of a behavior occurring, Fogg instructs designers to focus on simplicity as a function of the user's scarcest resource at that moment." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 3

**Cognitive biases that influence action:** Eyal catalogs several biases product designers leverage (drawn from Kahneman, Cialdini, and behavioral economics):
- **Scarcity effect** — limited availability increases perceived value.
- **Framing effect** — context shapes perception of the action.
- **Anchoring** — initial reference points shape subsequent judgments.
- **Endowed progress effect** — people who feel they've made progress toward a goal are more motivated to complete it.

### 3. Variable Reward

The variable reward is the hook's engine — it creates desire by introducing **unpredictability** into the reward. Eyal draws heavily on B.F. Skinner's research on variable-ratio reinforcement schedules and the neuroscience of the nucleus accumbens.

**Key distinction:** It is the **variability** that creates desire, not the reward itself. Predictable rewards become boring. Variable rewards sustain engagement because the brain's dopamine system responds more strongly to the *anticipation* of an uncertain outcome than to the outcome itself.

> "What draws us to act is not the sensation we receive from the reward itself, but the need to alleviate the craving for that reward." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 4

**Three types of variable rewards:**

1. **Rewards of the Tribe** — social rewards driven by our connectedness with other people. Validation, cooperation, competition, recognition.
   - Examples: Facebook likes, Stack Overflow reputation points, League of Legends team play.
   - Root: humans are social animals; uncertainty about what others think/do/say is inherently compelling.

2. **Rewards of the Hunt** — the search for material resources and information. The thrill of the chase.
   - Examples: Twitter/X feed (information hunt), slot machines (material hunt), Pinterest (visual hunt), Google search results.
   - Root: the same dopamine circuitry that drove our ancestors to forage for food now fires when we scroll for the next interesting piece of content.

3. **Rewards of the Self** — intrinsic rewards of mastery, competence, and completion. The drive to conquer obstacles and finish what we started.
   - Examples: video game leveling up, completing a to-do list, Codecademy skill badges, email inbox zero.
   - Root: humans seek consistency between what they believe they can do and what they actually accomplish.

**Critical nuance — autonomy:** Eyal emphasizes that variable rewards must preserve the user's sense of autonomy. Reactance theory (Brehm, 1966) shows that when people feel their freedom is being taken away, they rebel. Variable rewards that feel manipulative or coercive backfire.

> "Experiences with finite variability become increasingly predictable with use and lose their appeal over time. Experiences that maintain user interest by sustaining variability with use exhibit infinite variability." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 4

### 4. Investment

The investment phase is where users put something **into** the product — data, content, followers, reputation, skill, or effort — that (a) improves the product for next use and (b) loads the next trigger. Investment does NOT deliver immediate gratification — it is about **storing value** for the future.

**Key distinction:** The investment phase is what closes the loop. Without it, the hook is open — a one-time experience. With it, each cycle through the hook makes the next cycle more likely.

**Types of investment:**
- **Content** — photos uploaded, messages sent, playlists created.
- **Data** — preferences, usage history, personalization signals.
- **Followers / social capital** — audience built, reputation earned.
- **Reputation** — ratings, reviews, karma scores.
- **Skill** — learning the product's interface, developing proficiency.

**How investment loads the next trigger:**
- A user who follows accounts on Twitter loads their own feed (the next trigger).
- A user who uploads photos to Instagram invites comments and likes (external triggers) and develops identity attachment (internal triggers).
- A user who rates songs on Spotify improves recommendations (making the next session more rewarding).

**Author's own words:**
> "The more users invest in a product through tiny bits of work, the more valuable the product becomes in their lives and the less they question its use." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 5

**Cognitive principles at work:**
- **IKEA effect** — people value things more when they've invested labor in them (Norton, Mochon, Ariely, 2012).
- **Escalation of commitment** — small investments make larger ones more likely.
- **Cognitive dissonance** — we rationalize past investments by continuing to invest (Festinger).
- **Stored value** — unlike physical goods, digital products can appreciate with use (more data = better recommendations = more value).

## The Habit Zone

Not every product needs to be habit-forming. Eyal maps products on two axes:

- **Frequency** — how often does the user engage? (daily, weekly, monthly, annually)
- **Perceived utility** — how useful/pleasurable is the product relative to alternatives?

Products in the **Habit Zone** have both high frequency AND sufficient perceived utility. Products below the threshold on either axis don't form habits — and trying to force a hook onto an infrequently-used product is a misapplication.

> "A behavior that occurs with enough frequency and perceived utility enters the Habit Zone, helping to make it a default behavior." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 1

**Key distinction:** The Habit Zone is the precondition check. Before applying the Hook Model, ask: does my product have a shot at being used frequently enough to form a habit? If not (e.g., a product used once a year like tax software), the Hook Model is the wrong tool.

## The Manipulation Matrix

Eyal addresses the ethics of behavior design head-on with the Manipulation Matrix — a 2x2 grid based on two questions:

1. **"Would I use this product myself?"** (Yes / No)
2. **"Does this product materially improve the user's life?"** (Yes / No)

The four quadrants:

| | Materially improves life | Does NOT improve life |
|---|---|---|
| **Would use it myself** | **Facilitator** | **Entertainer** |
| **Would NOT use it myself** | **Peddler** | **Dealer** |

- **Facilitator** (Yes / Yes) — the ethical ideal. The maker uses the product and believes it genuinely helps. Examples: fitness apps used by the founder, educational tools built by teachers.
- **Entertainer** (Yes / No) — creates fun or pleasure but doesn't materially improve life. Not unethical, but the maker should be honest about what they're building.
- **Peddler** (No / Yes) — believes the product helps but wouldn't use it personally. Lacks empathy and runs the risk of building something that misses real needs.
- **Dealer** (No / No) — exploitative. Knowingly building something the maker wouldn't use that doesn't help people. Eyal's explicit warning: this is where manipulation crosses into exploitation.

> "I believe that the trinity of access, data, and speed presents an unprecedented opportunity to create products that improve lives through healthy habits. If the innovator has a clear conscience that the product materially improves people's lives — first, among users like the innovator herself — then the designer has nothing to hide." — Nir Eyal, *Hooked*, Chapter 8

**Key distinction:** Eyal positions the Manipulation Matrix as a **personal moral test**, not a regulatory framework. He doesn't argue for external restrictions on behavior design — he argues for internal accountability from builders.

## The Indistractable Model

Five years after *Hooked*, Eyal published *Indistractable* (2019) — essentially the user's defense manual against the hooks he taught companies to build. The two books are two sides of the same coin.

**Core concept — Traction vs. Distraction:**

All human behavior falls on a single axis:
- **Traction** — actions that pull you **toward** what you want (from Latin *trahere*, to pull).
- **Distraction** — actions that pull you **away** from what you want (dis + trahere).

The key insight: **the opposite of distraction is not focus — it is traction.** Understanding why we get distracted requires understanding what we're being distracted *from*.

**The Indistractable Model — 4 strategies:**

1. **Master internal triggers.** Most distraction starts from within — boredom, loneliness, insecurity, fatigue, uncertainty. The "10-minute rule": when the urge to distract hits, tell yourself "not yet" and wait 10 minutes. The urge usually passes.

2. **Make time for traction.** Turn your values into time. Use **timeboxing** — schedule every minute of your day based on what you value, not what's urgent. A calendar, not a to-do list.

3. **Hack back external triggers.** Not all external triggers are bad. Keep the ones that serve you (a calendar reminder to exercise); eliminate the ones that don't (social media notifications). Test each trigger: "Is this trigger serving ME, or am I serving IT?"

4. **Prevent distraction with pacts.** Three types:
   - **Effort pacts** — add friction to unwanted behavior (e.g., a browser blocker for social media during work hours).
   - **Price pacts** — attach a financial cost to distraction (e.g., putting $50 at stake if you check your phone during dinner).
   - **Identity pacts** — adopt a self-image that makes the unwanted behavior incongruent ("I am indistractable").

**Author's own words:**
> "The antidote to impulsiveness is forethought. Planning ahead is the single most important thing you can do to be indistractable." — Nir Eyal, *Indistractable*, Part 4

## Integration — Hooked + Indistractable

Eyal explicitly positions these as complementary:

- **Hooked** teaches builders how to design products that form habits (supply-side).
- **Indistractable** teaches individuals how to resist unwanted hooks and design their own behavior (demand-side).

Together they form a complete behavioral design perspective: understanding how hooks work gives you the knowledge to build them ethically AND to defend against them personally. A practitioner who reads only *Hooked* risks becoming a "Dealer" on the Manipulation Matrix; one who reads only *Indistractable* misses the product design dimension.

## Sequence of application

**For product builders (Hooked):**
1. **Identify the internal trigger.** What emotional pain does the user experience? Use the 5 Whys to get to the root.
2. **Define the action.** What is the simplest behavior the user does in anticipation of relief? Reduce friction (time, money, effort, brain cycles, social deviance, non-routine).
3. **Design the variable reward.** Which type(s) — Tribe, Hunt, Self — match the internal trigger? Ensure variability and preserve autonomy.
4. **Create the investment.** What can the user put in that improves the next cycle? Does it load the next trigger?
5. **Check the Manipulation Matrix.** Would you use this? Does it genuinely help? If the answer to both is not "yes," reconsider.

**For individuals (Indistractable):**
1. **Audit internal triggers.** What emotions are driving your distraction?
2. **Timebox your values.** Schedule traction, not tasks.
3. **Hack back external triggers.** Audit every notification, every default, every ambient cue.
4. **Make pacts.** Effort, price, or identity — add structure to protect your intentions.

## What this method is NOT

- **It is NOT a dark patterns manual.** Eyal is emphatic about the Manipulation Matrix. Hooks are ethical when they materially improve lives; they are exploitative when they don't. The method includes the ethics check as a load-bearing component, not a disclaimer.
- **It is NOT a growth-hacking checklist.** The Hook Model is a diagnostic and design framework, not a set of "engagement tricks." Slapping push notifications on a bad product is not building a hook.
- **It is NOT only about consumer apps.** Eyal uses consumer examples because they're vivid, but the model applies to any product where repeat engagement matters — B2B tools, educational platforms, health apps, productivity software.
- **It is NOT a substitute for product-market fit.** A hook on a product nobody wants is still a product nobody wants. The hook amplifies value; it doesn't create it.
- **It is NOT about addiction.** Eyal distinguishes between habits and addictions explicitly. Habits are behaviors performed with little or no conscious thought; addictions are persistent, compulsive dependencies that harm the user. The hook model is about the former.
