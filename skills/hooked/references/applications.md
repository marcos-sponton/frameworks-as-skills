# Hooked — Applications

> When to use the Hook Model, when NOT to, and what to reach for instead.

## Situations where the Hook Model fits well

- **Consumer apps designed for daily or near-daily use** — social media, messaging, fitness, news, entertainment, productivity. The Hook Model was designed for this: products that need repeat engagement to survive. Because the model requires high frequency to enter the Habit Zone.

- **Product engagement and retention work** — when a product has users but they're not coming back. The Hook Model is a diagnostic tool: walk through trigger → action → reward → investment and find where the loop breaks. Because each phase has specific failure modes that are testable.

- **Onboarding design** — getting new users to their first "aha moment" faster. The first hook cycle (first trigger → first action → first reward → first investment) is the onboarding. Because the habit path analysis (what habitual users did differently in their first sessions) directly informs onboarding optimization.

- **Health and wellness apps** — fitness tracking, medication reminders, mental health tools, habit trackers. Eyal explicitly endorses this as the Facilitator quadrant of the Manipulation Matrix. Because forming healthy habits is a use case where hooks and user value align perfectly.

- **Educational platforms** — language learning (Duolingo), coding practice (Codecademy), reading apps. Variable reward (Rewards of the Self — mastery, completion) and investment (progress, skill level) create durable engagement. Because learning is inherently a repeat-engagement domain.

- **Content platforms** — social feeds, news aggregators, content discovery apps. Rewards of the Hunt (the next interesting piece of content) provide natural variable reward. Because content consumption is a high-frequency behavior with built-in variability.

- **Diagnosing why engagement is dropping** — existing products losing DAU/MAU. Walk the hook: are triggers still firing? Is the action still simple? Is the reward still variable? Is investment loading the next trigger? Because the model is as valuable for diagnosis as for design.

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Low-frequency products** — tax software, insurance purchasing, enterprise procurement. If the product is used once a year or once a quarter, it cannot enter the Habit Zone. Trying to force a hook is wasted effort and can annoy users. **Alternative:** focus on service quality, trust, switching costs, and brand reputation. Reach for customer experience frameworks instead.

- **B2B enterprise products with infrequent use** — quarterly reporting tools, annual audit software, strategic planning platforms used once per planning cycle. The usage frequency is too low for habit formation. **Alternative:** focus on workflow integration, organizational switching costs, and outcome quality. The product's value is in its output, not in engagement frequency.

- **High-stakes, low-frequency decisions** — buying a house, choosing a university, major medical decisions. Users don't want to form habits around these; they want the best single outcome. **Alternative:** reach for decision-support frameworks. These situations need depth, not frequency.

- **Products where "engagement" is the wrong metric** — emergency services, safety equipment, products that succeed when they're NOT used (insurance, security systems). Optimizing for engagement in these domains is a category error.

- **When the user needs product-market fit, not engagement** — if nobody wants the product, no amount of hook design will save it. The Hook Model amplifies existing value; it doesn't create it. **Alternative:** validate demand first (Lean Startup, Continuous Discovery Habits, Customer Development).

- **When the ethical test fails** — if the builder wouldn't use the product AND it doesn't improve the user's life, applying the Hook Model makes a bad product stickier. This is the Dealer quadrant. **Alternative:** redesign the product's value proposition before worrying about engagement.

## Adjacent frameworks — when to reach for a different one

| If the user's situation is... | Reach for... | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Building personal habits (not product habits) | **Atomic Habits** (James Clear) | Clear's framework is about individual behavior change — cue, craving, response, reward. Hooked is about designing products that create habits in *users*. |
| Doing weekly product discovery cadence | **Continuous Discovery Habits** (Teresa Torres) | Torres covers the ongoing cadence of customer interviews, opportunity mapping, and assumption testing. Hooked is about engagement design, not discovery process. |
| Designing behavior change at the micro level | **BJ Fogg's Behavior Model** (Fogg) | Fogg's B=MAT model is more granular — it's the foundation Eyal builds on for the Action phase. Use Fogg when you need to optimize a single behavior, not a full engagement loop. |
| Fighting for attention against incumbents | **Play Bigger** (Ramadan et al.) | Category design creates the space; the Hook Model fills it with engagement. Use Play Bigger when the problem is "nobody knows this category exists." |
| Deciding whether to pivot the product | **Lean Startup** (Eric Ries) | Ries's pivot-or-persevere framework handles the strategic decision. Hooked handles the engagement design *after* you know what you're building. |
| Pricing or monetization strategy | **Obviously Awesome** (April Dunford) or **Four Fits** | Hooked doesn't address pricing. Reach for positioning and business model frameworks when the question is "how do we charge?" |
| Enterprise product stickiness through workflow integration | **Working Backwards** (Amazon) or **Escaping the Build Trap** (Perri) | Enterprise stickiness comes from workflow integration and organizational switching costs, not from habit loops. Different mechanism, different framework. |
| Understanding why users leave (churn analysis) | **Demand-Side Sales** (Moesta) | Moesta's forces model (push, pull, anxiety, habit) explains the switching decision. Hooked explains why they stay; Moesta explains why they leave. |

## How this framework composes with others

Some frameworks pair naturally. Others fight. Notes on both.

- **Pairs well with: BJ Fogg's Behavior Model** — Fogg is the micro layer (single behavior design); Hooked is the macro layer (engagement loop design). Eyal explicitly builds the Action phase on Fogg's B=MAT model. Use Fogg for precision on any individual action; use Hooked for the full cycle.

- **Pairs well with: Continuous Discovery Habits (Torres)** — Torres's cadence of customer interviews and opportunity mapping generates the insights (internal triggers, user needs) that inform hook design. Discovery first → Hook design second.

- **Pairs well with: Lean Startup (Ries)** — Lean Startup validates what to build; Hooked designs how to keep users engaged with what you built. Build-Measure-Learn can include the Habit Test (from Hooked Chapter 8) as one of its measures.

- **Pairs well with: Atomic Habits (Clear) for the builder personally** — use Hooked for the product, Atomic Habits for your own habits. Eyal even does this implicitly: *Indistractable* is his personal habit management system while *Hooked* is his product design system.

- **Tension with: Cal Newport's Digital Minimalism** — Newport argues for radical reduction of technology use; Eyal argues for intentional use. This is a philosophical disagreement about the right response to attention-exploiting design. A practitioner needs to pick a lane: either the product should be used less (Newport) or the user should manage their relationship with it (Eyal).

- **Tension with: "Tech addiction" framing (Haidt et al.)** — if you adopt the view that habit-forming products are fundamentally harmful, the Hook Model becomes the wrong tool entirely. Eyal rejects this framing but acknowledges the legitimate concern. A practitioner needs to decide whether they see behavioral design as a tool (Eyal) or a hazard (Haidt/Newport).

- **Composes carefully with: Gamification** — gamification elements (points, badges, leaderboards) can serve as variable reward components, but they are NOT the Hook Model. Bolting gamification onto a product without the full hook (trigger → action → variable reward → investment) is a common misapplication. If using gamification, embed it within the hook framework — don't treat it as a substitute.
