# Play Bigger — Method

> The canonical description of the Category Design method in the authors' own terms. Fidelity is the point — this framework is opinionated, and softening any of the components ("category positioning" instead of *Category Design*, "value prop" instead of *POV*, "launch" instead of *Lightning Strike*) collapses the method into generic marketing. When the assistant uses this skill, the vocabulary IS the method.

## The founding claim

> *"Winning today isn't about beating the competition at the old game. It's about inventing a whole new game — defining a new market category, developing it, and dominating it over time. You can't build a legendary company without building a legendary category."*
> — *Play Bigger* (2016), thesis.

**Category Design is the management discipline of creating and monetizing new markets.** The user's job — if the framework applies — is to become the **Category King** (or Queen), the company that dominates a category and captures ~76% of the category's market cap. Everyone else fights over the remaining ~24%.

### The 76% economics — anchor stat

From the Play Bigger [Time-to-Market-Cap (TTMC) report](https://www.playbigger.com/time-to-market-cap-report), the primary-research paper behind the 2016 book:

- **~76% of a category's market cap goes to one company** — the Category King.
- Extreme-end examples: Apple ~93% of smartphone profits. Uber ~25× Lyft in valuation.
- Post-book Category Pirates refinement: Category Kings/Queens with a **Data Flywheel** command ~$4.82 of market cap per $1 of revenue growth — ~5× the multiple of the typical company (Eddie Yoon research).

If the user's ambition is to be a top-2 or top-3 vendor inside a mature category, they are optimizing for the ~24% share pool. Category Design says: don't. Design a new category where you're the only one.

## The Magic Triangle — three legs in synchronicity

Category Design is **not a marketing exercise.** It's three disciplines running in parallel:

1. **Product Design** — build something that solves the category's problem. **Product/category fit.**
2. **Company Design** — business model, org, culture, hiring, all aligned to that category. **Company/category fit.**
3. **Category Design** — deliberately design and condition the market that gives the product its meaning.

Lochhead's phrasing: *"Magic Triangle — equal focus on designing company, product, and category simultaneously."*

**Failure modes when the triangle is broken:**
- Product-only (great product, no category work) → obscurity; the market can't place you.
- Category-talk-only (loud POV, weak product) → the ecosystem forms and then a real competitor takes it.
- Company-only (great org, wrong category) → stuck in the Existing Market Trap.

The flywheel effect kicks in only when all three reinforce.

## The Category Lifecycle — Define / Develop / Dominate

Three phases of a category's evolution. The King is the company that leads all three:

| Phase | What happens | Winner |
|---|---|---|
| **Define** | Thought leader emerges. The problem gets named. Multiple companies swarm around the emerging space. | The Category Designer who sets the language. |
| **Develop** | The number of players collapses. A king emerges. Ecosystem forms — analysts, partners, developers. | Whoever framed the problem best. |
| **Dominate** | Category King captures ~76% of category market cap. Data Flywheel spins. Ecosystem locks in. | The King (Queen). Nobody else. |

*Play Bigger*'s corollary: **"Whoever frames the problem best has the best chance of winning the category."** And: **"The first inventor is an innovator to be thanked. The first to define and develop a category is a category king to be followed."**

First to market ≠ first to King. First to *frame the problem* → first to King.

## The Category Design process — the 7 steps

The Play Bigger process, as productized by Category Design Agency (per the [current site](https://www.categorydesignadvisors.com/play-bigger/)):

### 1. Identify the Problem

**Whoever frames the problem best owns the category.** Start from a problem no one has framed yet — not from your product. The **founding insight** is either:

- **Market insight** — a problem the world doesn't yet realize it has (or hasn't been able to name). Play Bigger: *"when you have a good one, it seems a little crazy."*
- **Technology insight** — a capability looking for the right problem to name.

**Anti-move:** starting from your product's feature list and reverse-engineering a problem. That gets you the Better Trap.

### 2. Solution & Category Name

The category needs a **name**. 2–3 words. Powerful. Clear. If it takes a paragraph to explain, the category isn't sharp enough yet.

Naming is not decoration — it's the mechanism by which the market's mind reorganizes. Category Pirates on this (post-book): **Languaging** is the deliberate invention of new language to get ideas to stick. Same product, different languaging = different market position.

Examples of category names that did the work:
- *Home sharing* (Airbnb — reframed hotels as legacy)
- *On-demand transportation* (Uber — not "taxi 2.0")
- *End of software* (Salesforce — cloud CRM)
- *Athleisure* (Lululemon)
- *Experience Management / XM* (Qualtrics)
- *Canned water* (Liquid Death — a Category Pirates 2020s favorite)

**Test:** if the incumbent's category name still fits you, you haven't named a new category. You're inside their frame.

### 3. Category Blueprint & Ecosystem

Two artifacts — package the category so it can be shown:

- **Category Blueprint** — the vision of how the product/service works in this new category. Thought leadership positioning.
- **Category Ecosystem** — map every third party the category needs: developers, consultants, partners, analysts, adjacent products, media, competitors, and other players. **A category is not a category without an ecosystem.** If you're the only one talking about it, it's a monologue.

Also produced at this stage (the 5 packaging docs from the 2016 book):

1. Category Landscape
2. Category Ecosystem
3. **FROM-to Journey** (aka **Frotos**) — how the category works *from* today's state *to* the future state.
4. Category Name and Description
5. The Case for the New Category

### 4. Point of View (POV)

**The POV is the story that frames the world differently.** It's not a tagline, not a positioning statement, not a manifesto. It's the CEO-owned narrative that installs the category in the market's mind.

Lochhead's definition:
> "A POV is a story that articulates a problem/opportunity for customers and a different approach to solving the problem or capitalizing on the opportunity."

Structural components of a POV (Play Bigger):

- **Frames the problem** — not the product.
- **Names the ramifications** of not acting.
- **Presents a vision of the future** — the "to" side of Frotos.
- **Explains the business outcomes** of embracing the vision.
- Told with **simple language, short sentences, provocative tone, emotional content.**
- Has **beginning, middle, end** — like a movie trailer.
- **CEO-owned. "Make it religion."**

A good POV signals:
- **Mission-driven, not mercenary.** Missionary companies build categories; mercenaries can't.
- **Separates loved brands from tolerated ones.**
- **Magnetizes the ecosystem** — missionaries, analysts, partners, employees join because they believe.

**Test:** if your POV would fit unchanged on your closest competitor's homepage, it's not a POV — it's category-generic marketing copy.

### 5. Internal Mobilization

Codify the category in the **four seminal artifacts** (Play Bigger):

1. **Category Blueprint** — the category vision.
2. **Product Taxonomy** — repackage / rename / reprice product components to align with the category blueprint. (Same product, different taxonomy signals to the market that the category is real.)
3. **Use Cases** — how customers will use the product if the category develops as designed.
4. **Category Ecosystem** — the map of everyone orbiting the category.

Every function inside the company must be able to tell the same category story: sales, marketing, product, hiring, investor relations, customer success. The POV reinforces across:

- **Hiring** — attract mission-aligned employees; missionaries only.
- **Investor Relations** — primary tool for capital raising ("we are the [Category] company").
- **Product Development** — guides what to build and, critically, what NOT to build.
- **Brand** — articulates identity and why the company matters.
- **Employee Alignment** — organizational precision.

### 6. Lightning Strike Execution

**A Lightning Strike is not a launch and not a marketing campaign.** It's a concentrated *company event* that plants the category in the market's mind, timed to hijack attention.

Lochhead's definition:
> "A Lightning Strike is an event meant to explode onto the market, grab the attention of customers, investors, analysts, and media."

Play Bigger execution rules:

- **Content must be big, bold, different enough to cut through.** If it's not obviously different from anything else in the market, it's not a strike — it's a press release.
- **Evangelize the problem first, product second.** The POV frames the problem; the Lightning Strike detonates the POV.
- **Assign specific roles**: CEO, Strike Master Controller, Strike Leader, Chief Hijacker, Event Planner.
- **Plan 3–6 months out.** Identify what you're going to *hijack* — a conference, an analyst report, a competitor's user event, a market moment.
- **Codify all category-design work** into the strike materials.
- **Execute → hijack → adapt → sell → evaluate → accelerate the signals that landed.**
- **CEO must not waver.** *"If the CEO wavers or hedges or doesn't commit resources, the strike will fail."*

**Canonical strike:** Salesforce hiring actors to stage a mock "End of Software" protest outside Siebel's user conference. Siebel started defending itself. Press ran with the story. Salesforce was legitimized as the category challenger the moment the incumbent acknowledged them.

**Anti-move:** a "launch event" with your logo, product screenshots, and customer quotes. That's a press release with catering. A Lightning Strike installs a new category in the market's mind; it doesn't announce a product.

### 7. Strike Ops Flywheel

Sustained category dominance = compounding execution. Category Design Agency's current framing of the seventh step. Play Bigger's language: **spin the flywheel.**

Flywheel fuel:
- **Strong POV** — guiding force.
- **Constant mobilizations and Lightning Strikes** — anchor the category over time.
- **Ecosystem building** — partners, analysts, developers, missionaries.
- **Capital** — bet-the-company resource level.
- **Data and analytics** — the **Data Flywheel** (post-book, see `post-book.md`).
- **Talent attraction** — missionaries only.

Flywheel steps:
1. CEO drives all flywheel elements (Chief Category Officer).
2. Recommit to Category Design or expand the category's potential.
3. Decide: re-up on new categories, or harvest current category?
4. Consider path to *continuous category creation* (a Corporate Playbook move — for companies that already have one King category).

## POV — deeper

Full POV construction (Play Bigger):

- **Discovery** — assign an external strategist with the CEO's trust. Answer strategic + cultural questions about differentiation, product creation, outcomes, culture.
- **Definition** — clearly define the *problem* before describing the *solution*.
- **Craft** — simple language, short sentences, provocative tone, emotional content, company personality (like a movie trailer).
- **Evangelization** — CEO and leadership actively evangelize; "make it religion." External speaking, all-hands, investor updates, hiring pitch — all echo the same POV.

The POV is the **operating instrument** for:
- Investor pitch (primary capital-raising tool).
- Employee alignment.
- Product roadmap discipline (guides what to build AND what NOT to build).
- Hiring (missionaries self-select in).
- Sales narrative (feeds Raskin-style narrative artifacts — see `applications.md`).
- Analyst / media positioning.

## Lightning Strike — deeper

A **Lightning Strike is a company-level event, not a marketing tactic.** Requirements:

1. **Content big, bold, different** enough to cut through market noise.
2. **Evangelize the problem first, product second.**
3. **Concentrated resources** — Play Bigger describes it as CEO committing all political capital.

Execution phases:

- **Plan 3–6 months out** — venue, moment to hijack, deliverables.
- **Roles** — CEO owns; Strike Master Controller coordinates; Strike Leader executes; Chief Hijacker identifies the moment; Event Planner runs logistics.
- **Codify** — all category-design work turns into strike materials.
- **Execute + hijack + adapt + sell** — strike day is a war day.
- **Evaluate + accelerate** — the signals that land get resourced up; the ones that don't get retired.

**CEO wavering = strike fails.** Repeated in the book as a first-order rule. Nothing about a Lightning Strike is delegatable at the strategic level.

## The four mobilization artifacts (2016 book)

Required deliverables for executing category design at scale:

1. **Category Blueprint** — vision of how product/service works; thought leadership positioning.
2. **Product Taxonomy** — repackage product components aligned with blueprint; rename / reprice innovations to connect with category.
3. **Use Cases** — how customers will use the product if the category develops as designed.
4. **Category Ecosystem** — map third-party developers, consultants, partners, analysts, competitors, and other players.

If any of these is missing, the category doesn't hold up.

## What Category Design is NOT (repeat from SKILL, because the confusion is central)

- **NOT positioning.** Positioning (Dunford) happens inside a category — it chooses the frame of reference among existing categories. Category Design creates the category itself. See **[[obviously-awesome]]**.
- **NOT branding.** Brand is a name, symbol, identity system. It's downstream of category. A great brand for a company stuck in a legacy category is decoration on a losing position.
- **NOT messaging.** Messaging is the tactical expression of the POV. It's downstream.
- **NOT storytelling.** Sales narrative (Raskin) tells the category change into a buyer story. It's the *artifact*, not the *strategy*. See **[[strategic-narrative]]**.

## Continuous Category Design — the Corporate Playbook

For established companies creating *multiple* categories over time (Play Bigger's later chapters):

1. CEO commitment.
2. Condition investors on category-creation as strategy.
3. Leverage time advantage (6–10-year window before incumbents can respond).
4. Listen for "different, not just better" (avoid Innovator's Dilemma trap of incremental improvement).
5. Execute the full Category Design process.
6. Avoid half-hearted efforts that protect old-category profits (which cannibalize the new category).
7. Manage portfolio of categories at different lifecycle stages.

Play Bigger caveat: *"The odds of a start-up becoming a billion-dollar Category King are better than the odds of a new product from a big old corporation creating a new category."* Continuous category creation is available but expensive; most incumbents fail at it.

## Category Design for Personal Brands

The framework applies to individuals (Category Pirates' [[snow-leopard]] extension + Lochhead's *Niche Down*):

1. **Articulate the problem** — reputation for solving a specific problem beats generic "better."
2. **Design your three legs** — skills, capabilities, and category opportunity together.
3. **Develop your POV** — define what you are, what makes you different, why people care.
4. **Condition the market** — presentations, reports, social, media education.
5. **Build your ecosystem** — purposeful community of supports, followers, partners.
6. **Execute Lightning Strikes** — deadlines trigger action.
7. **Expand your category** — increase potential as you grow.

Category of one = **Snow Leopard**.
