# Play Bigger — Heuristics, do's, don'ts, gotchas

> Practical operating devices — the "if you see this, do this" content. Anti-patterns, common misapplications, and diagnostic questions, all with attribution. The authors have publicly named most of these; where they have, quote them. Where the assistant is extrapolating from the method, mark it as such.

## 1. The core do's

- **Frame the problem before you name the product.** *"Whoever frames the problem best has the best chance of winning the category."* — *Play Bigger* (2016). The problem-framing move is upstream of every other move.
- **Sell different, not better.** If the user's pitch can be steel-manned as "same as X, but 20% better/faster/cheaper", they're competing, not designing. *"People remember different in a way they don't remember better."* — Lochhead.
- **Name the category in 2–3 words.** If it takes a paragraph, the category isn't sharp enough. Naming does the work — see **Languaging** in `post-book.md`.
- **CEO-owned POV.** Not delegated. The CEO is the *Chief Category Officer*. *"If the CEO wavers or hedges or doesn't commit resources, the strike will fail."* — *Play Bigger* (2016).
- **Missionary founders.** 6–10-year belief horizons. Mercenaries won't survive the category-design cycle — they quit when the first analyst report looks bad.
- **Ecosystem before product feature race.** A category has partners, analysts, developers, missionaries. If you're the only one talking about the category, it's a monologue, not a category.
- **Lightning Strike, not launch.** Concentrated moments beat continuous churn. A launch announces a product; a Lightning Strike installs a category.
- **Languaging is the mechanism, not decoration.** Deliberately invent the words. If your language matches the incumbent's, so does your market position.
- **Design the Data Flywheel from day one.** (Post-book — Category Pirates.) The loop that will make the category defensible in year 5 has to be part of the year-1 architecture.
- **Package the category in artifacts** — Category Blueprint, Product Taxonomy, Use Cases, Category Ecosystem. Without artifacts, the category doesn't hold up under investor / analyst / hire pressure.

## 2. Anti-patterns / don'ts / gotchas

### 2.1 The Better Trap

Every version of "we're X for Y" that positions inside an incumbent's category is a Better Trap. The user is competing for the ~24% share pool, not designing for the ~76% King share. Category Pirates: **"Escape the 'Better' Trap"** is the subtitle of *A Marketer's Guide to Category Design*.

**Signals the user is in the Better Trap:**
- Their homepage headline could be signed by 3 competitors and nobody would notice.
- Their pitch deck's first substantive slide is a competitor comparison matrix.
- Sales reps say "we're basically like [Incumbent], but [X, Y, Z]."
- The category name they use in their own materials is the incumbent's category name.

### 2.2 The Existing Market Trap (2025, Ramadan + Lochhead)

The prequel-level frame from *The Existing Market Trap*: most companies don't fail because they build bad products — they fail because they get compared to something old and lose that comparison. Book premise:

> *"Markets are people. Categories are beliefs. A market is just a group of people who share a problem. A category is what they believe can solve it."*

The trap has **13 Deadly Sins** (the primer's naming — full list in the book). The escape is Category Design — build belief in a new market, not incremental improvement of an old one.

**Diagnostic:** *"Are we being compared to something old, and losing that comparison?"* If yes, the whole conversation reframes around escaping the trap.

### 2.3 The Bolt-on AI Trap (2025)

Post-2023 flavor of the Better Trap and the Existing Market Trap combined. From Lochhead (Follow Your Different ep 415, 2025):

> *"If your strategy involves simply bolting on an AI assistant or copilot, you're making a pussy move and you're fucked."*

**The languaging move that traps you:** calling your innovation an "assistant" or "copilot" or "for [Incumbent]" positions it as secondary to an existing category. You just signaled to the market that the existing category is real and you're an accessory. Declaring it its own category magnetizes the future instead.

### 2.4 "First mover advantage" as strategy

Wrong bar. *Play Bigger*: *"The first inventor is an innovator to be thanked. The first to define and develop a category is a category king to be followed."* First to market ≠ first to King. First to *frame the problem* = first to King.

### 2.5 Confusing Category Design with positioning

The single most common misapplication. **Positioning (Dunford) is inside a category; Category Design creates the category.** See **[[obviously-awesome]]**.

If the user has:
- An obvious, market-accepted category shape, and just needs to sharpen their competitive positioning inside it → **Dunford's method**.
- A category the market doesn't have (or a category they refuse to be inside) → **Play Bigger**.
- Both problems simultaneously → sequence Play Bigger to design the category, then Dunford to position inside it once it's real.

Dunford's own pushback: ~90% of recent successful public tech companies won *inside* existing categories, not by creating new ones. Category Design is high-variance and expensive. **Not for everyone.**

### 2.6 Confusing Category Design with branding, messaging, storytelling

- **Branding** is downstream of category. A beautiful brand for a legacy-category company doesn't rescue the trap.
- **Messaging** is the tactical expression of the POV. Downstream.
- **Storytelling / sales narrative** is the artifact form (Raskin) that renders the category change into a buyer story. Renders it — doesn't create it. See **[[strategic-narrative]]**.

Doing beautiful storytelling for a product that hasn't designed its category is decoration on a losing position.

### 2.7 Delegating POV to marketing

Kills the strike. Play Bigger's rule is repeated in every venue Ramadan and Lochhead speak: the CEO owns the POV. If the user says "I'll get marketing to draft the POV," restate the rule. Marketing can help *express* the POV; only the CEO can *own* it. This is one of the 13 Deadly Sins in *The Existing Market Trap*.

### 2.8 Weak POV that could be signed by any competitor

If the user's POV would fit unchanged on their competitor's homepage, it's not a POV — it's category-generic marketing copy. A real POV takes a **disputable** position on the problem. Something a reasonable competitor could plausibly disagree with. If there's nothing to disagree with, there's nothing to believe in.

### 2.9 Multiple categories at once, from a startup

The 6–10-year belief cost is *per category*. Startups almost never survive multi-category bets. Continuous category creation is a Corporate Playbook move — for companies that already have one King category and a Data Flywheel spinning. If a startup founder says "we're creating two new categories at once," push back hard.

### 2.10 Lightning Strike without ecosystem

A one-company event is a press release. A **real Lightning Strike hijacks a conference / analyst / customer / media moment** and depends on ecosystem players amplifying it. If the ecosystem isn't primed, the strike lands with a thud. Ecosystem work runs 3–6 months before the strike, not after.

### 2.11 Category creation for the sake of it

Category Design demands bold thinking, risk tolerance, focused leadership attention, and long-term commitment. It's the wrong tool for:
- Defending an existing profitable business inside a mature category.
- Small tactical launches inside a category the user has already accepted.
- Businesses that need to be profitable inside 12–18 months.
- Founders who describe themselves as "practical, incremental, execution-focused" — they'll bail at the first missionary-signal (the analyst reports the "new category" isn't a category yet).

If the user isn't structurally set up to run a 6–10-year missionary bet, Category Design will chew them up. Say so.

### 2.12 "We already have a great product, we'll figure out the category later"

Magic Triangle broken. Great product + no category work = obscurity. The market can't place the user; buyers default to a category they know (the incumbent's) and the user becomes the "better" version of that category → Better Trap → losing.

Category work is not "phase 2." It's Day 1.

### 2.13 Naming the category after the product

If the category name and the product name are the same word, the user is describing their product, not naming a category. The category has to be big enough that other companies can plausibly enter it and compete for the ~24% pool. If the category is just "your product," you don't have a category.

### 2.14 Confusing "we invented this thing" with "we designed this category"

Inventing a thing → nice. Designing the category around the thing → wins. Play Bigger's line about first inventors is exactly this. Xerox invented the GUI; Apple designed the category. The lesson is not "inventing is bad" — it's "inventing without category design is how the invention gets stolen."

## 3. Blue Ocean pushback — No Ocean

Category Pirates explicitly reject *Blue Ocean Strategy*. From the [No Ocean Strategy essay](https://www.categorypirates.news/p/no-ocean-strategy-why-sailing-for-e3f):

- Blue Ocean assumes there's an ocean to find. **No Ocean says: reject the premise — there's no pre-existing space; build the space itself.**
- *"Pirates reject the premise! They don't just blindly accept the way it is."* — Category Pirates.
- Blue Ocean is value-innovation inside a redefined industry; Category Design is **belief-building** to install a new category in the market's mind. Different assumption about what a "market" is.

If the user brings Blue Ocean framing to a category-design problem, name the pushback and reframe.

## 4. Missionary vs Mercenary — the founder test

Adopted from John Doerr. The authors cite it repeatedly.

| Missionary | Mercenary |
|---|---|
| Driven by passion / belief | Driven by paranoia / opportunism |
| Strategic thinking | Opportunistic thinking |
| Marathon (6–10 years for a category) | Sprint (18 months for a "win") |
| Focus on customers and value | Focus on competitors and financials |
| Own a disputable POV | Don't have a POV |
| Attract other missionaries (talent flywheel) | Attract fee-chasers |
| Survive analyst skepticism, media confusion, market silence | Bail when first signal is negative |

**Test:** ask the founder "if this takes 8 years to reach obvious dominance, what's your play?" A missionary says "great, that's the plan." A mercenary flinches.

Only Missionary founders / CEOs build categories. This is not a personality preference — it's a survival requirement for the category-design timeline.

## 5. Diagnostic questions the framework wants the user to answer

- Are we in the Existing Market Trap? (Are we being compared to something old and losing the comparison?)
- What is the problem — as we would name it, if we owned the language?
- What 2–3 words name this category?
- What is our POV in one story: problem → ramifications → future → outcomes? Is it disputable?
- Are we the Missionary company here, or the Mercenary?
- What's the Lightning Strike we can plan in 3–6 months? What are we hijacking?
- Where's the ecosystem going to come from — analysts, partners, developers, missionaries?
- What's the Data Flywheel we're building for year-5 defensibility?
- If we were the incumbent, how would we categorize us to bury us? Are we accepting that framing?
- Is our CEO ready to be Chief Category Officer, or are we trying to delegate this?

## 6. Tests for whether the category work is landing

- **Analysts start using your category name in their reports.**
- **Prospects volunteer the category to you** on discovery calls (they picked up the language on their own).
- **Competitors start defending themselves** against your framing — the classic Salesforce/Siebel signal.
- **Talent applies "because they believe"** — Missionary flywheel spinning.
- **Investors pattern-match to your category**, not to the old one.
- **Media doesn't need your press release** to write the category story.
- **A stranger asks you "You're the [Category Name] company, right?"** — someone you've never pitched, using your language.
- **Category-adjacent third parties build on top of you** — apps, integrations, agencies — because they think the category is real.

If none of these are firing after 12–18 months of category work, either the POV is too weak or the Lightning Strikes aren't hijacking anything real.

## 7. Composition rules with other frameworks (short)

- With **Dunford ([[obviously-awesome]])** — Play Bigger creates the category; Dunford positions inside it once it's real. Do not category-design a positioning problem.
- With **Raskin ([[strategic-narrative]])** — Play Bigger creates the category; Raskin renders it into a sales-narrative artifact. Raskin's "old game / new game" is a beautiful expression of Play Bigger's Frotos and POV.
- With **Martin ([[playing-to-win]])** — Martin's cascade (where to play / how to win) is answered inside a category. Play Bigger asks whether the category itself is the choice.
- With **Helmer ([[7-powers]])** — Category Kingship is where Counter-Positioning, Cornered Resource, Scale Economies, and Network Economies concentrate. Play Bigger describes the *how* of getting to a King position; 7 Powers describes the *why* it's durable.
- With **Moore ([[crossing-the-chasm]])** — Chasm is inside a category; Play Bigger creates the category. The authors themselves say they were writing "Chasm for a new era."
