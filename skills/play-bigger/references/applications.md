# Play Bigger — Applications and adjacencies

> When Category Design is the right tool, when it isn't, and how it composes with adjacent frameworks. This file exists so the assistant doesn't do Play Bigger at a positioning problem, or Dunford at a category-creation problem. Both mistakes are common and both destroy the user's work.

## When Play Bigger is the right tool

Reach for this skill when:

- The user's product exists in a market that **doesn't have a clean category name yet** — the market keeps categorizing them as an incumbent's "better version."
- The user has a **founding insight** (market or technology) that reframes an existing problem, or names a problem the market hasn't named yet.
- The user is **stuck in the Existing Market Trap** — being compared to something old and losing.
- The user is deciding **whether to create a category or compete inside one** — and has the risk tolerance / timeline for the 6–10-year missionary bet.
- The user is preparing to run a **Lightning Strike** — a bet-the-company market-conditioning event.
- The user is building **AI-native infrastructure** and needs to escape the Bolt-on AI Trap.
- The user is doing **personal brand / consulting / creator / writer** work and wants to be a Category of One (Snow Leopard).
- The user is an **incumbent** that wants to design a *new* category (not defend the old one) — the Corporate Continuous Category Design playbook.

## When Play Bigger is the WRONG tool

Redirect out of the skill when:

- **The user has PMF inside a real, accepted market category** and just needs to sharpen how they win against real competitive alternatives → **[[obviously-awesome]]** (April Dunford). Positioning is inside a category; Play Bigger creates the category. Do not category-design a positioning problem.
- **The user needs a sales-narrative artifact** (a deck, a CEO all-hands, an investor pitch, a launch narrative) for a category they're already designing → **[[strategic-narrative]]** (Andy Raskin). Raskin's 5-part narrative renders the category change into a buyer story. Play Bigger designs the category; Raskin narrates it. They compose beautifully.
- **The user is doing internal strategic-choice work** (where to play / how to win / capabilities / management systems) inside an accepted category → **[[playing-to-win]]** (Roger Martin).
- **The user is defending an existing profitable category** against a challenger. Category Design is a category-*creation* discipline; category defense is a different move (incumbent playbooks, 7 Powers, Christensen's incumbent survival material).
- **The user is asking for tactical copy** — a tagline, a homepage headline, a brand identity. All downstream of category design; a beautiful tagline for a company stuck in the Existing Market Trap doesn't rescue the trap.
- **The user is pre-founding-insight** — they don't have a problem framing yet, they have a "we should build something in AI." That's a discovery / JTBD phase problem, not a category-design problem.
- **The user's timeline / risk tolerance is 12–18 months to profitability.** Category Design is a 6–10-year missionary bet. If the structure doesn't support that, name it and don't set them up to fail.

## Adjacencies — how Play Bigger composes with other frameworks

### **[[obviously-awesome]] — April Dunford (Positioning + Sales Pitch)**

The most important adjacency and the most common confusion. **Complementary, not competing.**

- **Dunford's positioning is INSIDE a category.** Pick your competitive alternatives, define unique attributes, cluster value themes, choose the best-fit customer, choose the market category (frame of reference).
- **Play Bigger's category design CREATES the category.** Frame the problem, name the category, take a POV, run the Lightning Strike, build the ecosystem.
- **They compose:** Play Bigger creates the category. Once the category exists as a real, market-accepted container, Dunford positions individual product lines *inside* it. A Category King who won on Play Bigger uses Dunford's method to position their sub-products.
- **Dunford's own pushback:** ~90% of recent successful public tech companies won *inside* existing categories, not by creating new ones. Category Design is high-variance, expensive, and demands missionary founders. **Not for everyone.** If the user has an obvious category and just needs to win in it, Dunford's method is dramatically higher-probability.
- **Sequencing decision the assistant makes:** ask *"Do you already have a real category the market accepts, and you're trying to win inside it? Or are you trying to make the market accept a new category?"* First answer → Dunford. Second answer → Play Bigger.

### **[[strategic-narrative]] — Andy Raskin**

Very close adjacency in *tone*, different in *scope*. Also complementary.

- **Raskin's 5-part narrative** (Name a Change in the World → Winners & Losers → Promised Land → Magic Gifts → Evidence) is a **sales-narrative artifact** — the story a CEO tells in a deck, an all-hands, an investor pitch.
- **Play Bigger's POV** is the **category-defining posture** the whole company organizes around — the discipline behind the narrative.
- **They compose:**
  - Raskin's "Old Game / New Game" ≈ Play Bigger's category reframe (from incumbent's category to newly-designed category).
  - Raskin's "Promised Land" ≈ the "to" side of Play Bigger's Frotos.
  - Raskin's "Change in the World" ≈ Play Bigger's Founding Insight, externalized as a story.
  - Raskin's "Magic Gifts" ≈ Play Bigger's product taxonomy in the mobilization artifacts, framed as the buyer's tools.
- **Both agree on:** "different not better," "problem-first, product-last," CEO-owned, ecosystem-amplified.
- **The move for the assistant:** if the user needs the *strategy* (which category, what POV) → Play Bigger. If they need the *story* (the deck, the launch narrative) for a category they're already designing → Raskin. If they need both, sequence Play Bigger first, then Raskin.

### **[[playing-to-win]] — Roger Martin (with A.G. Lafley)**

Complementary; different altitudes.

- Martin's cascade (Winning Aspiration / Where to Play / How to Win / Capabilities / Management Systems) is answered **inside a chosen category** — Martin assumes the category exists.
- Play Bigger asks: **what if the category itself is the choice?** Design the category, then Martin's cascade runs cleanly inside it.
- **Composition:** Martin's Where-to-Play + How-to-Win are the answer once Play Bigger has answered "which category." A user who's clear on both is doing category-level strategy at both altitudes.

### **[[7-powers]] — Hamilton Helmer**

Play Bigger's Category Kingship is where several of Helmer's 7 Powers concentrate.

- **Counter-Positioning** — Category Kings win because incumbents can't respond without cannibalizing themselves (classic Christensen mechanism, Helmer's Power name). Category Design is one of the sharpest routes to Counter-Positioning.
- **Cornered Resource** — First to frame the problem often locks in analyst recognition, category-name control, and category-language — a cornered resource of *mindshare*.
- **Scale Economies + Network Economies** — The Data Flywheel is these two, applied to data-generation loops that only the King's scale can produce.
- **Play Bigger describes the *how* of getting to a King position; 7 Powers describes the *why* it's durable.** Read together.

### **[[crossing-the-chasm]] — Geoffrey Moore**

Play Bigger authors themselves say they were writing *"Chasm for a new era."*

- Moore's Chasm is about market-development inside an existing technology-adoption lifecycle — the gap between early adopters and pragmatists.
- Play Bigger says: **don't cross the chasm — dig a new one, in a new category, on your terms.** If you category-design well, you don't need to cross a chasm that's positioned against an incumbent's category.
- **They compose:** Chasm is inside a category; Play Bigger creates the category. A category-designed company still crosses a chasm inside its own new category — but the chasm is on the King's terms, not the incumbent's.

### **W. Chan Kim & Renée Mauborgne — Blue Ocean Strategy**

**Explicitly rejected** by Category Pirates. See `post-book.md` §4.

- Blue Ocean assumes there's an ocean to find. Play Bigger says: there isn't. Reject the premise. Build the space itself.
- Practically: Blue Ocean is *value-innovation* inside a redefined industry. Category Design is *belief-building* to install a new category in the market's mind.
- **Different assumption about what a "market" is.** Don't compose them uncritically. If the user brings Blue Ocean framing, name the pushback.

### **Clayton Christensen — The Innovator's Dilemma**

Play Bigger explicitly acknowledges Christensen.

- The Innovator's Dilemma is the *mechanism* by which incumbents fail to design new categories: doing so would cannibalize their existing profitable category, so they don't.
- Play Bigger prescribes: **a startup can, an incumbent almost never will.** Play Bigger's own line: *"The odds of a start-up becoming a billion-dollar category king are better than the odds of a new product from a big old corporation creating a new category."*
- **Composition:** Christensen explains why the incumbent's Existing Market Trap is structural. Play Bigger's Category Design is the escape route — but the escape is much more available to challengers than to incumbents.

### **Al Ries & Jack Trout — Positioning / 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing**

Foundational; Play Bigger cites them explicitly.

- Ries & Trout's immutable laws are cited in *Play Bigger*: Law of Leadership, Law of the Category, Law of the Mind, Law of Perception, Law of Focus, Law of the Ladder, Law of Division.
- Category Pirates' *The 22 Laws of Category Design* is the explicit successor to Ries & Trout's *22 Immutable Laws of Marketing* for the modern (Data Flywheel, ecosystem, AI-native) era.

### **JTBD (Christensen / Ulwick / Moesta)**

- JTBD gives you the raw material for **framing the problem** (Play Bigger step 1). The "Job" the customer is trying to get done maps directly to the problem the category names.
- JTBD switch interviews (Moesta) surface the forces of change that Play Bigger's POV externalizes as a category story.
- **Composition:** run JTBD to find the sharpest problem framing, then Play Bigger to name and dominate the category around it.

### **Marty Cagan — Product Operating Model / Product Vision**

- Cagan's product vision doc = a written narrative of the future the product enables, 3–10 years out. Product-org-facing (aligns PMs, engineers).
- Play Bigger's POV is GTM-facing (aligns sales, marketing, investors, ecosystem).
- **Composition:** a team using both has Cagan's product vision *inside* Play Bigger's category POV. Consistent if aligned; conflict signal if not.

### **Bob Sutton, Peter Drucker, generic "strategy" advice**

Don't blend Play Bigger with generic strategy advice. Play Bigger is opinionated (missionary, category creation, 6–10-year horizons, CEO-owned POV, "different not better"). Generic strategy frameworks (SWOT, 5 Forces, Ansoff, BCG matrix) either assume a category exists (they're inside-a-category tools) or don't take a position on category creation at all. If the user is running Play Bigger, keep the frame; if they're doing generic corp strategy, that's a different framework choice.

## Composition sequences that work well

- **Discovery → JTBD → Play Bigger → Dunford** — starts with problem discovery, sharpens the job, designs the category, then positions inside it once it's real.
- **Play Bigger → Raskin → Cagan** — designs the category, renders it as a sales narrative, then internalizes the narrative as product vision for engineering.
- **Play Bigger → 7 Powers audit** — designs the category, then audits which Helmer Powers the King position is actually generating and how to reinforce them.
- **Martin's Playing to Win + Play Bigger** — the cascade + the category-creation choice, run together for a company that treats category as a first-class strategic choice.

## Composition anti-patterns

- **Play Bigger + Blue Ocean** — different market assumptions. Blends produce muddled strategy.
- **Play Bigger + generic "brand strategy"** — brand is downstream of category. Doing brand work first sets the category by accident (usually as the incumbent's category).
- **Dunford + Play Bigger, blended** — the two produce different answers to "what category are we in?" (Dunford: pick the one that best highlights your value inside existing containers; Play Bigger: create a new one). Sequence, don't blend.
- **Play Bigger + waterfall enterprise strategy planning** — Category Design is missionary and non-consensus; waterfall enterprise planning is consensus-driven and quarterly. The two crush each other. Pick.
