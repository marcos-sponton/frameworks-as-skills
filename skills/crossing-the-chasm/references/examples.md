## Documentum — the archetypal beachhead case

**Where Moore uses it:** *Crossing the Chasm* 3rd ed (2014), Lenny's Podcast (Jan 2024), across Chasm Institute training material. HBS case exists: [Documentum, Inc.](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=28459).

**The situation:** early 1990s. Documentum was a content-management platform with a horizontal product story. Sales stalled. Broad-based positioning didn't move pragmatists.

**The beachhead choice:** **pharma regulatory-affairs departments preparing new-drug applications (NDAs)**.

- **Segment size:** ~40 target organizations globally.
- **Compelling reason to buy:** new-drug applications ran 250,000–500,000 pages. Every day of delay in filing cost approximately **$1M in lost patent life**. The process was broken, the pain was quantified, and every regulatory-affairs VP knew the number.
- **Whole product built for the segment:** integrations with specific FDA electronic-submission requirements, industry-standard document formats, partner services from pharma-experienced systems integrators, references from early pharma adopters.
- **Result:** won **30 of the ~40 target organizations in the first year** — near-total beachhead dominance.

**The Bowling Alley cascade:**
- **Pin 2:** chemical companies — same regulated-document workflow, different industry, references from pharma leveraged directly.
- **Pin 3:** oil refineries — same compliance-heavy documentation, adjacent industry, references from chemicals leveraged.
- **Pin 4:** financial services — different vertical, similar regulated-document use case.

**How the framework applied:**
- **TALC / stage:** crossing the chasm (Early Market → Bowling Alley).
- **Beachhead sizing:** ~40 orgs = small enough to lead, but the trapped value per org ($1M/day per delayed application) made it big enough to matter.
- **Whole product:** built explicitly for the pharma-regulatory workflow, then leveraged into adjacent verticals.
- **Bowling Alley discipline:** each new vertical won using the previous vertical's references, not new visionary sales.

**Why the case is instructive:** it's the archetypal story of a real compelling reason to buy (quantified broken-process pain) combined with disciplined beachhead selection and reference-leveraged expansion. Moore returns to this case constantly because it makes the point that the framework, when applied rigorously, works.

**Quotable moment:**
> "Documentum didn't try to serve every industry. They picked one — pharma regulatory affairs — because the pain was quantified and the whole product could be built for that specific workflow." — paraphrased across Moore's talks and *Crossing the Chasm* 3rd ed.

## Salesforce.com — SaaS as chasm-crossing move against on-prem CRM

**Where Moore uses it:** *Crossing the Chasm* 3rd ed (2014), across Chasm Institute training. Moore has been a consultant to Salesforce.

**The situation:** early 2000s. Enterprise CRM was dominated by Siebel — on-prem, heavy IT deployment, long sales cycles, high TCO. The pragmatist-CRM buyer was buying Siebel not because they loved it, but because it was the safe standard.

**The chasm-crossing move:** SaaS delivery + focus on sales-team-usability as the beachhead value. Not competing on Siebel's feature completeness (Salesforce lost that fight); competing on adoption + speed-to-value for sales users.

**Whole product for the beachhead:**
- Zero-IT deployment (browser + credit card).
- Sales-team-first UX (Siebel's UX was IT-administrator-first).
- Ecosystem of AppExchange integrations that filled the enterprise-integration gap partner-by-partner rather than product-completeness-first.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows how a delivery-model shift (SaaS vs. on-prem) plus a segment-appropriate value theme (sales adoption vs. IT deployment) can cross the chasm against a dominant on-prem incumbent. It also shows the Tornado transition — Salesforce eventually crossed into horizontal share-grab against Siebel and everyone else.

**Stage arc:** Early Market (2000–2003) → Chasm crossing (2003–2006, sales-focused SaaS beachhead) → Bowling Alley (mid-2000s, vertical CRM expansions) → Tornado (late 2000s, dominant SaaS CRM standard) → Main Street (2010s+, platform / share-of-wallet expansion via Force.com, acquisitions).

## Aruba Networks — beachhead in US higher education

**Where Moore uses it:** *Crossing the Chasm* 3rd ed (2014), Chasm Institute case material.

**The situation:** mid-2000s. Enterprise wireless-networking was dominated by Cisco. Aruba was a smaller player with a specific technical advantage (centralized wireless controllers vs. Cisco's distributed model).

**The beachhead choice:** **US colleges and universities**. A specific vertical where campus-scale wireless coverage, high user density, and BYOD dynamics made Aruba's centralized model demonstrably superior.

**Whole product for higher ed:** campus-scale wireless integration + IT-department-friendly management + references from early higher-ed adopters. Aruba built the vertical-specific whole product (services, integrations, references) before trying to compete horizontally against Cisco.

**Why the case is instructive:** shows how a smaller player can beachhead against a Gorilla by picking a vertical where the incumbent's model is structurally weaker, and by shipping a vertical-specific whole product before attempting horizontal competition.

## Intel — Operation Crush (tornado-era, 1980)

**Where Moore uses it:** episodically in *Inside the Tornado* and tornado discussions. **Original 1980s example — still canonical for Tornado dynamics.**

**The situation:** Intel's memory business was under pressure from Japanese DRAM manufacturers. Intel pivoted to microprocessors and launched "Operation Crush" — a horizontal share-grab campaign against Motorola's 68000 to establish the x86 as the industry standard.

**Why the case is instructive:** archetype of a Tornado-stage move — reverse the vertical / bespoke playbook, standardize, sell horizontal, grab share above all. Intel eventually became the microprocessor Gorilla with a power-law market-share distribution.

**Note:** this is an original historical example, retained in the 3rd edition because it remains the sharpest illustration of Tornado discipline.

## Cisco / Microsoft / SAP / Oracle — post-tornado gorillas (canonical historical structure)

**Where Moore uses it:** *Inside the Tornado* (1995), *The Gorilla Game* (1998), across all subsequent Zone to Win material.

Post-tornado power-law examples:
- **Cisco** — Gorilla of enterprise networking. Dominant share, network-effect + switching-cost moat.
- **Microsoft** — Gorilla of client operating systems (in the 1990s–2000s window). Also Gorilla of productivity suites.
- **SAP / Oracle** — enterprise-software Gorillas in their respective categories.
- **Intel** — Gorilla of CPUs.

**Why these cases are instructive:** they illustrate the ~50% share, ~75–80% of profits dominance that Moore's Tornado theory predicts. They also illustrate the Chimp / Monkey structure around each Gorilla (e.g., AMD as Chimp to Intel's Gorilla).

## AI use cases — 2024–2026 applied cases

**Source:** Moore's own 2024–2026 essays and interviews. These are Moore's most recent applied cases and the operational anchor for AI-era chasm-crossing sessions.

### AI use cases that HAVE crossed the chasm (mid-2024)

From [Forbes / Randy Bean, Jul 14, 2024](https://www.forbes.com/sites/randybean/2024/07/14/crossing-the-ai-chasm-with-technology-thinker-and-visionary-geoffrey-moore/):

- **High-frequency trading (HFT)** — quantified microsecond-latency edge with directly-attributable P&L.
- **Digital ad placement** — measurable click-through / conversion optimization with per-impression attribution.
- **Financial regulatory compliance** — quantified regulatory-fine-avoidance value plus repeatable audit-trail requirements.

**Common pattern:** each has a specific broken process where AI-driven decision-making removes the pain, and the value is measurable directly in dollars or basis points.

### AI use cases likely to cross next

From Moore, Forbes/Randy Bean, Jul 14, 2024:

- **Air traffic control** — organizations under unsurmountable performance pressure.
- **Public safety**.
- **Elementary education**.
- **Home elder care**.

**Common pattern:** organizations facing systemic performance pressure that current human-only processes can't meet. The trapped value is severe enough to force adoption despite change-management friction.

### Agentic AI beachheads (not yet across the chasm as of Feb 2026)

From [Moore blog / Diginomica, Feb 6, 2026](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/can-agentic-ai-cross-the-chasm-without-falling-in-tech-thought-leader-geoffrey-moore-assesses-progress-in-straddling-the-divide/):

- **Coding** — moving toward crossing, but not yet.
- **Customer service in call centers** — moving toward crossing, but not yet.

**Moore's diagnostic:** neither yet possesses enough trapped value in the process to cause the complete re-engineering that defines a real chasm crossing.

> "There are some beachhead markets, but when you 'cross the chasm' it's when one function completely re-engineers itself." — Moore, Diginomica Feb 2026

**Skill rule for AI-era sessions:** use Moore's use-case-by-use-case distinction, not the "AI has crossed" narrative dominant in secondary coverage. A use case has crossed the chasm only when one function has completely re-engineered itself around the AI.

## Cross-industry disruption arc (post-2020)

**Source:** Moore's [Memoori interview, Feb 3, 2022](https://memoori.com/are-we-crossing-the-proptech-chasm-geoffrey-moore-interview/).

Moore's framing of the shift in where tech disruption is happening:

> "In the past, most of the technology disruption was actually happening inside the tech sector itself… Now we're seeing it in hospitality, transportation, and buildings."

**Arc:**
1. **Within tech** — Microsoft vs. IBM, Google vs. Yahoo, Amazon vs. Borders.
2. **Adjacent industries** — media, streaming, retail, fintech.
3. **Traditional industries** — hospitality, transportation, real estate / buildings, elder care, education.

Useful mental model when placing a company on the disruption timeline: the further from software the target industry is, the more the whole-product gap tends to matter, and the more staged the chasm-crossing needs to be.

## Meta-note on case selection

Moore's case selection has always paired **a canonical historical example** (Documentum, Intel Operation Crush) with **a current-market illustration** (in the 3rd ed: Salesforce; in the 2024–2026 essays: AI use cases). This pattern is deliberate — the historical case anchors the framework's mechanics, the current case shows its ongoing applicability. Follow the same pattern in sessions: pair one canonical case with one current illustration whenever possible.
