# Crossing the Chasm — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical operating rules — Moore's "here's how it actually plays out" moves — that separate applying the framework well from doing generic GTM. Attribution is precise: this from the 1991/2014 book, this from *Inside the Tornado*, this from a 2024 LinkedIn Pulse essay, this from Lenny's Podcast Jan 25, 2024.

## Do's

### Locate yourself on the map before deciding anything

Moore's most repeated methodological instinct. Every recommendation is stage-conditional. **Where are you on the Technology Adoption Life Cycle / Category Maturity Life Cycle?** Answer that before making any GTM choice.

Practical version: run the 9-Point Strategy Checklist twice — once as-if you're in Stage A, once as-if you're in Stage B. If the recommended answers are radically different (they should be), the diagnosis has real leverage.

### Concentrate force on one beachhead segment (D-Day discipline)

The single most-repeated correction. Teams start chasm-crossing by listing 4 verticals they could pursue and picking one for Q1, one for Q2, and so on. That is a broad-front invasion; it will fail.

**How to apply:**
1. Generate ~50 target-customer scenarios via Target-Customer Characterization (customer profile + use case before + use case after).
2. Filter to a shortlist of 5 using the 9-Point Strategy Checklist criteria.
3. Pick ONE. Commit whole-product investment to that one for the next 12–18 months.
4. Say no — publicly and repeatedly — to the other four, especially when a big deal in a different segment tempts you.

> "The D-Day strategy keeps everyone on point—if we don't take Normandy, we don't have to worry about how we're going to take Paris." — *Crossing the Chasm*

### Ship the whole product for one segment before you ship a partial product for many

Pragmatists buy the whole product or they don't buy. A 60%-complete whole product for four segments serves nobody; a 100%-complete whole product for one segment cracks the chasm.

Build partnerships and services to close the gap. Don't try to build every component of the whole product internally — most segments need integrations, industry-specific configurations, and partner services that will always take longer to build than to partner for.

### Land a marquee / lighthouse customer in your beachhead

One prominent reference customer in your beachhead vertical is worth ten mid-tier logos. Pragmatists are looking sideways at their peers; a peer they respect adopting the product is the trigger.

> "Getting a lighthouse customer can put a company on the map and attract attention." — Moore, [Lenny's Podcast, Jan 25, 2024](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead)

### Guarantee outcomes, don't discount

Cutting price does not reduce a pragmatist's perceived risk — worse, discounts usually force the vendor to cut corners on scope or support, which *increases* risk. Instead, make a guaranteed commitment to solving the specific broken process.

> "You don't need to discount your product or service, but you do need to make a guaranteed commitment to solving the problem." — Moore, Lenny's Podcast, Jan 25, 2024

### Sequence the bowling pins for reference-leverage

The next pin must be knock-downable by *the current pin's references*. Two valid adjacency directions:
- Same customer type in a new industry.
- New use case for the same customer type.

If a new segment can't use your existing case studies, it isn't an adjacent pin. It's a second beachhead — and you can't afford one.

### Reverse the playbook when the tornado tips

Signals the tornado has begun: horizontal deals close on features/price without the whole product being pre-built for the buyer's vertical; competitors show up in every deal; standardization pressure from buyers exceeds customization requests. When you see it — **reverse everything you were doing in the Bowling Alley**: standardize, sell horizontal, grab share, ship product not solutions.

> "The winning strategy does not just change as we move from stage to stage, it actually reverses the prior strategy." — *Inside the Tornado*

### Transfer resources from Context to Core continuously (Core / Context)

CEO-level responsibility per *Dealing with Darwin* (2005). Anything that isn't your differentiation is context; automate, outsource, or eliminate it to free capacity for core. The bias is toward more Core, less Context, always.

### Position stage-appropriately (positioning is Moore's #1 lever on the buying decision)

> "Positioning is the single largest influence on the buying decision." — *Crossing the Chasm*

A Bowling Alley positioning statement and a Tornado positioning statement should look different — the audience psychographic is different. Vertical, whole-product-heavy language in the Bowling Alley; horizontal, category-standard language in the Tornado. See `[[obviously-awesome]]` for the operational method of writing the positioning statement once you know the stage.

### Use the 9-Point Strategy Checklist as the workshop artifact

Chasm Institute's canonical operating template. Nine slots, filled in stage-appropriately: Target Customer, Compelling Reason to Buy, Whole Product, Partners & Allies, Distribution, Pricing, Competition, Positioning, Next Target. Use it in every GTM planning session — it forces all nine dimensions to be answered consistently for the current stage.

### Use Zone to Win as an operating-model overlay, not an org chart

Same employees can serve multiple zones. What differs between zones is the rules of engagement — metrics, funding cadence, decision rights. Rewriting the org chart is not the point.

## Don'ts

### Don't chase visionary deals because "logos matter"

Visionary logos convince other visionaries, not pragmatists. Pragmatists discount visionary references entirely — a visionary logo may even *hurt* your credibility with pragmatists ("that's a science-project buyer, we're a business"). Chasing visionary deals post-chasm dilutes the segmentation discipline and burns whole-product investment on bespoke work that won't propagate.

### Don't try to serve a huge segment because "the market is big"

Moore's explicit anti-pattern:
> "Most entries by foreign companies into China, they get their faces shot off. And the reason is the rationale is, it's big, we could get some of that."

The valid reason to enter a segment: **customers there are bereft** — they lack something you can provide, and they know it. Not "the market is big."

### Don't build a platform pre-chasm

Pre-chasm you're a departmental point product. Platform ambition here just splits scarce whole-product investment across too many use cases. Nail one beachhead first, then let platform ambitions emerge from the bowling alley when adjacent pins reveal shared infrastructure needs.

> "You are still sub-scale for a customer CFO to consider you desirable as a go-to vendor. Your only ally will be a process owner with a problem process that is not getting the IT support they need." — Moore, ["After the Chasm—Scaling Beyond the Beachhead," 2024](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/after-the-chasm-scaling-beyond-the-beachhead/)

### Don't try to Tornado before the Bowling Alley base exists

If you standardize before you've won a repeatable beachhead + adjacent pins, you can't ship a whole product at scale. The horizontal deals will come in and you won't be able to fulfill them consistently. Result: reputation damage precisely at the moment you needed the mainstream to hear the buzz.

### Don't run Main Street plays during a Tornado

Customer intimacy and retention motions are Main Street plays. Deploy them during a Tornado and your competitors take horizontal share while you're busy loving your existing customers. Tornado plays are share-grab plays; save intimacy for Main Street.

### Don't keep Bowling Alley plays during a Tornado

Keep verticalizing during a Tornado and you cede horizontal share to a rival who standardized faster. This is the mistake that turns a would-be Gorilla into a Chimp.

### Don't discount to reduce a pragmatist's risk

See the "guarantee outcomes" heuristic above. Discounting increases perceived risk with pragmatists — it signals corner-cutting, not confidence. Guarantee the outcome instead.

### Don't confuse operational excellence with any of Moore's Powers

This is a Helmer-style critique that Moore shares (see `[[7-powers]]`). Excellence without a stage-conditional differentiator is table stakes. Moore's Hierarchy of Powers puts Execution Power *last* precisely because it's the least defensible on its own.

### Don't invoke the Transformation Zone as a rounding error

*Zone to Win* anti-pattern. A Transformation must be scaled aggressively enough to *matter* — Moore's threshold is **≥10% of company revenue**. Anything less is Incubation dressed up. Related anti-pattern: **funding Transformation from Performance Zone's operating budget** — kills both.

### Don't metricize Incubation like Performance

*Zone to Win* anti-pattern. Incubation must be run on VC-style milestones, not P&L. Applying revenue-growth KPIs kills the experiments before they show signal. Incubation is protected by its exemption from Performance and Productivity metrics.

### Don't treat context like core

*Dealing with Darwin* anti-pattern. Over-investing in what doesn't differentiate leaves you cost-disadvantaged against competitors who automated or outsourced the same work. Related: failing to *repatriate* labor as new context emerges — context work should be continuously moved to automation or partners.

### Don't confuse TALC with a marketing funnel

TALC is a market-adoption bell curve of populations, not a per-customer buying-stage sequence. A single customer does not "move through TALC"; they *are* one of the segments. Marketing-funnel thinking pushes you toward per-buyer nurture sequences; TALC pushes you toward segment-appropriate playbooks. Don't mix.

### Don't assume pragmatists become visionaries with better copy

Pragmatism is a stable disposition, not a copy problem. Rewriting the homepage more compellingly does not convert pragmatists into visionaries. What converts pragmatists is peer references + whole product + compelling reason to buy — in that order.

### Don't leapfrog the Digital Systems Maturity Model

Enterprise customers on Systems of Record can't consume Systems of Intelligence. The sequence matters. Selling AI-driven decision automation to a company still on ERP without an engagement layer is Moore's version of trying to Tornado before Bowling Alley — the whole-product gap is too large.

### Don't use 1991-era examples as your default illustrations

Moore rewrote most cases for the 3rd edition (2014). Default to Salesforce, Documentum, Aruba, Workday, VMware, Mozilla, Lithium — not the retired Blockbuster / generic HP / generic Oracle 1991 canon. When a 1991 case is still the sharpest illustration, mark it explicitly as an "original 1991 example."

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The whale that isn't a beachhead

One giant visionary deal comes in, everyone celebrates, and the company tilts toward customizing for that customer. The whale isn't a pragmatist; the whale isn't in a repeatable segment; the whale is a bespoke build with a big logo. Result: whole-product investment burned on non-transferable work.

**Diagnostic:** if the champion inside the whale is a technology enthusiast or visionary (Chief Innovation Officer, Head of Emerging Tech), you're building bespoke, not crossing.

### The "we're a platform" trap pre-chasm

Every founder wants to be a platform. Pre-chasm, every additional use case you support splits your scarce whole-product investment. Stay departmental. Ally with one process owner who has one broken process. Let the platform ambition emerge from the pins that share infrastructure once you've won them.

### Longer sales cycles as a chasm indicator

Sales cycles typically shorten as a product matures. If yours are *lengthening* — deals stalling in evaluation, RFPs proliferating, pilots not converting — the read is almost always: you've cleared the Early Market and are now trying to sell to pragmatists without pragmatist-appropriate proof, positioning, and whole product.

### Reference calls that stop landing

Related. Your existing customer references are visionaries; the prospect is pragmatist; the reference call goes fine on the technology and terribly on the buying decision. The reference isn't wrong — they're the wrong reference for this buyer. Fix: get a beachhead reference in the pragmatist's own vertical.

### Bowling-pin adjacency that isn't actually adjacent

You pick a "next pin" that a marketer decided was similar to the beachhead — but the pin doesn't accept your beachhead's case studies as evidence. That's a second beachhead, not an adjacent pin. Symptom: sales cycles in the new segment behave like Early Market cycles, not Bowling Alley cycles.

### Tornado false positives

A big feature-driven deal wave comes in and someone declares "we're in the tornado." Test: are the horizontal deals closing on features/price without whole-product-per-vertical pre-work? Are competitors in every deal? Are buyers pushing standardization requests? If two of three answers are no, it's not yet a tornado — it's a good quarter in the Bowling Alley.

### Tornado false negatives (the more dangerous version)

The market has actually tipped and you're still verticalizing, ceding horizontal share to a rival. This is how would-be Gorillas become Chimps. Symptom: your win rate is holding in your beachhead but your total addressable share is shrinking.

### The Zone to Win org rewrite

Executives read *Zone to Win* and re-org into four divisions matching the zones. That's not how Moore intends it. Zones are operating-model overlays, not org units — same employees can serve multiple zones with different rules of engagement. Re-orgs into the four zones usually stall Transformation because Performance and Productivity resources get walled off from the bet.

### The "AI has crossed the chasm" claim

Moore is explicit: AI as a category has crossed the chasm for *narrow, high-value use cases* (high-frequency trading, digital ad placement, financial regulatory compliance). But use-case-specific crossings don't mean the category as a whole has crossed. Agentic AI, per Moore's Feb 2026 Diginomica piece, has NOT crossed the chasm — beachheads exist (coding, customer service) but "neither possesses enough trapped value in the process to cause the complete takeover."

> "There are some beachhead markets, but when you 'cross the chasm' it's when one function completely re-engineers itself." — Moore, [Diginomica, Feb 6, 2026](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/can-agentic-ai-cross-the-chasm-without-falling-in-tech-thought-leader-geoffrey-moore-assesses-progress-in-straddling-the-divide/)

Don't overclaim chasm-crossing when the trapped value in the specific customer's process isn't sufficient to force complete re-engineering.

### The customer-delight substitution for compelling reason

Post-book, Moore has become sharper on this — from ["Delighting your customers is bunk. Delivering on their desired outcomes is not."](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/delighting-your-customers-is-bunk-delivering-on-their-desired-outcomes-is-not/):

> "Delight, after all, is an evanescent experience that comes and goes pretty much as it pleases. It cannot be reliably evoked."

> "What they really want to buy is outcomes."

Customer-delight metrics (NPS, CSAT) substituted for compelling-reason-to-buy metrics (does the buyer's broken process still cost them $X/day?) is a symptom of the framework being abandoned in favor of feel-good ops.

## Pro tips

### Anchor every workshop with a stage-diagnosis question first

Open every planning session with: *"Where are we on the TALC, and what evidence tells us that?"* If the room can't agree — or the evidence is thin — that's the highest-leverage 30 minutes of the day. Deferring stage diagnosis produces stage-mixed plans.

### Prefer segment-level thinking over persona-level thinking

TALC is a segment framework, not a persona framework. Pragmatist ≠ persona. A pragmatist in pharma regulatory affairs and a pragmatist in oil-refinery operations are the same psychographic in different verticals — the pragmatism is stable, the vertical determines the whole product. Personas at buyer titles is Dunford-altitude work (see `[[obviously-awesome]]`); segment-level chasm work is Moore-altitude.

### Use "trapped value" as the deciding variable in AI-era decisions

Moore's 2024–2026 essays lean heavily on trapped value — the pain a use case currently accumulates. If the current process doesn't accumulate enough pain, agentic AI (or any innovation) will not displace it, however cool the demo. Use trapped value as the discriminator when a founder is picking between beachheads.

### Reference Documentum when explaining beachhead selection

Documentum is Moore's canonical Bowling Alley success. Pharma regulatory affairs = ~40 target organizations, ~$1M/day in lost patent life per delayed application = textbook compelling reason. Won 30/40 in the first year. Cascaded into chemicals, oil refineries, financial services. Use it as the reference case whenever the user is picking a first vertical.

### Use the Hierarchy of Powers as a growth-diagnosis tool

When growth is fading, walk down the hierarchy top-to-bottom: is the *category* losing steam (Category Power)? Are partners routing business away (Company Power)? Are the hot segments shifting (Market Power)? Is our offer no longer superior (Offer Power)? Or is execution failing (Execution Power)? Fix from the top down — Execution fixes on top of dying Category Power don't stick.

### Cross-link with Dunford when positioning comes up

Moore says *what* has to happen (segment, position, whole product); Dunford (see `[[obviously-awesome]]`) says *how* to actually write the positioning statement. Positioning is one operation inside the chasm-crossing play. If the user's problem is "we don't know what to call ourselves" and they've located themselves on TALC, hand the positioning-craft part to Dunford's method.

## Anti-patterns (bad chasm-crossing Moore explicitly names)

### Broad-front invasion (dissipation of force)

**What it looks like:** "we're going after healthcare, financial services, retail, and manufacturing simultaneously."
**Why it fails:** whole-product investment splits four ways; no single segment gets pragmatist-complete; no segment cracks; the pipeline stalls everywhere at once.
**How to redirect:** apply D-Day discipline. Pick ONE segment. Kill investment in the other three publicly.

### Mixing playbooks

**What it looks like:** during Bowling Alley, someone runs Tornado plays (kill customization, standardize) or vice versa.
**Why it fails:** each stage's playbook works because it's coherent with the buyer psychographic at that stage. Mixing produces incoherence — the customization signals fear to pragmatists, the standardization signals abandonment to bowling-alley references.
**How to redirect:** enforce stage discipline in the 9-Point Strategy Checklist. Any dimension that reads like a different stage's answer is a red flag.

### Chasing the biggest deal in front of you regardless of segment

**What it looks like:** a $2M deal comes in from a segment that isn't your beachhead. The team celebrates and drops beachhead work to close it.
**Why it fails:** the deal is bespoke; the whole-product investment doesn't propagate; the beachhead momentum stalls. You end up with one big customized customer and no repeatable motion.
**How to redirect:** apply the two-part test — is this deal in your beachhead or an adjacent pin? If no, treat it as bespoke consulting revenue with a fenced team, and don't let it dilute the beachhead campaign.

### Selling to visionaries after the chasm

**What it looks like:** the sales team keeps hunting visionaries because those are the deals they know how to close.
**Why it fails:** visionary sales don't ladder into pragmatist references. You stay in the Early Market forever, decorated with visionary logos.
**How to redirect:** re-baseline the sales team on pragmatist-appropriate proof, whole product, and reference-check flow. Different buyer, different pitch — see `[[obviously-awesome]]` for the operational method of designing the pragmatist pitch.

### Zone confusion (Transformation as rounding error)

**What it looks like:** the CEO announces a "transformation initiative" with a small team, small budget, small P&L expectation.
**Why it fails:** Transformation requires ≥10% of company revenue at the target and Performance-Zone-level executive sponsorship. Small transformation = Incubation dressed up = no signal, no traction, and cynicism from the Performance Zone.
**How to redirect:** if the bet doesn't warrant Transformation Zone treatment, keep it in Incubation. Transformation is rare and expensive; invoke it deliberately.

### "AI-first" as positioning

**What it looks like:** every product line rebrands with "AI-powered" in 2026.
**Why it fails:** AI is a trend, not (usually) a differentiator. Moore's framework holds: layer trend on top only if it makes the value more obvious. Leading with "AI-powered" when the AI isn't the differentiated value dilutes the actual positioning.
**How to redirect:** apply the Hierarchy of Powers. Which power does AI actually give you — Category (you're in the hot new category)? Offer (your product is measurably better because of AI)? Or none (AI is the tech stack, not the moat)? Position accordingly.

## Common misapplications

Teams saying they're doing chasm-crossing but aren't:

- **Multi-segment "chasm crossing"** — a broad-front invasion by another name. Not the framework.
- **"Verticalized go-to-market" without a whole product per vertical** — verticalized sales collateral doesn't equal Bowling Alley. Whole product per vertical is the test.
- **Persona-based marketing** — TALC is segment-based, not persona-based. Persona-level work is Dunford / Product Marketing altitude, downstream.
- **"Tornado" claimed with sub-30% market growth** — Moore's Category Maturity Life Cycle reserves Tornado for the mainstream stampede. Sub-30% growth is more likely Main Street or a false-positive Tornado.
- **Zone to Win as an org restructure** — operating-model overlay, not org chart.
- **Reciting the framework without staging** — advice-giving that isn't conditional on the stage is not Moore's method; it's generic GTM with vocabulary borrowed from Moore.

**Test for the real thing:** did a stage-conditional decision get made? Did the company choose ONE beachhead segment, ship a WHOLE product for that segment's compelling reason, and decline attractive-looking work in other segments? If none of the three happened, no chasm crossing is under way.
