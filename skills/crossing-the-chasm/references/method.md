# Crossing the Chasm — Method

> The canonical description of Geoffrey Moore's method in his own terms. Fidelity is the point — the framework is opinionated, and softening any of the staged distinctions ("adopters" instead of the specific psychographic labels; "market" instead of "beachhead segment"; "customization" instead of "whole product") collapses the method into generic GTM language. **The 3rd edition (2014) of *Crossing the Chasm* is canonical; where 1991 and 2014 differ, prefer 2014.**

## Definition

Moore's operating frame:

> "The point of greatest peril in the development of a high-tech market lies in making the transition from an early market dominated by a few visionary customers to a mainstream market dominated by a large block of customers who are predominantly pragmatists in orientation."
> — Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*

Two words in that definition are non-negotiable:

- **"Pragmatists"** — the mainstream is a *specific psychographic*, not a bigger version of the early market. Their buying behavior is qualitatively different.
- **"Transition"** — the failure mode isn't gradual erosion; it's a discontinuity. Companies discover they haven't crossed only after the pipeline has already collapsed.

## The Technology Adoption Life Cycle (TALC)

Adapted from Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962). Moore kept Rogers' bell curve; he added the **chasm** (the discontinuity between segments 2 and 3) plus smaller cracks between other adjacent segments.

### The five psychographic segments

| # | Segment | Moore's label | ~% of market | Buys because | Tolerates |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Innovators | **Technology enthusiasts** | ~2.5% | Interest in the technology itself | Anything |
| 2 | Early Adopters | **Visionaries** | ~13.5% | Strategic discontinuity / competitive breakthrough | Immaturity, missing pieces |
| 3 | Early Majority | **Pragmatists** | ~34% | Incremental productivity, peer references | Nothing incomplete |
| 4 | Late Majority | **Conservatives** | ~34% | Once the standard is set, price-sensitive | Nothing untested |
| 5 | Laggards | **Skeptics** | ~16% | Involuntarily, if at all | Nothing |

**Diagnostic pattern:** almost every case Moore analyzes eventually pivots on the visionary-pragmatist contrast. When a company says "our early customers love us but the market isn't taking off," the read is almost always: you're at the chasm; your early customers are visionaries; the mainstream is pragmatist; visionary references don't convert pragmatists.

**Moore's addition to Rogers:** the *chasm* between visionaries and pragmatists is Moore's contribution. Rogers did not name it. Moore also named smaller "cracks" between innovators/visionaries and between pragmatists/conservatives — the chasm is the killer, but the cracks matter for later plays.

**Common misapplication:** TALC is a *market*-adoption bell curve, not a per-customer buying-stage funnel. A single customer does not "move through TALC"; different customers *are* different segments.

## The Chasm — why it exists

The chasm exists because **pragmatists will not accept visionaries as references**. Their reasoning is not irrational — it's rational to their goals. Visionaries seek discontinuous change (breakthrough, disruption, first-mover advantage). Pragmatists seek continuity (safety, standardization, low-risk incremental improvement). A pragmatist buys because *other pragmatists* have bought — not because a visionary evangelist tells them to.

Consequence: a company that has cleared the Early Market has *zero* transferable credibility for the mainstream. The visionary logos you're proud of are, from the pragmatist's perspective, evidence that you're a science project, not evidence that you're safe to buy.

> "If pragmatists are hard to win over, they are loyal once won, often enforcing a company standard that requires the purchase of your product, and only your product, for a given requirement." — *Crossing the Chasm*

The chasm's economic sign: sales cycles get longer instead of shorter. Deals stall in evaluation. Reference calls stop landing. Whale hunting produces occasional bespoke wins that don't compound into a repeatable motion.

## The D-Day analogy

Moore's strategic frame for chasm crossing.

- **Long-term goal:** enter the mainstream (Europe).
- **The mainstream is held** by an entrenched competitor or the status quo (the Axis).
- **The chasm** is the English Channel.
- **You cross** by concentrating overwhelming force on one narrow beachhead (Normandy), not by broad-front invasion.

> "The D-Day strategy keeps everyone on point—if we don't take Normandy, we don't have to worry about how we're going to take Paris." — *Crossing the Chasm*

**The discipline is negative as much as positive.** Refusing attractive-looking segments that dissipate force is the point. A broad-front invasion kills the campaign. Moore is emphatic on Lenny's Podcast (Jan 25, 2024) that "mixing playbooks undermines effectiveness — teams must commit to one stage's approach consistently."

## Beachhead segmentation

The chosen segment must be:
- **Big enough to matter** — Moore's operational floor from Lenny (Jan 2024): capable of $100M revenue within 5 years.
- **Small enough to lead** — you must plausibly dominate it end-to-end. "A big fish in a small pond." If the largest incumbent already owns >30% of the segment, you're not going to displace them from a beachhead position.
- **A good fit with your crown jewels** — your differentiated capability must map to their compelling pain.

Segments are **psychographic + vertical + use-case**, not just "industry." Documentum's beachhead wasn't "pharma"; it was "pharma regulatory-affairs departments preparing new-drug applications" (a specific use case in a specific function of a specific industry).

**Target-Customer Characterization (TCC)** — Moore's canonical technique. The team generates ~50 one-page target-customer scenarios (customer profile + use case *before* + use case *after*), then filters down. The filtering criteria: compelling reason to buy, whole-product feasibility, competitive alternatives, partners and allies, distribution fit, pricing acceptability, positioning viability. This is the practical operationalization of the beachhead choice.

## Compelling Reason to Buy / Compelling Reason to Act

The pragmatist's threshold. Not "nice to have." Not "productivity gain." A **broken process where the pain is currently costing the customer real money and the customer knows it**.

Documentum's canonical beachhead was pharma regulatory-affairs departments where new-drug applications running 250,000–500,000 pages cost approximately **$1M/day in lost patent life** per delayed application. Textbook Compelling Reason: the process was broken, the pain was quantified, the customer knew it.

> "You don't have to evangelize the technology. You just have to take their problem off the table." — Moore, [Forbes / Randy Bean, Jul 14, 2024](https://www.forbes.com/sites/randybean/2024/07/14/crossing-the-ai-chasm-with-technology-thinker-and-visionary-geoffrey-moore/)

**Test for a real CRTB:** if the CFO can't articulate the current cost of the broken process in dollars or hours, the reason isn't compelling — it's aspirational, and pragmatists won't act on it.

## Whole Product

Adapted from Ted Levitt's generic / expected / augmented / potential product model.

**Whole product** = the minimum set of products and services required to satisfy the *pragmatist's* compelling reason to buy — not just what you ship. It includes integrations, services, training, partner ecosystem, references, documentation, and industry-specific configurations.

- In the Early Market, visionaries **fill the whole-product gap themselves** — they'll build integrations, hire consultants, tolerate missing pieces because the breakthrough is worth it.
- Pragmatists **refuse to**. They need the whole product to be *there*, not "on the roadmap."

> "Pragmatists evaluate and buy whole products." — *Crossing the Chasm* (canonical formulation)

**The whole-product gap is the operational reason the chasm exists.** Crossing the chasm = closing the whole-product gap *for one beachhead segment* via partnerships and services — not building the whole product for every possible segment simultaneously.

**Test:** for a chosen beachhead segment, can a pragmatist buy your product on Monday, have it installed by their standard integrator by Friday, get results within the quarter, and cite a peer-industry reference to their boss? If any of those breaks, the whole product isn't complete for that segment.

## Bowling Alley

Post-chasm, pre-tornado. The company owns a beachhead vertical; adjacent segments are "pins." Each new pin is won using **references from the prior pin**. Two adjacency directions:

- **Same customer type in a new industry** (same-persona, new-industry adjacency).
- **New use case for the same customer type** (same-industry, new-use-case adjacency).

Vertical-focused expansion. Whole-product investment leverages across pins (integrations built for pin 1 partially serve pin 2). Documentum's post-pharma cascade into chemicals, oil refineries, and financial services is the archetype.

> "You want your team to be plowing in the adjacent field, not harvesting in the initial one." — Moore, ["After the Chasm—Scaling Beyond the Beachhead," geoffreyamoore.com](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/after-the-chasm-scaling-beyond-the-beachhead/)

> "Marketing is still a territory capture game, so you want to go after targets that are big enough to matter but small enough to lead." — same post

**Test for the next pin:** can the new segment be won using the current pin's case studies? If no, it isn't an adjacent pin — it's a second beachhead you can't afford.

## Tornado

Book: *Inside the Tornado* (1995). The mass-market hypergrowth phase when the pragmatist herd tips *en masse*.

**The playbook reverses everything you did in the Bowling Alley:**

| Bowling Alley | Tornado |
|---|---|
| Sell **vertical** | Sell **horizontal** |
| Ship **solutions** (whole product per segment) | Ship **product** (standardized) |
| Grow **share within the vertical** | Grab **market share above all** |
| **Customize** to fit each vertical | **Standardize** aggressively; kill customization |
| Margin over volume | Volume over margin |

> "The winning strategy does not just change as we move from stage to stage, it actually reverses the prior strategy." — *Inside the Tornado*

> "Infrastructure, to be useful, must be standard and global, so once the market moves to switch out the old for the new, it wants to complete this transition as rapidly as possible." — *Inside the Tornado*

**Post-tornado power-law market-share distribution:**
- **Gorilla** — dominant vendor, ~50% share, earns 75–80% of industry profits.
- **Chimps** — challengers who wanted to be gorillas and failed. 15–30% share each. Must discount 20–40% to win deals. Perpetually contest features without closing the gap.
- **Monkeys** — low-cost clones. Single-digit share. Survive on niches the Gorilla ignores.

## Main Street

Post-tornado maturity. Growth slows, category solidifies. Playbook = **share-of-wallet expansion** within the installed base:
- Extensions and add-ons.
- Up-market moves (new tiers, enterprise editions).
- Customer intimacy and customer-success motions.
- **"+1" niche modifications** — small vertical adaptations for retention and expansion.

This is where the modern customer-success operating model lives. Moore explicitly mapped Main Street to customer success at Gainsight Pulse 2022 — see [Diginomica coverage](https://diginomica.com/gainsight-pulse-2022-geoffrey-moore-maturity-model-customer-success).

## Category Maturity Life Cycle (CMLC / TMLC) — Chasm Institute canonical form

The consolidated life-cycle Chasm Institute teaches. Six phases integrating the above:

1. **Early Market** — visionaries, immature product.
2. **The Chasm** — extended sales cycles, no referenceable base.
3. **Bowling Alley** — pragmatists inside vertical niches.
4. **Tornado** — mainstream stampede, price wars, feature wars.
5. **Main Street** — saturation, niche modifications, share-of-wallet.
6. **End of Life** — obsolescence / replacement.

Reference: [Chasm Institute Frameworks page](https://www.chasminstitute.com/Resources/Frameworks/tabid/309/Default.aspx).

## 9-Point Strategy Checklist (Chasm Institute)

Applied differently per CMLC stage. **Same nine slots, different content per stage.** This is the operational template Moore's consulting practice runs on.

1. **Target Customer**
2. **Compelling Reason to Buy**
3. **Whole Product**
4. **Partners & Allies**
5. **Distribution**
6. **Pricing**
7. **Competition**
8. **Positioning**
9. **Next Target**

Use as the workshop artifact when running a stage-appropriate GTM planning session. In the Bowling Alley, points 1–4 look verticalized; in the Tornado, points 3, 5, 6, 8 shift toward standardization and horizontal reach.

## Hierarchy of Powers — from *Escape Velocity* (2011)

Investor-lens diagnostic of growth sources. Descending from most general to most specific:

1. **Category Power** — growth from being in a category the world is adopting.
2. **Company Power** — growth from partners routing business to you (gravitational pull).
3. **Market Power** — growth from being in the hot *segments* within a category at the right time.
4. **Offer Power** — growth from a demonstrably superior offer vs. alternatives.
5. **Execution Power** — growth from strategic initiatives taken past the tipping point.

An investor evaluates top-down; an operator tends to over-weight Offer + Execution and under-weight Category + Company. Moore's diagnostic instinct: when growth is fading, look up the hierarchy first (category is losing steam) before optimizing execution.

> "Contemporary management protocols and practices overweight a focus on performance and underweight a focus on power." — Moore, *Escape Velocity* (excerpted on [LinkedIn Pulse, Aug 22, 2014](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/20140822002750-649711-geoffrey-moore-escape-velocity))

## Zone to Win — 4 zones (2015 book)

Organizational operating model for incumbents managing disruption. Four zones, each with a distinct operating model, funding logic, and KPI structure.

1. **Performance Zone** — the revenue-producing lines of business. "Make the plan, make the numbers, report to the board."
2. **Productivity Zone** — shared services (IT, HR, marketing, customer success). Process-oriented: onboarding, NPS, journeys.
3. **Incubation Zone** — venture-style trial balloons for disruptive innovations. Funded on VC-style milestones. **Explicitly exempt** from Performance / Productivity metrics.
4. **Transformation Zone** — the specific bet chosen from Incubation and scaled aggressively to either (a) defend against a disruptor or (b) create a new performance line **≥10% of company revenue**.

> "Transformation is hard. You should invoke the Transformation Zone rarely." — Moore, paraphrased across the Diginomica coverage of Gainsight Pulse 2022. See [Gainsight blog on Moore's four zones for customer success](https://www.gainsight.com/blog/geoffrey-moore-applies-his-four-zones-to-customer-success/).

**Key rule:** Zone to Win is an *operating-model overlay*, not an org chart. Same employees can serve multiple zones; the rules of engagement (metrics, funding, cadence) differ per zone.

## Core vs. Context — from *Dealing with Darwin* (2005)

Innovation-portfolio filter.

- **Core** = work that drives your differentiation vs. competition.
- **Context** = everything else, however necessary.

**Rule:** transfer resources *from* context *to* core continuously — including via outsourcing or automation. Context work should be automated or outsourced continuously to free capacity for core. Moore is unusually blunt: liberating core from context is the CEO's job, not a middle-management optimization.

**Three innovation types:**
- **Differentiation** — creates competitive separation.
- **Neutralization** — closes a competitive gap to reach parity.
- **Optimization** — makes existing processes more efficient.

Core-context is the operational sibling of the Hierarchy of Powers.

## Digital Systems Maturity Model — LinkedIn Pulse, May 2017

Sequential "stairway" for enterprise digital investment. Sequence matters — no leapfrogging.

1. **Systems of Record** (ERP, CRM) — the transactional backbone.
2. **Systems of Engagement** (mobile, omni-channel) — the interaction layer.
3. **Engagement Analytics** (dashboards) — closing the feedback loop on interactions.
4. **Systems of Intelligence** (ML-driven) — decisions embedded in workflows.
5. **Systems of Disruption** (proprietary insight → new market entry) — using the accumulated data + intelligence to enter adjacent categories.

Reference: [Moore, "Digital Systems Maturity Model," LinkedIn Pulse, May 22, 2017](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/digital-systems-maturity-model-geoffrey-moore).

The pragmatic use: when a CIO wants to "do AI" but is still on step 1, the sequence tells you the next honest move is step 2, not step 4.

## Integration

Moore's frameworks nest:

- **Category-scale**: TALC / Chasm / Bowling Alley / Tornado / Main Street / CMLC — describe how *a category* evolves over time.
- **Company-scale**: Escape Velocity / Hierarchy of Powers / Zone to Win — describe how *an incumbent company* manages its portfolio across categories at different stages.
- **Portfolio-scale**: Core vs. Context, Three Innovation Types — describe how to allocate innovation resources within a company.
- **Enterprise-digital-scale**: Digital Systems Maturity Model — describes how *a customer* moves through digital investment.

Concretely, this means:
- A **company** may have multiple product lines *each at a different TALC stage*. Zone to Win is the tool for managing that portfolio; TALC is the tool for managing each product line.
- **Positioning, messaging, sales motion, pricing** are all stage-conditional. Load the current stage before deciding any of them.
- **The 9-Point Strategy Checklist is the workshop artifact** that forces stage-appropriate decisions across all nine dimensions simultaneously.

## Sequence of application

Moore does not prescribe a strict order, but the practical sequence when a user brings a problem:

1. **Locate on the TALC / CMLC map.** Where is the category? Where is the specific product line?
2. **Diagnose the compelling reason to buy for the target segment.** Is it a real broken-process pain with quantified cost, or is it aspirational?
3. **Test the whole product against the compelling reason.** What's missing? Which pieces are partner-supplied vs. must-be-yours?
4. **Choose the beachhead segment (or the next bowling pin, or declare tornado readiness).** Apply the two-part test: big enough to matter, small enough to lead.
5. **Deploy the stage-appropriate playbook** across the 9-Point Checklist. Refuse to mix stages.
6. **Watch for stage transition signals.** The tornado tips when the pragmatist herd moves en masse; you'll know because horizontal deals close on features/price without the vertical whole product being pre-built. When that happens, reverse the playbook.

## What this method is NOT

Moore is emphatic about the negative definition. TALC is NOT:

- **A per-customer buying-stage funnel.** It's a *market*-adoption bell curve of populations.
- **A campaign timeline.** Stages don't have fixed durations; some categories chasm-cross in months, others in decades, some never.
- **An excuse for lifetime bespoke selling.** The whole point of Bowling Alley is that whole-product investment leverages across pins; if every deal is bespoke, you don't have a bowling alley, you have a service business.
- **Interchangeable with disruption theory.** Christensen asks why incumbents fail; Moore asks why startups can't move the mainstream. Different failure modes.
- **A one-time framework applied once at launch.** Categories mature, and the stage changes underneath you. Re-diagnose at every major planning cycle.
- **Only for tech companies.** Moore's PropTech interview (Memoori, Feb 2022) and 2022 Diginomica material extend the framework to hospitality, transportation, and real estate. The framework describes *discontinuous innovation adoption*, wherever it happens.

**Author's words on this:**
> "In the past, most of the technology disruption was actually happening inside the tech sector itself… Now we're seeing it in hospitality, transportation, and buildings." — Moore, [Memoori interview, Feb 3, 2022](https://memoori.com/are-we-crossing-the-proptech-chasm-geoffrey-moore-interview/)
