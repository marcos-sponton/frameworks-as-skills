# Crossing the Chasm — Applications

> When Moore's framework fits, when it doesn't, and what to reach for instead. Also: how *Crossing the Chasm* composes with adjacent frameworks in the strategy / GTM / positioning stack.

## Situations where the framework fits well

- **Discontinuous-innovation B2B products** (SaaS, AI/ML, dev tools, infrastructure, enterprise apps, deep tech) where adoption behavior differs qualitatively across customer segments. This is Moore's sweet spot — the framework was built for tech markets and still applies most crisply here.
- **Deciding "which vertical do we go after first?"** — beachhead selection is the operational core of the chasm-crossing play.
- **Diagnosing why traction with early adopters isn't converting into a repeatable mainstream motion** — the classic chasm symptom ("we have visionary logos but pragmatists won't buy").
- **Sequencing adjacent verticals** — bowling-alley discipline for reference-leveraged expansion after the beachhead is secured.
- **Deciding whether the market has entered a tornado** — standardize/verticalize question with major operational consequences.
- **Sizing a whole-product gap** — what integrations, services, partners, and references does a pragmatist need before they'll buy?
- **Building a compelling reason to buy for a pragmatist audience** — quantifying broken-process pain.
- **Applying Zone to Win for an incumbent managing a disruption** — Performance / Productivity / Incubation / Transformation.
- **Using the Hierarchy of Powers to diagnose growth-source problems** — which of the 5 powers is fading?
- **Positioning around the Digital Systems Maturity Model** — where is the enterprise customer on the stairway, and what's the next honest step?
- **Reasoning about where a specific AI use case sits on the adoption curve** — Moore's 2024–2026 essays are the anchor for this.
- **Post-chasm scaling discipline** — how to grow beyond the beachhead without prematurely trying to become a platform or reach for CFO-level buyers (see [After the Chasm—Scaling Beyond the Beachhead](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/after-the-chasm-scaling-beyond-the-beachhead/)).
- **Portfolio management of multiple product lines at different TALC stages** — Zone to Win is designed for exactly this.

## Situations where it does NOT fit

- **Pre-product/market fit.** TALC assumes a shippable innovation with some demonstrated adoption. Pre-PMF startups are still in search mode. Positioning them on TALC prematurely locks in the wrong frame. Reach for: JTBD, continuous discovery (Torres), lean startup, customer development.

- **Firm-level competitive advantage / defensibility (moats).** Moore describes *category-adoption dynamics*, not why one firm persistently earns above-average returns relative to competitors. That's Hamilton Helmer's altitude — reach for **7 Powers** (see `[[7-powers]]`).

- **Corporate-level "which markets should we play in at all?"** — the choice of *where to play* at company-portfolio level is Roger Martin's Playing to Win altitude — reach for `[[playing-to-win]]`. Chasm is downstream: once you've chosen a market, chasm-crossing tells you how to reach the mainstream inside it.

- **B2C brand identity / lifestyle branding.** Moore's framework is B2B / enterprise-tech-first. It has been adapted to consumer categories (Moore added a consumer-adoption appendix in the 3rd ed), but branding-first thinking (Byron Sharp, Marty Neumeier) owns the altitude Moore doesn't work in for consumer.

- **Positioning-statement craft for a specific product.** Chasm-crossing gives you the *stage-appropriate positioning strategy* (what to position against, which competitive frame, which audience psychographic); the *craft* of writing the actual positioning statement is April Dunford's altitude — reach for `[[obviously-awesome]]`. Dunford explicitly builds on Moore's segmentation ideas.

- **Late-stage growth-loop optimization** (product-market-model-channel fit tuning at scale). That's Brian Balfour's Four Fits altitude — reach for `[[four-fits]]`. Moore's TALC lens is upstream: it tells you *whether* you're at the stage where growth-loop optimization is even the right work.

- **Category-creation as bet-the-company move.** Moore's framework doesn't insist on category creation; Play Bigger does. If the user is committed to inventing a new category, Play Bigger is the operational tool; Moore's framework will still tell you *how* to cross the chasm once the category is defined.

- **Diagnosis of "what's actually wrong with our strategy?"** — Rumelt's kernel / crux is more useful when the primary question is diagnostic rather than staging. Reach for `[[good-strategy-bad-strategy]]`.

- **The user just wants a summary of *Crossing the Chasm*.** Give them the 3rd edition book link. Don't run the framework at them.

## How Crossing the Chasm composes with other frameworks

### Everett Rogers — *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962)

**Foundational.** Rogers is Moore's antecedent. Rogers defined the bell-curve segmentation (Innovators, Early Adopters, Early Majority, Late Majority, Laggards) and the S-curve of adoption. Moore's contribution:
- **The chasm** — a discontinuity between visionaries and pragmatists that Rogers did not name.
- **Stage-conditional playbooks** — Rogers described diffusion; Moore prescribed operator moves per stage.
- **Bowling Alley, Tornado, Main Street** — Moore's staged extensions.

Rule: attribute the segment names to Rogers, the chasm and playbooks to Moore.

### April Dunford — *Obviously Awesome* / *Sales Pitch* — see `[[obviously-awesome]]`

**Dunford builds on Moore.** Her book explicitly identifies that positioning change is often needed to cross the chasm, and treats her 5-component positioning method as the operational recipe Moore's framework calls for but doesn't fully specify.

- **Moore says *what* has to happen** — segment the market, choose a beachhead, ship the whole product, position for pragmatists.
- **Dunford says *how* to actually re-position** — the 5 components (competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value themes, best-fit customers, market category) executed as a cross-functional workshop.

If the user's problem is "we don't know what to call ourselves" and they've located themselves on TALC, hand the positioning-craft part to Dunford's method. Both operate on beachheads. Both are practitioner-first. Cross-link: `[[obviously-awesome]]`.

### Andy Raskin — Strategic Narrative — see `[[strategic-narrative]]`

**Complementary, not overlapping.** Raskin's "Name a Big Relevant Change → Winners & Losers → Promised Land → Magic Gifts → Evidence" is a narrative shell that maps naturally onto Moore's Compelling Reason to Buy for a pragmatist audience. If the user is building a keynote for a market moment (product launch event, customer conference, investor day) that reframes what the category is about, Raskin is the operative frame. If they're diagnosing where the market is on TALC, Moore is.

### Roger Martin — Playing to Win — see `[[playing-to-win]]`

**Compatible, upstream.** Martin's cascade (winning aspiration / where to play / how to win / capabilities / management systems) is a *making* framework at company altitude. Moore's TALC / CMLC is a *timing and staging* framework the cascade should be conditioned on. Where Martin's cascade says "where to play," Moore forces the answer to name a psychographic segment + a life-cycle stage. Use PtW to make the strategic choice; use Moore to operationalize the stage-appropriate playbook.

### Richard Rumelt — *Good Strategy Bad Strategy* / *The Crux* — see `[[good-strategy-bad-strategy]]`

**Compatible, upstream.** Rumelt is diagnosis-first (what's the pivotal challenge?). Moore is staging-first (where are we in the adoption arc, and what's the corresponding playbook?). Complementary: Rumelt tells you *what problem to solve*; Moore tells you *which playbook the market currently rewards*.

### Hamilton Helmer — *7 Powers* — see `[[7-powers]]`

**Different lens.** Helmer = *why does this business persist in earning above-average returns?* (moats). Moore = *how does this category evolve, and where is this business in its arc?* (adoption). 7 Powers is largely stage-agnostic; TALC is stage-explicit.

They compose well: Helmer's **Counter-Positioning** maps onto Moore's Bowling Alley / early-tornado plays where the incumbent can't respond without cannibalizing. Helmer's **Scale Economies** and **Network Economies** typically emerge in the Tornado phase. **Process Power** and **Branding** — Helmer's Hysteresis-barrier powers — usually solidify on Main Street. Use both when a session touches both moats and staging.

### Brian Balfour — Four Fits — see `[[four-fits]]`

**Downstream / later-stage.** Four Fits (product-market, market-model, model-channel, channel-product) is a *later-stage* growth-loop framework. Assumes you are past chasm and building the growth engine. Moore's TALC lens is upstream: are you even the right *type* of business for a growth-loop model at this stage? Pre-chasm, most growth-loop optimization is premature.

### Clayton Christensen — *The Innovator's Dilemma* / Disruption Theory

**Compatible, sibling framework.** Christensen: incumbents fail because rational resource-allocation systems reject low-margin disruptive tech. Moore: startups fail because they can't move the *pragmatist* mainstream, regardless of the incumbent's behavior. They address different failure modes in the same historical cases (steel mini-mills, disk drives) but from opposite sides of the market.

Moore's *Zone to Win* is the incumbent-side answer that partially overlaps with Christensen's autonomous-unit prescription — but Moore is more prescriptive about organizational metrics and funding. If the user is a startup, read Moore. If the user is an incumbent facing a disruptor, read both.

### Play Bigger — Category Design (Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, Maney, 2016)

**Adjacent, more assertive.** Play Bigger reframes Moore for the modern SaaS category-king era: same TALC bones, but the "category king" thesis (winner takes 76% of category economics) generalizes Moore's Gorilla concept. Moore treats category-creation as one path; Play Bigger insists it is the *only* path worth pursuing. Use Play Bigger when the user is committed to inventing a new category as a bet-the-company move.

### Ted Levitt — Marketing Myopia + generic/expected/augmented/potential product

**Foundational for Whole Product.** Moore's Whole Product concept is his adaptation of Levitt's product-layer model. Levitt's "people don't buy drills, they buy holes" is invoked directly in Moore's 2024 "customers buy outcomes" post.

### Roger Martin's *A New Way to Think* + Moore's Core vs. Context

Both frameworks push toward resource-allocation discipline that liberates high-differentiation work from commodity operations. Not overlapping — Martin's altitude is company-strategic; Moore's Core vs. Context is portfolio-operational. Compose well.

## Frameworks Moore has explicitly extended or resisted

**Extended (Moore builds on, credits, and adds):**
- **Rogers' Diffusion of Innovations** — Moore's chasm is the addition.
- **Levitt's whole product** — Moore's pragmatist-buying application.
- **Buffett's economic castles + moats** — Moore's Hierarchy of Powers is more granular.

**Resisted / warned against (Moore explicitly critiques):**
- **"Delight the customer"** as strategy — substitute for compelling reason. See ["Delighting your customers is bunk," 2024](https://geoffreyamoore.com/business_blogs/delighting-your-customers-is-bunk-delivering-on-their-desired-outcomes-is-not/).
- **Broad-front market entry** — "it's big, we could get some of that."
- **Pre-chasm platform ambition** — splits scarce whole-product investment.
- **Metricizing Incubation like Performance** — Zone to Win anti-pattern.
- **Transformation as rounding error** — Zone to Win anti-pattern.
- **"AI-first"** as generic positioning — trend confused with differentiation.
- **Operational excellence as competitive advantage** — table stakes, not Power.

If the user invokes any of these expecting them to work, redirect using Moore's stage-conditional discipline.

## Framework composition in a real session

A common realistic session pattern:

1. **User arrives with a symptom** ("we have great early customers but pipeline stalled").
2. **Locate them on TALC** — likely at the chasm.
3. **Diagnose the compelling reason to buy** for the pragmatist target — is the process-pain quantified?
4. **Assess the whole product** for the intended beachhead — what's missing?
5. **If positioning is unclear** → hand off to Dunford (`[[obviously-awesome]]`) with the beachhead segment as input.
6. **If the strategic choice of market is unclear** → back up to Martin (`[[playing-to-win]]`).
7. **If the moat question is live** → cross-lens with Helmer (`[[7-powers]]`).
8. **If they need a narrative shell for the pitch or a launch event** → hand off to Raskin (`[[strategic-narrative]]`).
9. **If they're incumbent and managing a disruptor** → apply Zone to Win.
10. **If they're mid-tornado and need to standardize** → apply the *Inside the Tornado* stage-reversal doctrine.

The point: Moore's framework is the **staging spine**. The other frameworks bolt on at specific stages to handle specific operational sub-problems.
