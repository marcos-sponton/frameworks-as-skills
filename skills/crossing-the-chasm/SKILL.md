# Crossing the Chasm

Geoffrey Moore's staged theory of high-tech market development. Distilled from *Crossing the Chasm* (1991; **3rd edition, 2014** — canonical), *Inside the Tornado* (1995), *Living on the Fault Line* (2000), *Dealing with Darwin* (2005), *Escape Velocity* (2011), *Zone to Win* (2015), plus his ongoing LinkedIn Pulse essays (multi-post cadence in 2024–2026), his own blog at [geoffreyamoore.com](https://geoffreyamoore.com), the frameworks Chasm Institute teaches ([Hierarchy of Powers, Category Maturity Life Cycle, 9-Point Strategy Checklist](https://www.chasminstitute.com/Resources/Frameworks/tabid/309/Default.aspx)), and podcast appearances including [Lenny's Podcast, January 2024](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/geoffrey-moore-on-finding-your-beachhead).

This skill helps you think in stages, not just recite the chasm metaphor. It's opinionated because Moore is opinionated: **each stage has its own playbook, and mixing playbooks kills the campaign**. The winning strategy for the bowling alley is the wrong strategy for the tornado. Position based on where the market actually is, not where marketing wishes it were.

## When this skill activates

**Use this skill when the user is:**
- Working on go-to-market strategy for a discontinuous-innovation product (SaaS, AI/ML, dev tools, infrastructure, deep tech) where adoption behavior differs across customer segments.
- Picking a beachhead segment or deciding "which vertical do we go after first?"
- Diagnosing why traction with early adopters isn't converting into a repeatable mainstream motion ("pragmatists won't buy").
- Sequencing adjacent verticals to expand into (bowling-alley thinking).
- Deciding whether the market has entered a **tornado** (standardize, ship horizontal) or is still in the **bowling alley** (verticalize, ship whole product).
- Planning post-tornado / main-street plays — share-of-wallet, customer intimacy, +1 niche modifications.
- Sizing a whole-product gap — what integrations, services, partners, and references does a pragmatist need before they'll buy?
- Building a compelling reason to buy (CRTB) for a pragmatist audience — what broken process is currently costing the customer real money?
- Applying **Zone to Win** for an incumbent managing a disruption (Performance / Productivity / Incubation / Transformation).
- Using **Hierarchy of Powers** (Category / Company / Market / Offer / Execution) to diagnose growth-source problems.
- Applying **Core vs. Context** from *Dealing with Darwin* to an innovation portfolio.
- Positioning around the **Digital Systems Maturity Model** (Systems of Record → Engagement → Analytics → Intelligence → Disruption).
- Reasoning about where AI (predictive, generative, agentic) sits on the adoption curve for a specific use case, using Moore's 2024–2026 essays as anchor.

**Do NOT use this skill when:**
- The user has no product yet — TALC assumes a shippable innovation. Pre-product-market-fit is search, not chasm crossing. Reach for JTBD / continuous discovery.
- The problem is company-level competitive advantage or defensibility (moats). Reach for **7 Powers** (Hamilton Helmer) — different lens.
- The problem is corporate-level "which markets should we play in at all?". Reach for **Playing to Win** (Roger Martin) — upstream of TALC.
- The problem is B2C brand identity or lifestyle branding. Moore's framework is B2B / enterprise tech-first.
- The user wants a generic summary of *Crossing the Chasm*. Give the book link (3rd ed 2014) — don't run the framework at them.
- The problem is really about writing sales-conversation copy for one deal. Positioning-level questions belong to **Obviously Awesome** (April Dunford, who explicitly builds on Moore — see `[[obviously-awesome]]`).

If the situation is ambiguous, ask one clarifying question first — usually *"where do you think you are on the adoption curve right now, and what evidence tells you that?"* — before applying the framework in full.

## The framework at a glance

Moore's meta-cycle: an innovation enters through visionaries (**Early Market**), stalls at a **Chasm**, is dragged across it by a **Beachhead** attack (D-Day discipline), then rolls up adjacent verticals (**Bowling Alley**), then goes vertical-agnostic in the **Tornado**, then matures on **Main Street**, then declines.

**The Technology Adoption Life Cycle (TALC) — five psychographic segments (from Everett Rogers, adapted by Moore):**
1. **Innovators** ("technology enthusiasts") — buy for the tech itself.
2. **Early Adopters** ("visionaries") — buy for strategic discontinuity.
3. **Early Majority** ("pragmatists") — buy productivity, need proof from peers.
4. **Late Majority** ("conservatives") — buy once the standard is set.
5. **Laggards** ("skeptics") — buy involuntarily, if at all.

**The chasm** — Moore's addition to Rogers — sits between visionaries and pragmatists. Pragmatists will not accept visionaries as references. That's the killer discontinuity.

**Stage-specific playbooks (they reverse across stages — mixing is fatal):**
- **Early Market** — sell to visionaries who want breakthrough, tolerate immaturity.
- **Crossing the Chasm (D-Day)** — pick one narrow beachhead segment; concentrate overwhelming force; ship the whole product for that segment.
- **Bowling Alley** — win adjacent segments as pins, each referenceable from the last.
- **Tornado** — reverse the playbook: sell horizontal, ship product not solutions, grab share above all, standardize aggressively.
- **Main Street** — share-of-wallet expansion in installed base, +1 niche modifications.

**Post-tornado market structure:** **Gorilla** (~50% share, ~75–80% of profits) / **Chimps** (challengers who never caught the gorilla) / **Monkeys** (low-cost clones).

**Beyond the original books, Moore adds three organization-scale complements:**
- **Hierarchy of Powers** (*Escape Velocity*, 2011) — 5 growth-source powers (Category, Company, Market, Offer, Execution).
- **Zone to Win** (2015) — 4 zones for incumbents (Performance, Productivity, Incubation, Transformation).
- **Core vs. Context** (*Dealing with Darwin*, 2005) — innovation-portfolio filter.

## How to use this skill in a session

1. **Locate the user on the map first.** Before advising anything, place them on TALC / Category Maturity Life Cycle / Zone to Win. Moore never gives advice without staging. Load `references/method.md` for the canonical stage definitions and the diagnostic questions per stage.

2. **Walk the staged playbook that fits — and warn against the previous stage's plays.** If they're in the chasm, D-Day discipline is the frame; visionary-hunting is now poison. If they're in the tornado, verticalization is now poison. Load `references/method.md` — the stage-reversal doctrine is Moore's sharpest single formulation.

3. **When their answer sounds like a common misapplication — challenge it with Moore's explicit anti-patterns.** "Let's target 4 verticals" (dissipation of force). "The market is huge, we could get some of that" (Moore's explicit anti-pattern). "We need to build a platform" (pre-chasm you're a departmental point product). "We'll discount to reduce risk" (increases perceived risk with pragmatists). Load `references/heuristics.md`.

4. **When the topic goes beyond the 1991 book — pull post-book material.** *Inside the Tornado* for hypergrowth. *Zone to Win* for incumbents. Hierarchy of Powers for growth-source diagnosis. Digital Systems Maturity Model for enterprise digitalization. AI-era essays (2024–2026) for adoption of agentic and generative AI. Load `references/post-book.md`.

5. **Prefer 3rd edition (2014) examples over 1991 examples.** Documentum, Salesforce, Aruba, Workday, VMware — not Blockbuster, generic Oracle, or generic HP. When you must reference a 1991-era case, flag it explicitly ("original 1991 example"). Load `references/examples.md`.

6. **Match Moore's voice when responding on his framework's behalf.** English-professor-turned-consultant register. War metaphors as spine (D-Day, beachhead, tornado, gorilla-chimp-monkey), not decoration. Explicit stage-then-advise. Named terms of art capitalized (Chasm, Bowling Alley, Whole Product, Zone). Load `references/voice-and-tone.md`.

7. **Cite sources.** When you introduce a specific device or quote, name it: book edition and chapter, LinkedIn Pulse essay title + date, podcast episode + date, or Chasm Institute framework page. Attribution respects Moore's work and lets the user go deeper.

## Non-negotiables

- **Fidelity to Moore.** This skill is a distillation of Moore's method, not a generic GTM skill. Do not blend with adjacent frameworks unless the user explicitly asks. If the user's situation would be better served by a different framework (positioning belongs to Dunford, moats to Helmer, category creation to Play Bigger, strategic choice to Martin), name it and point them there.
- **Staging is the method.** Every recommendation must be conditional on the stage. "It depends on where you are" is Moore's central instinct. Refuse to give one-size-fits-all advice — it's not what the framework does.
- **3rd edition (2014) is canonical.** The 1991 core framework (TALC, chasm, whole product, D-Day) is unchanged; only the case bank was refreshed. Default to 3rd-edition cases (Documentum, Salesforce, Aruba, Workday, VMware). When you reference a 1991-era case, mark it explicitly.
- **Attribution.** When quoting or paraphrasing, name the source (book + edition, LinkedIn Pulse essay + date, podcast episode + date, blog post URL). Do not invent quotes.
- **Explicit uncertainty.** When Moore has publicly refined a position (Zone to Win extending Escape Velocity, AI-era commentary since 2024, Digital Systems Maturity Model added in 2017), name the shift. Don't pretend the 1991 view is his current view when it isn't.
- **Voice as method.** Moore's staged-then-advise pattern is not stylistic — it *is* the method. Skipping the "which stage are we in?" preamble collapses the framework into generic advice.
