## Chase Manhattan bank robbery (Brooklyn, 1993) — the foundational case

**Where Voss uses it:** *Never Split the Difference* Ch. 1, MasterClass Introduction, Lex Fridman #364, most keynotes.

**The situation:** Voss's first hostage-negotiation case as an FBI negotiator. Two men had robbed a Chase Manhattan branch in Brooklyn and taken hostages. Standard doctrine at the time was "negotiate to buy time until tactical resolution." Voss and his mentor sat with the phone.

**The Black Swan:** one of the hostage-takers wanted out and was ashamed to say so directly. Ashamed of the crime, ashamed of the failure, ashamed of surrender. Voss's team, using mirroring and labels ("It sounds like this isn't what you signed up for" / "It seems like there's a lot going on inside there"), created the space where the hostage-taker could give up without losing face.

**How it resolved:** the first hostage-taker surrendered. This was, at the time, an unusual outcome — negotiations of that era were often expected to end tactically. The case became foundational for the FBI's shift toward emotion-based hostage negotiation.

**Why the case is instructive:** it establishes the core Voss thesis — negotiation is emotion-first, information-gathering-driven, and the hidden thing you don't know (the shame the counterpart won't admit) is usually the pivot. The tactic that surfaced the Black Swan was mirroring + labeling + silence.

## Jeff Schilling kidnapping (Philippines, 2000) — the "no ransom" negotiation

**Where Voss uses it:** *Never Split the Difference*, Diary of a CEO E147, keynotes.

**The situation:** American Jeff Schilling was kidnapped by the Abu Sayyaf Group in the Philippines. Standard playbook: the group demanded a large ransom. Standard operator move: negotiate the ransom down.

**What Voss did:** he treated the kidnappers as a group with internal dynamics, not a monolith. The Black Swans surfaced through patient discovery — the group was afraid of internal disagreement more than of the FBI. Different factions had different incentives.

**How it resolved:** Schilling was released without ransom payment. Voss's team exploited the internal dynamics of the group; the negotiation shifted from "how much money" to "how do we make the extremist within the group look weak internally."

**Why the case is instructive:** it's Voss's canonical proof that the surface question (how much do we pay?) is rarely the real negotiation. The real negotiation is about the counterpart's internal world — which Black Swans reveal.

**Translation for business:** when the counterpart is pushing hard on price, the real negotiation is rarely about price. Something else is going on (a boss's mandate, an internal politics problem, a career bonus, a fear of being blamed for a bad decision). Hunt for it.

## Bill Griffin conversion — the *"That's right"* moment

**Where Voss uses it:** *Never Split the Difference* Ch. 6, Lex Fridman #364.

**The situation:** Bill Griffin was a fugitive holed up with a hostage. Voss's team was negotiating. The conversation was going nowhere on the surface — Griffin wasn't budging on the demands.

**What Voss did:** rather than counter Griffin's demands or offer terms, Voss's team summarized Griffin's world back to him — his frustration, his sense of being trapped by circumstances, his reluctance to add to what he'd already done. The summary was accurate enough that Griffin exhaled: *"That's right."*

**How it resolved:** the *"That's right"* was the pivot. From that moment Griffin was open to being influenced; the standoff broke.

**Why the case is instructive:** it establishes *"That's right"* as the target signal of tactical empathy. Not agreement with a proposal — recognition of accurate understanding. The distinction between *"That's right"* (real) and *"You're right"* (dismissive) comes from this case.

**Translation for business:** when the counterpart is stuck, don't offer more. Summarize their world back to them so accurately that they exhale *"That's right."* From that moment forward you have leverage you didn't have before.

## The "one black, one brown shoe" — the anti-compromise frame

**Where Voss uses it:** *Never Split the Difference* introduction, most keynotes and podcasts.

**The situation:** Voss's wife wanted to compromise on which pair of shoes he wore out — she liked one, he liked another. Her proposal: *"Why don't you wear one of each?"*

**Why the story lands:** the compromise is worse than either point. One black and one brown shoe satisfies no one; it's not half of two good outcomes, it's a bad outcome dressed as fairness.

**The general lesson:** compromise-as-default deal-shape usually produces the worst outcome. It satisfies neither side, and it trains the counterpart to open with extreme numbers next time (they know you'll split).

**Translation for business:** when someone reaches for *"let's meet in the middle"*, the honest answer is often *"the middle doesn't work for either of us."* Ackerman with empathy to find a real deal shape; don't reach for the middle as the default.

## The salary Ackerman sequence — MasterClass Mock Negotiation

**Where Voss uses it:** MasterClass "Mock Negotiation: Salary."

**The situation:** you're negotiating a salary for a new role. Target: $150k. The employer opens by asking you what number you're looking for.

**The move:**

1. Do NOT anchor with a point. Use a **range**: *"I was thinking somewhere in the $175–195k range, based on what similar roles are paying and the scope of this one."* (Anchored 15–20% above target.)

2. Employer counters at $130k.

3. You empathize + calibrated question: *"How did you get to that number? Help me understand the constraints on your side."* (Do NOT counter immediately.)

4. Employer explains — budget line, comp bands, comparable hires. You listen. Mirror the constraint that seems most alive.

5. Label: *"It sounds like the comp band is the tight constraint more than the budget."*

6. Employer confirms — or reveals a Black Swan (equity, sign-on, bonus structure, comp-band exception process).

7. You use the Black Swan. Reformulate your ask: *"How would we make $165k base work within the band? Would it help if we sequenced it — $150k now, $165k at the six-month review?"*

8. If they hold at $130k, deploy forced empathy: *"How am I supposed to accept $130k when comparable roles are paying $160k+?"* (Curious tone, not aggressive. Wait.)

9. If they move to $145k, empathize + hold. Then land on a non-round number with a non-monetary throw-in: *"I can start at $147,500 if we lock in the six-month review to $162,500, and I get five days of PTO on top of the standard package."*

**Why the case is instructive:** it shows the range play + calibrated question + labeling + Black Swan hunt + forced empathy + non-round-final + non-monetary throw-in all in one sequence. Not all of it always deploys, but the shape is the shape.

## The fee-defense sequence — synthetic, Full-Fee-Agent-flavored

**Source:** synthesis of *The Full Fee Agent* (Voss + Shull, 2022) applied to any professional-services operator.

**The situation:** you're a consultant / agency / freelancer / founder-CEO. A prospective client says: *"We like your proposal, but the fee is 30% higher than the other proposals we're considering. Can you come down?"*

**The default (bad) move:** offer 15% off "as a compromise." Split the difference.

**Why the default fails:** you've trained the client that your fee is negotiable by default. You've signaled that you doubt your own worth. You've discounted the value of everything after this deal (renewals, referrals, future scope). And the client STILL might not close — because the discount conversation is often about their fear of overpaying, not about the actual number.

**The Voss sequence:**

1. **Late-night FM DJ voice.** Slow down. Don't respond immediately.

2. **Accusation audit:** *"You're probably thinking my fee is high compared to what you've seen. You may think I'm not accounting for the constraints you have. And you may feel I'm not respecting the fact that you've done the work of getting comparable proposals."*

3. **Label the real concern:** *"It sounds like the concern isn't the total number, so much as making sure the number is defensible to whoever else is looking at this decision."*

4. **Silence.** Let them respond. Often they reveal the Black Swan — the internal budget approver, the comparable proposals that are lower-scope, the pressure from above to negotiate.

5. **Forced empathy:** *"How am I supposed to deliver the scope we scoped for a lower fee, without changing what we deliver? Help me understand what would work on your end."*

6. **Wait.** Do not fill the silence with softening.

7. **Options that hold the fee:** if they push back, offer non-monetary adjustments that hold the number: reduce scope by one workstream (so the per-unit rate is unchanged), phase the payment, add a small extra deliverable they'd value.

8. **No-oriented walk-away:** *"Have you given up on working together at the fee I've proposed?"*

**Why the case is instructive:** it shows the method deployed to *hold* rather than to *bargain down*. The Full Fee Agent generalizes to any professional operator. The load-bearing move is *not* the negotiation itself — it's the discipline of not reflex-discounting in the first place.

## The ultimatum-response sequence — synthetic

**Source:** synthesis of Voss's teachings on responding to ultimatums (podcasts, MasterClass Bending Reality).

**The situation:** the counterpart says *"That's my final offer. Take it or leave it. I need an answer by Friday."*

**The default (bad) move:** either accept, or counter with what you actually want (which they'll reject, so the ultimatum stands).

**The Voss sequence:**

1. **Do not respond in real time to the ultimatum.** Late-night FM DJ voice. Pause.

2. **Mirror:** *"Take it or leave it?"* (upward inflection, curious tone, silence)

3. Wait. Often the counterpart will elaborate — soften the ultimatum, explain the pressure they're under, or reveal the Black Swan (their real deadline, their internal politics, the constraint driving the ultimatum).

4. **Label:** *"It sounds like there's pressure on your end to close this by Friday."*

5. **Test the deadline:** *"What happens if we don't hit Friday?"* (Genuinely curious. Wait for a specific consequence. Nine times out of ten the counterpart cannot articulate one.)

6. **Reject the ultimatum's frame without rejecting the counterpart:** *"I want to find a deal that works for both of us. Take-it-or-leave-it doesn't give me anything to work with. Help me understand what's driving the frame."*

7. **Calibrated question:** *"How can we solve this problem so it works on both sides?"*

**Why the case is instructive:** ultimatums are almost always leverage moves, not real constraints. The response is to not accept the frame — mirror, label, test the deadline, invite discovery. The counterpart who issued the ultimatum often backs off within a few exchanges.

## The Black Swan hunt sequence — synthetic

**Source:** synthesis of *Never Split the Difference* Ch. 10 + MasterClass "Black Swans."

**The situation:** a deal that was moving has gone silent. You've done the normal follow-ups. The counterpart hasn't responded to your last three messages. You suspect there's something you don't know.

**The move:**

1. Assume 3+ Black Swans exist. List what you *don't* know:
   - Has the decision-maker changed?
   - Has an internal politics dynamic shifted?
   - Is there a competitor you don't know about?
   - Has a career/comp event on their side changed priorities?
   - Is there a legal / compliance blocker they haven't named?
   - Has the counterpart been overruled?
   - Is there a fear of being blamed for the wrong choice?

2. Reach out with a no-oriented question that invites discovery: *"Have you given up on this project?"* / *"Is now a bad time to be talking about this?"*

3. If they respond, mirror + label whatever they say. Silence.

4. Ask a calibrated question specifically about the hidden dynamic: *"What's changed on your side since we last talked?"* / *"What's making this hard to close right now?"*

5. Do NOT reach for urgency, discount, or "checking in" language. Those amplify the counterpart's fear (see [[challenger-sale]] on FOMU-beats-FOMO).

6. If face-to-face becomes possible, prioritize it. Black Swans surface in unstructured moments — coffee, walking to the parking lot, the throwaway comment during a break.

**Why the case is instructive:** stalled deals are almost always Black-Swan-driven. The default reflex (send more content, offer a discount, add urgency) makes the situation worse. The Voss response is patience + discovery + no-oriented questions to invite the hidden thing into the room.

## The hard conversation prep — synthetic accusation audit walkthrough

**Source:** synthesis of MasterClass "The Accusations Audit."

**The situation:** you have to tell a long-standing client that you're raising your rates 40%. They've been with you three years. They will react badly.

**The default (bad) move:** lead with justification (*"our costs have gone up, we've added capabilities, the market rate is now higher…"*). The client hears defensiveness and pushes back harder.

**The Voss sequence:**

1. Draft the accusation audit. Write down every accusation the client could make. Don't self-edit. Some examples:
   - *"You're being greedy after everything I've paid you."*
   - *"You're not respecting our loyalty."*
   - *"You're pricing us out because you have bigger clients now."*
   - *"You're testing to see if I'll just accept it."*
   - *"You should have told me this was coming, not sprung it."*
   - *"This is going to make me look bad internally."*

2. Prune the list to the 4–5 accusations you believe the client is *actually* going to think.

3. Open the conversation with them, named up front, before you deliver the news:
   > *"[Client], I need to have a conversation with you today that I've been thinking about for a while. You're going to think I'm being greedy. You may feel I'm not respecting the three years we've worked together. You may feel I should have told you this was coming months ago, not sprung it. This is going to be uncomfortable to hear, and it's going to be complicated to explain internally on your side."*

4. **Pause.**

5. Then deliver: *"I'm raising my rates 40% for the next engagement. Here's why, and here's how I'd like to work it out with you."*

6. Now — the counterpart's emotional response is already largely defused. The accusations you named cannot be wielded, because you named them first. What remains is the business conversation.

7. Continue with mirroring + labeling + calibrated questions.

**Why the case is instructive:** the accusation audit inverts the default. Instead of trying to justify the ask (which invites push-back), you name every reason the counterpart has to be upset before you make it. It costs nothing and it removes most of the emotional charge.
