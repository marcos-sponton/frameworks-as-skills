## Netflix — the canonical pedagogical example

**Where Torres uses it:** across essays, courses, and podcast appearances — recurring anchor for the story-based interview technique.

**The situation:** Netflix as a universally-shared product-behavior example. Not a client case; the everyday product Torres uses to make interview mechanics concrete.

**How Torres uses the example:**
- **The opening question:** *"Tell me about the last time you watched Netflix."* — this exact form.
- **Set the scene:** where were you, what device, who was with you, what were you doing before?
- **Build the timeline:** how did you decide to open Netflix, what did you look for, what did you pick, what happened during the show, what happened after?
- **Redirect generalizations:** when the participant says "and I usually browse for a while before picking", pull them back: "but this specific time — what did you actually pick?"

**Why the example is instructive:** Netflix is universal enough that anyone can play both roles (interviewer and participant) in a practice exercise. The mechanics of the technique become visible without domain-specific complexity in the way.

## Interview Coach — Torres's own internal case

**Where Torres uses it:** [Product Talk essay, 2025 — How I Designed & Implemented Evals for Product Talk's Interview Coach](https://www.producttalk.org/interview-coach-evals/).

**The situation:** Torres and the Product Talk team built the Interview Coach — an AI-powered feedback tool that grades student practice interviews across four teaching dimensions (opening with story-based question, setting the scene, building the timeline, redirecting generalizations).

**How Torres uses the case:**
- Demonstrates AI + product built by her own team using her own method.
- Reveals the operational rubric she uses for interview quality — the four dimensions are directly reusable.
- Live demonstration of AI evals as a discovery discipline:
  > "Evals are an essential aspect of AI tool development that help you answer the question of whether your product is any good."
- Shows the trio doing AI product work:
  > "Your engineer [is] writing code, but your product manager and your designer are probably going to be involved in prompt design and even eval design."

**Why the case is instructive:** it's Torres eating her own dog food. Also the source of the interview rubric the skill can use to coach a user on their own interview technique.

## Product in Practice — anonymized team journeys

**Where Torres uses it:** the [Product in Practice series](https://www.producttalk.org/2023/08/learning-to-interview-continuously/) on Product Talk — real teams' adoption journeys, told with the team's permission.

**Sample piece:** *Learning to Interview Continuously* (Aug 2023) — a real team's journey from zero interview cadence to weekly practice, with the anti-patterns they hit along the way.

**How Torres uses these:**

**Why this matters for the skill:** when a user's own situation feels too specific for a public case, ground the diagnosis in "this pattern shows up across the teams in the Product in Practice series" — you're not inventing; you're pointing to a documented pattern.

## Anonymized composites — from the Product Talk Academy

**Where Torres uses it:** across her coaching, courses, and podcast episodes. She names patterns, not specific companies, when the material comes from her academy or coaching engagements.

**How Torres uses these:** the anti-pattern examples — the tree that became a poster, the team that reverted to project-based discovery after 6 weeks, the trio where the PM interviewed alone every week, the "opportunity" that was really a feature — are drawn from composite student and client experience across 16,000+ students and hundreds of coached teams.

**Why this matters for the skill:** when the user's own situation is unique, ground the diagnosis in "this pattern is common across the 16,000+ students in the Product Talk Academy" — you're pointing to the anonymized composite Torres references.
