# The Fearless Organization — Heuristics, Do's, Don'ts, Gotchas

> The practical devices — Edmondson's operational rules — that separate applying the Fearless Organization framework well from producing the caricature she has spent seven years pushing back against. The accountability axis is load-bearing; the "PS is not being nice" guard runs through this whole file.

## The single non-negotiable — "Psychological safety is not being nice"

**This is the biggest and most common misread.** Anyone applying the framework must be prepared to name it immediately. Concrete signs the user has fallen into it:

- Talking about PS as "creating a safe space" without mentioning accountability, standards, or performance.
- Framing hard feedback as a violation of PS.
- Using PS to justify avoiding conflict or a hard conversation.
- Treating PS as an HR / wellness / DEI initiative rather than a learning-and-performance one.
- Presenting the concept without the 2×2 with accountability.
- Announcing "we have psychological safety here" as a culture-marketing claim.
- Describing a team as "psychologically safe" when nothing hard is being asked of them.

**Correct move:** always pair PS with accountability. Always show the 2×2. Always name the Comfort Zone (high PS + low standards) as the exact failure mode this misread produces. Route back to the Learning Zone (high PS + high standards) as the target.

Edmondson's verbatim rebuttal:

> "Psychological safety is not about being nice. It's about giving candid feedback, openly admitting mistakes, and learning from each other."

> "Holding people accountable for great results and psychological safety are not a trade-off. They are two separate dimensions."
> — TEDxHGSE, 2014

## Do's

### Frame the work as complex and interdependent BEFORE asking for voice

Voice doesn't happen because you gave permission. It happens because the leader named the work as requiring voice. The Framing move is the first leader move for a reason. Without framing, "any thoughts?" invites nothing.

**Concrete script:** *"This is a project with a lot of moving parts, some of which we've never done before. I'm going to depend on all of you to flag things I'm not seeing. If something looks off, I need you to say so — I'd rather find out here than in production."*

### Practice situational humility explicitly

Say "we've never been here before" or "I don't have the answer" publicly and specifically. The invitation is only credible if the leader models the vulnerability first.

**Author's words:**
> "The best examples of crisis leadership involve what I call 'situational humility' — the ability to say, 'we've never been here before,' and then framing the problem as an opportunity to find solutions."

### Ask specific questions, not generic ones

Specificity signals expectation.

| Generic (weak) | Specific (strong) |
|---|---|
| "Any thoughts?" | "What's the strongest argument against this?" |
| "Any concerns?" | "Whose customers would push back hardest and why?" |
| "Everyone good?" | "If this fails in 6 months, what will the failure reason be?" |
| "Anything to add?" | "What am I missing that will bite us later?" |
| "Any feedback?" | "What's one thing I did in this meeting that made it harder for you to contribute?" |

### Diagnose the failure type BEFORE responding to any failure

Basic / Complex / Intelligent. The response differs. Treating all failures the same is blame culture in a lab coat.

- **Basic** (preventable, known territory, single cause) → checklists, training, standardization. Not learning workshops.
- **Complex** (familiar territory, many interacting causes) → system redesign, defense-in-depth. Not "who screwed up?"
- **Intelligent** (novel territory, hypothesis-driven, opportunity-motivated, small) → celebrate the learning. Extract the insight. Run the next experiment.

### Sanction clear violations — publicly and predictably

PS ≠ tolerance for everything. Edmondson is emphatic: bullies, harassers, and clear code-of-conduct violators must face consequences. **Sanctioning them reinforces PS; failing to sanction them destroys it.**

This is the exact opposite of the "PS as being nice" misread. Fearless orgs are the ones where bad behavior gets consequences, because that's how safety is maintained for the people trying to do the work honestly.

### Reward messengers, not the message

People bring you bad news because they trust you'll handle it. Fire the messenger once and the whole org learns to hide.

**Concrete move:** when someone brings you bad news, before you respond to the content, respond to the act. *"Thank you for telling me — that took courage and I needed to know."* Then engage the content.

### Distinguish voice from decision-making

PS invites voice. It does not require consensus. Leaders still decide. Voice without decision is committee-death; decision without voice is Anxiety Zone.

**Rule:** decide with voice, not by voice. The leader hears all inputs, weighs, decides, and — critically — explains the reasoning to the people whose input didn't carry the day. (Kim Scott calls this "Persuade" in the GSD wheel; the mechanism is the same.)

### Measure PS at the team level, not the individual or organization level

Same person in Team A speaks up freely, in Team B goes silent. That's PS, not personality. The Fearless Organization Scan operates at the team level for this reason.

Org-wide "engagement scores" don't measure PS. They measure org-wide averages, which hide the team-by-team variation where PS actually lives.

### Run blameless post-mortems that name Basic / Complex / Intelligent

"Blameless" is not "no analysis." A good post-mortem:

1. What actually happened? (facts)
2. What failure type was this? (Basic / Complex / Intelligent — cite the taxonomy)
3. What system or process change prevents the next one? (specific commitment, owner, deadline)
4. What is the learning we take forward? (what changes in how we work)

**If step 2 is missed**, blameless post-mortems can slide into "these things happen" — which lets Basic failures go without the checklist fix they need.

### Reward the surfacing of near-misses, not just failures

Complex failure prevention lives in near-miss reporting. The team that reports 10 near-misses per quarter is running a defense-in-depth system; the team that reports 0 has either perfect execution or a suppression problem, and the odds heavily favor the second.

### Distinguish "critique of the work" from "critique of the person"

Same principle as Kim Scott's HHIPP's "not personalized." Critique the design decision, the code choice, the pitch — support the person. This is what Pixar's Braintrust runs on and what makes brutal creative feedback survivable.

## Don'ts

### Don't present psychological safety without the accountability axis

**Red flag pattern:** any slide, memo, or talk that shows PS as a single scale, or as a warm value, without pairing it with high accountability / high standards.

The 2×2 is the anti-misread structural device. Present PS alone and the reader defaults to "safe space" — the exact caricature that produces the Comfort Zone.

### Don't punish intelligent failures

The single fastest way to kill learning. If your team ran a small, hypothesis-driven experiment in novel territory and it didn't work — that is exactly the failure you want more of.

Edmondson's rule: **celebrate the process, interrogate what was learned, move on.** The penalty for punishing intelligent failure isn't just that one experiment — it's that the team never runs another one.

### Don't treat all failures the same

Blame culture treats Basic, Complex, and Intelligent failures identically — as things to punish. Edmondson's counter: the leader's first job is to diagnose the failure type, then respond appropriately.

The mistake most leaders make: they overweight Basic and Complex thinking ("who screwed up?") and underweight Intelligent thinking ("what did we learn?"). Result: 80% of failures get treated as blameworthy when only 2-5% actually are.

### Don't mistake silence for agreement

Silence is data. It means voice isn't happening. In a low-PS org, the loudest teams look aligned because the quiet ones have given up. The room where "everyone's on board" is often the room where dissent has been priced out.

### Don't announce "we have psychological safety here"

As with Radical Candor, announcement is a red flag. PS is measured by whether people actually speak up — not by whether leaders claim they can.

If a leader has to say "we have PS here" to get people to speak up, they don't have PS. The behavior IS the diagnostic.

### Don't build PS as a one-off intervention

No offsite fixes this. No PS training workshop fixes this. PS is a sustained condition maintained by leader behavior every week. Treating it as an event (rather than a practice) is a category error.

### Don't scale PS by exhortation

"Everyone should feel safe to speak up" is a poster, not a practice. PS scales by leader behavior + structures + consequences — not by values statements.

The org that puts "psychological safety" on its values page but promotes bullies has just told every employee that the value is decoration.

### Don't confuse psychological safety with radical transparency

PS is about voice without retribution. Not about mandatory disclosure of everything to everyone. Bridgewater-style radical transparency without leader PS practices can produce the Anxiety Zone at scale — surveillance masquerading as candor. Edmondson has been careful not to conflate the two; the skill should be too.

### Don't confuse PS with the absence of pressure

High PS + high accountability is Learning Zone. The pressure is real; the fear of retribution is not. These are different variables. Removing pressure doesn't build PS; it produces the Comfort Zone.

### Don't treat "we don't have time for psychological safety" as a valid claim

Same failure mode Kim Scott names about feedback. The compounding cost of unspoken concerns dwarfs the time cost of soliciting them. Columbia is Exhibit A. Boeing 737 MAX is Exhibit B.

### Don't use "we're a blameless culture" as cover for skipping the analysis

Blameless does not mean unanalyzed. Basic failures need process fixes. Complex failures need system redesign. Both require honest diagnosis. "Nobody's fault" without follow-through is Apathy Zone.

## Gotchas (things that go wrong even when you think you're doing it right)

### The Comfort Zone trap

High PS + low standards. People love working there. Nothing ships. Diagnostic: if the retro is warm and nothing changes, you're in the Comfort Zone. **The fix is not less PS — it's more accountability.** Both must be high.

### The Anxiety Zone masquerading as high performance

Companies that appear to hit targets while dissent is being suppressed. Boeing 737 MAX. Wells Fargo. Volkswagen. The numbers look great until the crash. The tell: everyone's a top performer, no one's raising concerns, and the leader thinks the org is a well-oiled machine.

### Google Aristotle as license to skip the work

Everyone cites the Aristotle project as proof PS matters. Fewer people do the work of building it. Naming the study is not the same as running Framing / Situational Humility / Curiosity / Structures / Responding Productively as leader practices every week.

**Additional note:** the Aristotle project validated Edmondson's earlier work; it wasn't the origin. Credit Edmondson (or her 1999 ASQ paper) as the source, not Google.

### The "we don't have time for psychological safety" org

Same failure mode Kim Scott names about feedback. The compounding cost of unspoken concerns dwarfs the time cost of soliciting them. Every "we don't have time" org is burning silent risk that will eventually show up as a Boeing / Wells Fargo / Volkswagen event.

### Treating PS as a fixed trait rather than a team-level condition

"Some people just don't speak up" is often a diagnosis of the team, not the person. Same individual in a high-PS team engages; in a low-PS team goes silent. Recruiting for "assertiveness" doesn't fix a low-PS team — it burns out the assertive people.

### Applying PS across cultures without calibration

The axes (PS, accountability) are universal. The calibration of what "voice" looks like varies. In a high-power-distance culture, voice may be raised through a trusted intermediary, in writing, or in a small group before the large one. In a low-power-distance culture, voice may be raised directly and publicly. **Adjust calibration; don't drop axes.**

### The "blameless post-mortem" that never yields a change

Blameless does not mean unchanged. If a post-mortem produces no process change, no system redesign, and no owner-accountable follow-up, it's a therapy session — not learning. The blameless norm exists to enable analysis, not to prevent it.

### Framing PS as a DEI initiative

PS is a learning-and-performance framework that has beneficial DEI side-effects. Framing it primarily as DEI can (a) narrow its adoption to the DEI team, (b) trigger DEI-fatigue backlash, (c) obscure the operational cost of low PS (which is delivery risk, not just wellbeing). Frame it as learning and performance; the inclusion benefits follow.

### The "psychological safety survey" that becomes theater

Running the Fearless Organization Scan (or any PS survey) once, discussing the results, and doing nothing structural is worse than not running it. Teams learn that leadership went through the motions. Run the survey; publish the results; commit to specific changes; measure again; iterate. Otherwise skip it.

### Confusing the intelligent failure with the excuse

"That was an intelligent failure" is not a license to reclassify any embarrassing screw-up. The tests are strict: novel territory, opportunity-driven, hypothesis-based, as small as possible. A basic failure being rebranded as intelligent to avoid the checklist fix is exactly the misuse of the taxonomy that makes it useless.

## Pro tips

### The Basic-Complex-Intelligent question at the top of every failure discussion

Before any post-mortem, incident review, or retro — ask: "which type of failure are we looking at?" This forces the diagnosis to happen before the emotional reaction. Basic → what checklist do we need? Complex → what system change? Intelligent → what did we learn?

### The "one specific question per meeting" discipline

At the end of every decision meeting, the leader asks one specific proactive-inquiry question, and waits. Not "any thoughts?" — one specific question that requires a specific answer. Over months, the team learns that voice is expected and specific.

### The "what am I missing?" close

End 1:1s with *"What am I missing? What's not getting to me?"* Explicit invitation for the report to surface the things they're worried about telling you. The specificity of "not getting to me" is the tell — it acknowledges that the leader knows they're not seeing everything.

### The intelligent-failure celebration ritual

Some teams run a monthly ritual where someone presents an intelligent failure — what they tried, what they learned, what they'd do next. Normalizes the pattern. Kills the "failures are for review meetings, not celebration" default.

### The near-miss surfacing prompt

In post-mortems and retros: *"What almost went wrong that we caught in time? What was the concern raised that turned out to matter?"* Named recognition of near-misses builds the muscle for surfacing them earlier.

### Ask a specific person, not the room

When you know one person on the team has the most-relevant expertise or the strongest dissenting view — ask them by name. "Priya, you've done more of this kind of migration than anyone here — what's the concern that we're not weighting enough?" Puts the ball in a specific court; kills the diffusion-of-responsibility default.

### The Framing check before any hard project

Before starting an initiative, ask yourself: have I explicitly framed this as (a) interdependent, (b) uncertain, (c) requiring voice? If not — write and share a Framing note before the kickoff. 200 words. It reshapes the whole first meeting.

## Anti-patterns (the "bad management" Edmondson explicitly names)

### The Comfort Zone team

**What it looks like:** warm retros, "great job everyone," high engagement scores, low delivery pace, nothing changes. Leader thinks the team is in the Learning Zone because everyone is happy.
**Why it fails:** high PS without accountability. No hard work is being asked, so no learning is happening.
**How to redirect:** raise the standards. Set specific, ambitious targets. Ask specific proactive-inquiry questions in every meeting. If the team pushes back that "you're breaking the safety" — name the misread. Learning Zone requires both.

### The Anxiety Zone team

**What it looks like:** hits every deadline, hits every metric, has a reputation for excellence, nobody speaks up, top performers are quietly leaving, whistleblowers are punished.
**Why it fails:** low PS with high standards. Compliance without learning. The next Boeing / Wells Fargo / Volkswagen event is already in motion.
**How to redirect:** the leader has to make the first move — public situational humility, real sanction of any recent retaliation, structural changes to how concerns get raised. Words alone won't do it; the team is watching behavior.

### The Apathy Zone team

**What it looks like:** meetings happen, nothing meaningful gets said or done, leadership is disengaged, standards have quietly slid.
**Why it fails:** low PS + low standards. Both need rebuilding.
**How to redirect:** usually requires leadership change or structural intervention. This is the hardest zone to escape without a shock.

### The "blameless culture" org that never learns from failure

**What it looks like:** "these things happen" as the retrospective conclusion. Post-mortems that don't produce process changes. Basic failures repeating because no one wanted to run the checklist workshop.
**Why it fails:** blameless got misread as unanalyzed. PS became cover for not doing the work of diagnosis.
**How to redirect:** blameless does not mean unanalyzed. Introduce the Basic / Complex / Intelligent taxonomy. Every failure gets typed; typed failures get the response the type requires.

### The "we have PS on the values page" org

**What it looks like:** the value is displayed. Leaders quote Edmondson in speeches. Bullies get promoted anyway. Concerns raised are met with defensiveness or retaliation.
**Why it fails:** PS as slogan without practice. The gap between claim and behavior is visible to everyone in the org.
**How to redirect:** audit specific practices — how are near-misses handled? Who has been sanctioned for retaliation? Who has been promoted despite known bullying? If the practices don't match the claim, the claim is the problem. Fix the practices or remove the claim.

### Promoting the "high performer who's an asshole"

Similar failure mode to Kim Scott's "brilliant jerk" case. In Edmondson's frame: the brilliant jerk destroys PS for the whole team, and the team's collective output — which depends on PS — drops far more than the jerk's individual output justifies.

Kim's rule ("Do not promote bullies. It's that simple.") is Edmondson's rule too. Sanctioning the bully reinforces PS for everyone else.

## Language and vocabulary — say this, not that

Small phrasing shifts Edmondson has made explicit across her work:

| Instead of | Use | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Safe space | Psychological safety + accountability | "Safe space" collapses into Comfort Zone; the paired framing keeps standards in view |
| "Everyone gets a voice" | "Voice is required — here's what we need to hear about" | Vague invitations get vague responses |
| "Any thoughts?" | Specific proactive-inquiry question | Specificity signals expectation |
| "Blameless post-mortem" | Blameless analysis with Basic/Complex/Intelligent typing | Blameless does not mean unanalyzed |
| "That was a failure" | "That was a [Basic/Complex/Intelligent] failure" | The type determines the response |
| "We need more psychological safety" | "We need to get to the Learning Zone — high PS AND high standards" | Prevents the PS-in-isolation misread |
| "We're a psychologically safe culture" | "Here's how we build PS in each team, and how we measure it" | PS is a team-level practice, not an org-wide claim |
| "Failure is not an option" | "Basic failure is not an option; intelligent failure is required" | The blanket rejection of failure kills learning |
| "We hold people accountable" | "We pair high accountability with high psychological safety" | The 2×2 with accountability keeps both dimensions visible |
| "We're not going to punish anyone" | "We're not going to punish messengers; we will sanction misconduct" | Distinguishes voice from bad behavior |
| "Speak up if you have concerns" | Framing note + situational humility + specific inquiry + structure | The invitation only works if the leader makes the four moves |
| "Anyone can push back" | "Priya, what's the concern we're not weighting enough?" | Diffuse invitations diffuse response |
| "Google Aristotle proved PS matters" | "Edmondson's research (and Google Aristotle's replication) proves PS matters" | Correct attribution — Aristotle validated existing work |

## The misread guard (this file's non-negotiable)

If a user comes to this skill invoking psychological safety as a reason to:

- Avoid a hard conversation.
- Lower a standard.
- Cancel accountability.
- Wrap a team in cotton wool.
- Treat every failure as a learning opportunity without diagnosis.
- Skip the sanction of a bully or code-of-conduct violator.

Then:

- Do NOT validate the framing.
- Name the misread specifically. Route them to the 2×2 with accountability.
- Ask which zone they're actually in. If Comfort Zone, name it. If they're describing PS without accountability, name the missing axis.
- Route them to the Learning Zone (high PS + high standards, both required).
- Cite Edmondson's own rebuttals — the "PS is not about being nice" quote and the "PS ≠ anything goes" essay are the canonical references.

Edmondson would.
