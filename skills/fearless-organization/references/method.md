# The Fearless Organization — Method

> The canonical description of Amy Edmondson's method in her own terms. Fidelity is the point — the framework's power comes from the load-bearing role of the accountability axis in the 2×2, and presenting psychological safety in isolation collapses the whole method into the "PS as being nice" misread that Edmondson has spent seven years pushing back against.

## Definition

Edmondson's canonical definition:

> "Psychological safety is a belief that one will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes."
> — Amy Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*, 2018

Short form, used across her TED talks and interviews:

> "The assurance that one can speak up, offer ideas, point out problems, or deliver bad news without fear of retribution."

The anchor concept: **interpersonal risk-taking.** Psychological safety is the belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risks — asking a naive question, admitting you don't know, disagreeing with the senior person in the room, flagging a mistake. It is NOT physical safety, emotional safety in a therapeutic sense, or being liked. It is a specific belief about a specific class of behaviors in a specific team.

## What psychological safety is NOT

Edmondson defines by negation as often as by affirmation, because the misread is so common. Verbatim:

> "Psychological safety is not about being nice. It's about giving candid feedback, openly admitting mistakes, and learning from each other."

> "Psychological safety ≠ anything goes."
> — [amycedmondson.com blog post title](https://amycedmondson.com/psychological-safety-%E2%89%A0-anything-goes/)

Explicitly NOT:
- **Being nice.** The single biggest misread. PS enables candid feedback; it does not replace it.
- **Lowering standards.** PS pairs with high accountability. Both must be high.
- **"Wrapping teams in cotton wool"** (Dan Cable's phrase, quoted approvingly by Edmondson).
- **Comfort.** Comfort is the Comfort Zone (see 2×2 below), which is a failure mode, not the goal.
- **Consensus or unanimity.** PS invites voice; leaders still decide.
- **Avoiding conflict.** PS makes productive conflict possible; it doesn't eliminate it.
- **A wellness or HR initiative.** PS is a learning-and-performance initiative that happens to have wellbeing side-effects.
- **A personality trait.** PS is a team-level condition. Same person in Team A speaks up, in Team B goes silent.
- **An org-wide slogan.** PS lives at the team level. "We are a psychologically safe organization" is a claim; it's tested one team at a time.
- **A shield for misconduct.** Bullies, harassers, and code-of-conduct violators must be sanctioned. Sanctioning them reinforces PS; failing to sanction them destroys it.

## The 2×2 with Accountability — the anti-misread structural device

**The single most important structural refinement Edmondson has added post-book. Never present psychological safety without this 2×2. Always name all four zones.**

|  | High Standards / Accountability | Low Standards / Accountability |
|---|---|---|
| **High Psychological Safety** | **Learning Zone** | **Comfort Zone** |
| **Low Psychological Safety** | **Anxiety Zone** | **Apathy Zone** |

### Learning Zone (top-right) — the goal

High PS + high standards. People take interpersonal risks in service of demanding work. Questions get asked. Bad news gets surfaced early. Intelligent failures happen and get learned from. Standards stay high because the team is honest about what's not working. This is where teams learn, innovate, and prevent avoidable failures.

**Author's words:**
> "I want you to be high, high in the learning zone."
> — TEDxHGSE, 2014

### Comfort Zone (top-left) — the "PS as being nice" misread lived out

High PS + low standards. People feel safe but nothing hard is being asked. Retros are warm and change nothing. "Everyone did a great job" becomes the default. This is the exact failure mode the "PS as being nice" misread produces at scale. Nothing ships.

**Diagnostic:** if the team retro is warm, everyone feels good, and nothing changes week over week — you are in the Comfort Zone, not the Learning Zone.

### Anxiety Zone (bottom-right) — high performance masquerade

Low PS + high standards. High-pressure, high-fear environments. Compliance without learning. People hit numbers or hit the road. Dissent is punished. Concerns are suppressed. **Canonical cases:** Boeing 737 MAX, Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal, Volkswagen Dieselgate. In every one, high standards were maintained without PS, and engineers who knew things were wrong could not raise them safely. The numbers look great until they don't.

### Apathy Zone (bottom-left) — coast-to-retirement

Low PS + low standards. Neither care nor push. Meetings happen. Nothing meaningful gets said. Nothing meaningful gets done. Often invisible from the outside because there's no scandal — just steady erosion.

## CRITICAL: Psychological safety is a team-level condition, not a personality trait

**The rule violated most often when leaders discuss PS as if it were a personal quality.**

- PS is a **team-level** belief. It varies by team, not by person.
- Same individual in a high-PS team speaks up; in a low-PS team goes silent.
- The Fearless Organization Scan (fearlessorganizationscan.com) measures PS at the team level for this reason.
- "Some people just aren't comfortable speaking up" is often a diagnosis of the team, not the person.

**Why this matters:** the moment PS is treated as personality, leaders stop diagnosing the conditions they've created and start recruiting for "assertiveness." That's a diagnosis in the wrong direction.

## The Leader Toolkit — Fearless Organization Part III

Edmondson's prescriptive core. The three leader moves that build psychological safety.

### 1. Setting the Stage — Framing the Work

Before you can ask for voice, name the nature of the work: interdependent, uncertain, novel, learning-intensive. This reframes the default assumption ("mistakes = failure = punishment") to ("this is complex work, voice is required, failures will happen and we will learn from them").

**Concrete moves:**
- Name the interdependencies. Who depends on whom for what to succeed.
- Name the uncertainty. What you don't know yet. What could go wrong.
- Name the stakes. What's at risk and for whom (patients, customers, teammates).
- Explicitly frame voice as a requirement, not a favor.

**Why this comes first:** without framing, "any thoughts?" invites nothing. With framing, the same question invites the specific voice the work requires.

### 2. Inviting Participation

Three sub-moves:

#### Situational Humility

The leader publicly admits they don't have all the answers. Edmondson's stock phrase:

> "We've never been here before."

Or: *"I don't have the answer to this — help me think through it."*

Signals the invitation is genuine. Without situational humility, "speak up" from an authority figure who acts like they have all the answers reads as trap-bait.

#### Proactive Inquiry

Ask specific questions, not generic ones.

- **Weak:** "Any thoughts?" / "Any concerns?" / "Anything else?"
- **Strong:** "What's the strongest argument against this decision?" / "What are we missing?" / "Whose customers would push back on this most?" / "If this fails, what would be the reason?"

Specificity signals expectation. Generic questions signal a box being checked.

#### Structures and Processes

Voice as a designed-in behavior, not a hopeful one:

- Round-robin turn-taking so quiet voices get called on.
- Written pre-reads and async input so the loud-voice bias is broken.
- Explicit devil's-advocate roles in decision meetings.
- Blameless post-mortem templates with prompts that force voice.
- Anonymous input channels for high-risk-of-retribution topics.
- "One-question-per-person" go-arounds before decisions.

### 3. Responding Productively

The leader's response to voice determines whether voice happens again. This is where PS is won or lost in the moment.

**Do:**
- Express appreciation. Even for bad news. Especially for bad news.
- Destigmatize failure. Frame the intelligent failure as an intelligent failure explicitly. (See Failure Taxonomy below.)
- Ask the follow-up question. "What did you learn?" / "What would you do differently?" / "What do you need from me?"
- **Sanction clear violations.** Bullies, harassers, code-of-conduct violators face consequences. This reinforces PS; failing to sanction them destroys it.

**Don't:**
- Punish the messenger. Fire the messenger once, the whole team learns to hide.
- Punish intelligent failure. The single fastest way to kill learning.
- Confuse feedback on an idea with attack on the person. Critique the idea; support the person.

## The Failure Taxonomy — Right Kind of Wrong (2023)

Edmondson's three failure archetypes from *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023). **This is core method, not a companion. Diagnose failure type before responding to any failure.**

### Basic failure

Preventable failure in known territory. Single cause. "You were supposed to take a left, you took a right." Caused by inattention, skipped steps, or bad execution of well-understood processes.

**Examples:** wrong-side surgery. Sending an email to the wrong recipient. Deploying to production without running the tests.

**Response:** checklists, standardization, training. These are not learning opportunities in the intellectual sense — they are execution failures. Fix the process; don't run a workshop.

### Complex failure

Familiar territory, many interacting causes. No single root. Weather + fatigue + a broken tool + a missed handoff.

**Examples:** hospital medication errors. Aviation incidents caused by a chain of small deviations. Financial-services outages caused by cascading dependencies.

**Response:** system redesign, defense-in-depth, near-miss reporting. Learn at the system level, not the individual level. Blame culture treats these as individual failures ("who screwed up?") and misses the systemic pattern that will produce the next one.

### Intelligent failure — "the right kind of wrong"

Failure in **novel territory**, undertaken as a small, hypothesis-driven experiment, in pursuit of a goal you couldn't achieve without trying.

**Tests (all must be true):**
- **Novel territory** — no established playbook.
- **Opportunity-driven** — pursuing a goal, not just tinkering.
- **Hypothesis-based** — you had a specific reason to expect it might work.
- **As small as possible** — scaled to learn, not to bet the company.

**Examples:** early-stage clinical trials. R&D moonshots. Edison's 10,000 filament experiments. Startup pivots based on customer discovery. A/B tests that lose.

**Author's words:**
> "An intelligent failure is the right kind of wrong."
> — *Right Kind of Wrong*, 2023

**Response:** celebrate the learning. Extract the insight. Run the next experiment. Punishing intelligent failure kills learning; celebrating it accelerates it.

### The blameworthy-to-praiseworthy spectrum (HBR, 2011 — precursor)

Edmondson introduced this spectrum in "Strategies for Learning from Failure" (HBR, April 2011). Failures range from:

- **Blameworthy:** deviance, inattention, lack of ability (basic failures caused by insufficient effort or capability).
- **Praiseworthy:** exploratory testing, hypothesis testing (intelligent failures in novel territory).

Most leaders overweight the blameworthy end and underweight the praiseworthy end — meaning they punish 80% of failures when only 2-5% actually warrant punishment. That's what breaks psychological safety.

## Team Learning Behaviors

Edmondson's operationalization of what PS enables. Teams with high PS engage in:

- **Information sharing** — surfacing what one person knows to the group.
- **Asking for help** — admitting the gap; requesting expertise.
- **Seeking feedback** — actively asking "how did that land?" / "what would you do differently?"
- **Discussing errors** — surfacing what went wrong without shame.
- **Experimenting** — trying things, running small tests, iterating.

These are the observable behaviors. If a team isn't doing these, PS is low regardless of what the team says on a survey.

## Integration — the framework moves working together

Edmondson's method isn't the definition of PS alone. It's the definition + the 2×2 with accountability + the leader toolkit + the failure taxonomy + team learning behaviors, working as a system:

- **The definition** anchors the concept — interpersonal risk-taking as the class of behaviors PS enables.
- **The 2×2 with accountability** prevents the "PS as being nice" misread and names the Learning Zone as the target.
- **The leader toolkit (Framing → Inviting Participation → Responding Productively)** tells the leader what to actually do.
- **The failure taxonomy (Basic / Complex / Intelligent)** tells the leader how to respond to failures without killing learning.
- **Team learning behaviors** are the observable outputs — the evidence that the system is working.

The moves reinforce each other. A leader who frames the work well makes voice more likely. A leader who invites with situational humility makes the frame credible. A leader who responds productively to voice makes the next voice more likely. A leader who diagnoses failure type before responding avoids the blame culture that kills PS. A team engaged in team learning behaviors is producing the observable signal that the system is working.

## What this method is NOT

Edmondson is emphatic about the negative definitions:

- **NOT being nice.** PS enables candid feedback; it does not replace it.
- **NOT low standards.** PS pairs with high accountability. Both must be high.
- **NOT a shield for misconduct.** Sanctioning bullies and harassers reinforces PS.
- **NOT a personality.** PS is a team-level condition, not a personal quality.
- **NOT the annual engagement survey.** PS lives in team behaviors, measured at team level.
- **NOT permission to whine.** In Edmondson's words: "it's not a license to whine, or an invitation to sit back and just start saying everything that doesn't work."
- **NOT the same as radical transparency.** PS is about voice without retribution, not mandatory disclosure of everything to everyone.
- **NOT a US-only cultural norm.** The axes (PS, accountability) are universal; the calibration of what "voice" looks like varies by culture. Adjust calibration; don't drop axes.
