# The Fearless Organization — Worked Cases Edmondson Uses Publicly

> The recurring cases Amy Edmondson returns to across her book, HBR articles, podcasts, and talks. She uses them like a case-method professor uses cases — because she is one. Reach for these when helping a user recognize their own situation on the 2×2.

## The canonical origin

### 1. The hospital medication error study (1996 PhD work) — the origin of psychological safety

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization* (2018); *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023); [Behavioral Scientist essay](https://behavioralscientist.org/the-intelligent-failure-that-led-to-the-discovery-of-psychological-safety/); most keynotes.

**The story:**
- Edmondson, PhD student. Designed a study expecting that better hospital teams would have fewer medication errors.
- Data showed the OPPOSITE — better teams reported MORE errors.
- She spent months confused. The hypothesis was wrong; the data was clear.
- Realization: better teams weren't making more errors — they were more willing to REPORT them.
- The variable she had actually stumbled onto was psychological safety.

**The double lesson:** the story explains where the concept came from AND demonstrates the intelligent-failure taxonomy applied to Edmondson's own work. She got the hypothesis wrong; the wrong hypothesis in novel territory produced a discovery. Textbook intelligent failure.

**When to reach for this case:**
- Helping a user understand where PS comes from as a research concept.
- Illustrating what an intelligent failure looks like.
- Modeling how a leader can own their own wrong hypothesis publicly.

## The Learning Zone exemplars

### 2. Ford under Alan Mulally — the color-coded status meeting

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization*; multiple keynotes.

**The story:**
- Alan Mulally becomes CEO of Ford in 2006. Ford is losing $17 billion a year.
- Mulally introduces weekly "Business Plan Review" (BPR) meetings. Every executive color-codes their metrics red / yellow / green.
- First few weeks: everyone reports green. Everyone.
- Mulally's line, roughly: *"We're going to lose $17 billion this year and nothing is red?"*
- One executive (Mark Fields, then head of the Americas) finally reports red on the Ford Edge launch.
- Mulally applauds. Publicly. In the meeting.
- The room cracks open. Reds start appearing across the company. Ford begins its turnaround.

**The framework moves:** Framing (weekly BPR structure making the interdependencies visible) + Situational Humility (Mulally admitting Ford's condition without blame) + Proactive Inquiry (the color-coding as forced specific voice) + Responding Productively (applauding the first "red" report).

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user think about how to introduce PS practices to a low-PS org. Mulally's move — publicly applauding the first bad-news report — is the canonical Responding Productively moment.

### 3. Julie Morath at Children's Hospitals and Clinics of Minnesota

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization*, most detailed treatment.

**The story:**
- Julie Morath becomes COO of Children's Hospitals and Clinics of Minnesota. Patient safety was low; error reporting was suppressed by blame culture.
- Morath deliberately reframes error reporting from blame to learning. Explicit language shifts: "medical accidents" instead of "errors"; "focus study" instead of "investigation."
- Puts structures in place: anonymous reporting channels, blameless review meetings, patient safety officer role.
- Personally models the reframe — takes bad news gratefully, publicly.
- Over years, error reporting goes UP (the intelligent-failure signature — reporting rates rise while actual harm falls).

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user design a structural intervention for a low-PS healthcare-like environment. Morath's language shifts are worth borrowing verbatim.

### 4. Pixar Braintrust — brutal feedback in a norm-protected space

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization*; Edmondson references Ed Catmull's *Creativity, Inc.*

**The story:**
- Pixar filmmakers present rough cuts to the Braintrust — a group of Pixar's most experienced directors and story leads.
- Feedback is brutal. Explicit critique of what's not working in the film. No sugar-coating.
- The critical feature: **the Braintrust has no authority over the director's decisions.** They give feedback; the director decides. Feedback is separated from authority.
- Result: filmmakers get honest input without the power dynamic that suppresses honest input elsewhere.

**Why it maps to Learning Zone:** high PS (feedback without retribution) + high standards (Pixar's creative bar) held together explicitly. NOT nice; NOT low-standards; deeply psychologically safe.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user design a feedback ritual where honest input is needed but authority dynamics would otherwise suppress it. The separation of feedback from decision authority is the key move.

### 5. Google Aristotle project — validation at scale

**Source:** widely cited; Edmondson references it as validation of her earlier research.

**The story:**
- Google Aristotle project studied 180+ Google teams to identify what made high-performing teams different.
- Ran through many hypotheses: skill mix, tenure, team size, gender balance, personality types.
- The strongest single predictor of team performance: psychological safety.
- Google published the finding in 2015-2016; it became the widely-cited data-driven case for Edmondson's earlier work.

**Attribution note:** the Aristotle project VALIDATED Edmondson's earlier research (Edmondson published her core PS paper in 1999). It did not originate the concept. Credit Edmondson (or her 1999 ASQ paper) as the source, not Google.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user justify investment in PS to a data-driven audience. But always with the attribution note — the research is Edmondson's; Aristotle is the scaled validation.

### 6. IDEO — design-thinking teams

**Source:** *Teaming* (2012); *The Fearless Organization*.

**The story:** IDEO's design-thinking process depends on rapid iteration, ideation without judgment in early stages, and constructive critique in later stages. The team norms explicitly enable half-baked ideas to surface — because half-baked ideas are the raw material for design breakthroughs.

**Why it maps to Learning Zone:** IDEO teams have high standards (design excellence) and high PS (permission to bring half-formed ideas). Both.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user design ideation processes that don't shut down early-stage ideas. IDEO's explicit norms are borrowable.

## The Anxiety Zone catastrophes

### 7. Columbia Space Shuttle disaster (2003) — the canonical PS-failure case

**Source:** Edmondson's HBS case study; [HBR IdeaCast — "Leadership Lessons from a NASA Tragedy" (August 2024)](https://hbr.org/podcast/2024/08/leadership-lessons-from-a-nasa-tragedy) is her most current treatment.

**The story:**
- Foam broke off Columbia's external tank during launch and struck the wing.
- Structural engineer Rodney Rocha had concerns: imaging wasn't clear enough to rule out catastrophic failure on re-entry.
- Boeing produced an official report claiming no danger.
- In the final safety meeting: no probing questions asked of the Boeing report. The shuttle was cleared.
- On re-entry: the wing failed. All seven crew members died.
- Post-investigation: Rocha's concerns HAD been raised — through channels that never reached the decision meeting effectively. The low-PS culture of NASA under time and budget pressure suppressed voice.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user understand what suppressed voice actually costs. The single most-cited case Edmondson uses. The August 2024 HBR IdeaCast episode is her deep modern treatment.

### 8. Boeing 737 MAX — Anxiety Zone modern

**Source:** *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023); multiple recent talks and podcast appearances.

**The story:**
- Boeing under pressure to compete with Airbus A320neo. Rushed the 737 MAX to market.
- MCAS software system added to compensate for larger engines that changed the aircraft's flight characteristics.
- Engineers and pilots raised concerns internally about the system's design and pilot training implications.
- Boeing culture at the time: retribution against whistleblowers, pressure to meet delivery schedules, PS was low.
- Two crashes (Lion Air 610 in 2018, Ethiopian Airlines 302 in 2019). 346 deaths.
- Subsequent investigations documented the suppressed engineering concerns.

**Why it maps to Anxiety Zone:** high standards (delivery schedules, competitive pressure) + low PS (engineering concerns suppressed) = catastrophic execution failure.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize an Anxiety Zone culture in an org that appears high-performing from the outside.

### 9. Wells Fargo cross-selling scandal — Anxiety Zone in finance

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization*; recent talks.

**The story:**
- Wells Fargo executives set aggressive cross-selling quotas (eight products per household).
- Front-line workers raised concerns that the targets were impossible without cheating.
- Concerns were dismissed; targets were maintained; retaliation against complainers was documented.
- Workers responded by creating millions of fake accounts to hit quotas.
- Scandal broke in 2016. Fines, resignations, cultural damage.

**Why it maps to Anxiety Zone:** high standards (quotas) + low PS (unable to raise impossibility) = systematic fraud as compliance behavior.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user understand how high-pressure targets without PS produce fraud rather than performance.

### 10. Volkswagen Dieselgate — Anxiety Zone in engineering

**Source:** *The Fearless Organization*; multiple talks.

**The story:**
- VW engineers were tasked with producing diesel engines that could meet US emissions standards.
- Technical solution didn't exist within the constraints (cost, timeline, engine design).
- Engineers implemented "defeat device" software that produced clean-emissions performance ONLY when the car detected emissions testing conditions.
- Engineers knew. Managers knew. No safe way to raise the impossibility of the honest task.
- Scandal broke in 2015. $30+ billion in fines and settlements. Cultural damage that continues.

**Why it maps to Anxiety Zone:** high standards (regulatory compliance + delivery schedules) + low PS (unable to raise "this can't be done honestly") = systematic engineering fraud.

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize the same Anxiety Zone pattern in an engineering context where "we can make this work" becomes the only permitted answer.

## The Right Kind of Wrong (2023) failure taxonomy cases

### 11. Thomas Edison's 10,000 filament experiments — canonical intelligent failure at scale

**Source:** *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023).

**The story:**
- Edison and his team tested thousands of materials for the incandescent light bulb filament.
- Most failed. Some failed spectacularly.
- Each failure narrowed the search space. Each failure was documented, learned from, extended.
- Eventually: the carbonized bamboo filament. Then improvements from there.
- Edison's line (paraphrased in various tellings): *"I have not failed. I've just found 10,000 ways that won't work."*

**Why it maps to intelligent failure:** novel territory (nobody had built a commercial light bulb), hypothesis-driven (each material choice was a specific bet), opportunity-motivated (huge market for practical electric light), as small as possible (each test isolated one variable).

**When to reach for this case:** helping a user recognize what intelligent failure looks like at scale — and what it requires (systematic learning capture, patience, tolerance for many "misses" in service of the eventual hit).

### 12. Basic failure examples

**Source:** *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023).

- Wrong-site surgery — surgeon operates on the wrong body part. Prevented by pre-operative checklists (Atul Gawande's overlap with Edmondson).
- Pharmacy mis-dispensing — pharmacist gives patient wrong medication. Prevented by two-person verification.
- Deploy without running tests — engineer pushes to production without CI green light. Prevented by pipeline gates.

**Response:** checklists, standardization, training. Not learning workshops. Fix the process.

### 13. Complex failure examples

**Source:** *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023).

- Fukushima nuclear disaster — earthquake + tsunami + backup power failure + hydrogen buildup + cooling failure. No single cause; a cascade.
- 2008 financial crisis — mortgage-backed securities + rating agency incentives + regulatory gaps + leverage + herd behavior. No single cause.
- Hospital patient dies during care — medication interaction + fatigue + missed handoff + monitor alarm ignored. Multiple contributing factors.

**Response:** system redesign, defense-in-depth, near-miss reporting. Not "who screwed up?" — the answer is "the system did."

### 14. Intelligent failure examples

**Source:** *Right Kind of Wrong* (2023).

- Early-stage clinical trials — testing a new drug's safety and efficacy. Some fail. Failure produces knowledge that shapes the next trial.
- R&D moonshots — Google X's various shuttered projects. Loon (balloon internet) failed as a business but produced learning about high-altitude balloon operations.
- Startup pivots — customer discovery reveals the product-market fit isn't where the founder thought. The failed initial hypothesis produces the pivot to what works.
- A/B tests — most tested variants lose. Losing variants produce learning about customer preferences.

**Response:** celebrate the learning. Extract the insight. Run the next experiment.

## Composite cases the assistant can construct

When a user brings a scenario that maps to Edmondson's canonical cases, use hers by analogy. When the scenario is different enough that Edmondson's canonical cases don't fit, construct a case in her register:

- **Definition-first** — name the PS condition (interpersonal risk-taking) and immediately negate the misread (not being nice).
- **2×2-based** — locate the team in one of the four zones.
- **Specific behaviors** — cite the team learning behaviors that are or aren't happening.
- **Named leader moves** — Framing / Situational Humility / Proactive Inquiry / Structures / Responding Productively.
- **Failure typing** — if failure is involved, name Basic / Complex / Intelligent explicitly.

**Do not invent quotes from Edmondson.** If you're using a constructed case, don't attribute it to her. Say *"in Edmondson's framework, this would be…"* rather than putting words in her mouth.

## When NOT to reach for these cases

- **When the user's situation is legally sensitive** (harassment, discrimination, safety-of-life). Don't collapse it into a Fearless Organization case study — direct the user to HR / legal counsel first. The framework informs; it doesn't replace them.
- **When the user is fishing for permission to lower standards** — don't reach for the Pixar case as license for "brutal feedback with no accountability structure." Pixar's Braintrust works BECAUSE of the specific separation-of-feedback-from-authority structure. Reach for the 2×2 with accountability instead.
- **When the user is describing personal crisis** — the framework isn't the primary tool. Basic humanity + EAP resources + appropriate professional support come first.
- **When the failure at hand is clear misconduct** — Volkswagen and Wells Fargo cases are useful for the pattern recognition, but the response to actual misconduct is sanction (not "let's understand this as an intelligent failure"). PS does not shield misconduct.
