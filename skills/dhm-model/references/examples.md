# DHM Model — Examples

> Netflix cases in depth + Chegg case study + guidance for applying DHM to non-Netflix companies. All cases attributed to specific Biddle essays.

## Netflix — The master class

Gibson Biddle was VP of Product at Netflix (2005-2010). Every example below is first-person.

### 1. Personalization — the canonical DHM trifecta

**Source:** Essay #1 "The DHM Model," Essay #11 "Netflix 2020," "A Brief History of Netflix Personalization" (Medium/Marker), "Ask Gib" Substack Part Two.

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Better recommendations = dramatically easier for members to find movies they love. Reduced choice overload. In a catalog of thousands, personalization made the interface feel like "this service knows me." |
| **Hard to copy** | **Unique technology** — Netflix knows the tastes of 165M+ members worldwide. The algorithms are proprietary, built on years of data. **Economies of scale** — the more members, the more taste data, the better the recommendations. A competitor starting fresh can't replicate a decade of taste data. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Merchandises cheaper content — the algorithm surfaces catalog titles (already paid for) rather than only expensive new releases. "Right-sizes" content investment based on forecasts of what members will actually watch. Improves retention = higher LTV. |

**Key insight:** It took Netflix more than a decade to demonstrate that personalization improved retention. The team invested through years of ambiguous results because the hypothesis was strong. Proxy metrics (ratings volume, queue depth) showed progress even when retention was slow to move.

**Result:** Netflix improved its monthly cancel rate from ~10% to 2% over two decades. Personalization was one of the key drivers.

### 2. Original content

**Source:** Essay #11 "Netflix 2020," Essay #8 "The GLEe Model."

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Exclusive shows (House of Cards, Orange Is the New Black, Stranger Things) that you can't get elsewhere. Creates appointment viewing and cultural conversation. |
| **Hard to copy** | **Economies of scale** — with 150M+ members, Netflix can amortize content cost across a larger base than any competitor. A service with 10M subscribers can't afford the same content investment per member. **Brand** — Netflix original content became synonymous with quality entertainment. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Owned content has better long-term margins than licensed content (no renewal cost escalation from studios; no risk of losing popular titles to competing services). Shifts the cost structure from variable licensing to owned assets that appreciate. |

**Key insight:** Original content required economies of scale first. Netflix didn't invest heavily in originals until the subscriber base was large enough to make the unit economics work. This is the GLEe phasing in action — you need to Get Big before you can Lead with original content.

### 3. Simple / Easy UX

**Source:** Essay #2 "From DHM to Product Strategy," Lenny's Podcast episode (2022).

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Reduced friction in finding and watching content. Netflix's interface became synonymous with "just works." Fewer clicks to start watching. |
| **Hard to copy** | **Brand** — users associate Netflix with simplicity. This brand perception takes years to build and is hard to replicate even with similar design. **Unique technology** — the simplicity is backed by sophisticated A/B testing infrastructure and deep understanding of user behavior patterns. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Reduced support costs (fewer confused users contacting help). Higher conversion from trial to paid. Better retention (less friction = fewer reasons to cancel). |

**Proxy metric:** "Percentage of new members who add at least three titles to their queue during their very first session." The team focused on making the service simpler to increase this percentage. Moving this proxy correlated with improved retention.

### 4. Device ecosystem (network effects)

**Source:** Essay #2 "From DHM to Product Strategy," Essay #11 "Netflix 2020."

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Watch anywhere — TV, phone, tablet, game console, laptop. Seamless cross-device experience. |
| **Hard to copy** | **Network effects** — starting in 2008 with Xbox, Netflix built partnerships so every TV, Blu-Ray player, game system, set-top box, and mobile device came pre-wired for Netflix. Each new device partner makes Netflix more ubiquitous. A new streaming service can't replicate this distribution overnight. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Distribution cost approaches zero as device manufacturers pre-install Netflix. Each new device is a free acquisition channel. Compare to the cost of marketing to acquire the same member through paid channels. |

**Key insight:** The device ecosystem is a network-effect advantage that started slowly (one device partner at a time in 2008) and became a massive moat by 2015+. This is a "hard to copy" advantage that compounds over time.

### 5. Social features — the strategy that didn't work

**Source:** Essay #2 "From DHM to Product Strategy," Lenny's Podcast episode (2022).

In 2005, one of Netflix's six strategies was Social — connecting members with friends' movie tastes to improve discovery. The hypothesis: if you see what your friends watch and rate, you'll find better movies and be more delighted.

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Hypothesis was that social features would improve discovery. In practice, social features did NOT significantly improve engagement or retention. |
| **Hard to copy** | Would have been hard to copy if it worked (unique social graph + taste data). But it didn't work. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | No impact — the strategy didn't move the engagement metric. |

**Key insight:** This is the "strategy = hypotheses" principle in action. Social was a legitimate hypothesis. It was tested. It failed. The team killed it and reallocated resources. That's not a failure of strategy — it's strategy working as intended.

### 6. The Netflix monthly cancel rate trajectory

**Source:** Essay #1 "The DHM Model," Essay Intro.

Over two decades, Netflix improved its monthly cancel rate from ~10% to ~2%. This is the aggregate result of multiple DHM strategies working together:
- Personalization improved discovery -> better engagement -> lower cancel rate
- Original content created exclusive value -> lower cancel rate
- Simple UX reduced friction -> lower cancel rate
- Device ecosystem increased accessibility -> lower cancel rate
- Each strategy was margin-enhancing, creating a flywheel: better retention -> more subscribers -> more economies of scale -> more content investment -> better retention

## Chegg — The startup case study

**Source:** Essay #12 "A Startup Case Study: Chegg" — [gibsonbiddle.medium.com](https://gibsonbiddle.medium.com/11-case-study-chegg-8044679939f9)

Gibson Biddle joined Chegg as CPO in 2010.

### Background
- Founded 2001 as "Craigslist for college students" — no traction.
- Pivoted to textbook rental (2008) — massive product-market fit.
- By the end of Biddle's first year: $150M in revenue.
- Went public 2014-2015.

### Phase 1: Textbook rental (Get Big)

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Overwhelming value — rent a textbook for $50 instead of buying new for $200. 75% savings. |
| **Hard to copy** | **Brand** with college students + logistics infrastructure for shipping/receiving textbooks. But weak unique technology initially. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Good margins due to textbook reuse across semesters (same physical book rented multiple times). |

### Phase 2: Digital homework help (Lead)

As the textbook rental business grew, Chegg set its sights on leading digital homework help:
- Step-by-step solutions to all textbook answers
- Expanded into writing help, tutoring, flashcards
- Created monthly subscription "Chegg Study"

| Dimension | Analysis |
|---|---|
| **Delight** | Students could get homework answers and step-by-step explanations instantly. |
| **Hard to copy** | **Economies of scale** — building the solution database required massive upfront investment. **Unique technology** — proprietary answer database + matching algorithms. |
| **Margin-enhancing** | Digital products have near-zero marginal cost (no physical textbooks to ship). Subscription model = recurring revenue. |

### GLEe for Chegg
Get big on textbook rental -> Lead digital homework help -> Expand into broader student services.

### Cautionary note (not from Biddle directly)
Chegg's market cap dropped from $1.2B to ~$150M in 2024 after ChatGPT disrupted the homework-help value proposition. While Biddle does not use this example (he left Chegg years before), it illustrates a critical DHM lesson: "hard to copy" advantages can be destroyed by platform shifts. Chegg's solution database was hard to copy by another homework-help company, but was commoditized by a general-purpose AI.

## Applying DHM to non-Netflix companies

Biddle's examples are Netflix-heavy because that's his first-person experience. When applying DHM to other companies, use the following guidance:

### Consumer subscription (Netflix-adjacent)
DHM translates most directly. The three dimensions map cleanly:
- D: Does the user love the product? (engagement, retention)
- H: What moat do we have? (brand, network effects, unique data, scale)
- M: Does the product improve unit economics? (LTV, churn, content/cost efficiency)

### B2B SaaS
The "Delight" dimension shifts from emotional delight to "solves a critical workflow problem with less friction than alternatives."
- D: Do users rely on it daily? Would they fight to keep it?
- H: Switching costs become important (integration depth, data lock-in). Unique technology matters. Brand matters less than in consumer.
- M: Does the product create pricing power? Does it reduce churn? Does it improve net revenue retention?

### Marketplace / platform
Network effects become the dominant "H" factor.
- D: Does the platform make it easier for both sides to transact?
- H: Network effects — does more supply attract more demand, and vice versa? Does the platform have unique data from both sides?
- M: Does the platform improve take rate or reduce cost-to-serve as it scales?

### Hardware / physical product
"Hard to copy" often comes from patents, manufacturing scale, and brand.
- D: Does the product create a moment of delight (Apple's unboxing, Dyson's engineering)?
- H: Patents, design IP, manufacturing scale, brand.
- M: Does the product enable recurring revenue (consumables, services, ecosystem)?

### Startups with limited data
At early stage, the DHM filter still applies but the evidence bar is lower:
- D: Qualitative signals (customer interviews, usage patterns in a small cohort)
- H: The honest answer may be "nothing yet, but we're building toward X"
- M: Unit economics modeled, not proven. The question is whether the path to margin-enhancement is credible.

The key adaptation: at startup stage, DHM is a planning filter ("we intend to build hard-to-copy advantages in X") rather than a measurement filter ("we've proven this is hard to copy").
