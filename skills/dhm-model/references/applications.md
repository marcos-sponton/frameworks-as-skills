# DHM Model — Applications

> When DHM is the right tool, when it's not, and how it relates to adjacent frameworks.

## When to use DHM

### Product strategy definition
The primary use case. You have ideas about what to build and need a filter for which ideas are strategic vs. which are just nice features. DHM forces the three-dimensional evaluation: delight + hard to copy + margin-enhancing.

### Roadmap prioritization
After filtering through DHM, use GEM (Growth, Engagement, Monetization) to prioritize surviving initiatives. DHM says "is this strategic?"; GEM says "which strategic initiative first?"

### Competitive advantage analysis (product level)
When the user asks "what makes us hard to copy?" or "what's our competitive advantage at the product level?" — the four types of hard-to-copy advantage (brand, network effects, economies of scale, unique technology) provide a structured diagnostic.

### Strategy articulation
When a team has a product but can't articulate their product strategy. The Strategy/Metric/Tactic lock-up gives them a format: name the strategy, the metric, and the projects. If they can't fill in the table, they don't have a strategy.

### Proxy metric definition
When a team is measuring the wrong things or can't tell if their strategy is working. The proxy metric framework provides a hierarchy: high-level engagement metric -> proxy metrics -> project-level metrics.

### Quarterly strategy review
When a team needs a cadence for reviewing product strategy. Biddle's quarterly meeting format provides structure: review each swimlane, share learnings, debate whether strategies should continue or pivot.

### Product initiative evaluation
For any individual initiative: "Should we build X?" Score it on D, H, M. If it scores well on all three, proceed. If it fails on any dimension, either redesign or kill.

## When NOT to use DHM

### Pre-problem-discovery
If the user doesn't yet know what problem to solve or for whom, DHM is premature. The framework assumes you have ideas about what to build and need to evaluate them. For problem discovery, use JTBD, Continuous Discovery Habits, or Lean Startup.

### Growth distribution mechanics
If the user's question is about channels, loops, viral mechanics, paid vs. organic, or why growth has stalled — this is growth distribution, not product strategy. Use Balfour's Four Fits instead. DHM helps you decide WHAT to build; Four Fits helps you decide how to distribute and monetize it.

### Corporate strategy
If the user's question is about which markets to enter, M&A decisions, portfolio strategy, or competitive positioning at the corporate (not product) level — DHM operates at the product level. Use Playing to Win (Lafley/Martin) or Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Rumelt).

### Organizational design
If the user's question is about team structure, hiring, culture, or processes — Biddle has opinions (swimlanes, pods) but DHM is a strategy filter, not an org-design framework. Use Empowered (Cagan) or Team Topologies.

### Pricing
If the user's primary question is about pricing — how to set prices, pricing tiers, willingness-to-pay — DHM's "M" dimension is about margin, not pricing specifically. Use Madhavan Ramanujam's work or Simon-Kucher methods.

### Deep competitive moat analysis
If the user needs a thorough analysis of competitive dynamics and barriers to entry, Biddle's four types of "hard to copy" are a simplified version of Hamilton Helmer's 7 Powers. For deep moat analysis, use 7 Powers instead.

## Adjacent frameworks and when to prefer them

### Hamilton Helmer — 7 Powers
**Relationship:** Helmer's 7 Powers (Scale Economies, Network Economies, Counter-Positioning, Switching Costs, Branding, Cornered Resource, Process Power) is the deep, rigorous analysis of competitive advantage. Biddle's four hard-to-copy types (brand, network effects, economies of scale, unique technology) are a simplified version.
**When to prefer 7 Powers:** When you need a thorough competitive analysis — understanding not just "are we hard to copy?" but precisely WHICH power applies and how strong it is. 7 Powers is the analytical tool; DHM is the practitioner filter.
**When to prefer DHM:** When you need a quick, actionable evaluation of a product initiative. DHM is faster and more accessible. Teams can score initiatives on DHM in a meeting; 7 Powers requires deeper analysis.

### Brian Balfour — Four Fits
**Relationship:** Balfour's Four Fits is a growth-system diagnostic (Product-Market, Product-Channel, Channel-Model, Model-Market). DHM is a product-strategy filter. They operate at different levels.
**When to prefer Four Fits:** When growth has stalled, when the user is choosing channels, when the user's ARPU and CAC don't work together. Four Fits diagnoses the go-to-market chain.
**When to prefer DHM:** When deciding what to build. DHM filters initiatives on strategic value; Four Fits ensures the go-to-market chain holds once you've built it.
**Using together:** A team could use DHM to filter which initiatives are strategic and then use Four Fits to ensure the surviving initiatives can be distributed and monetized.

### Marty Cagan — Inspired / Empowered
**Relationship:** Cagan focuses on how product teams are organized and empowered (dual-track, cross-functional squads, product discovery). Biddle's frameworks sit one level above: they define WHAT the empowered team should work on (strategy) and HOW to measure it (metrics).
**When to prefer Cagan:** When the user's problem is team structure, product discovery process, or how to empower product teams. Cagan's work is about the operating model for product development.
**When to prefer DHM:** When the user knows HOW to do product work but needs to define WHAT to work on and how to evaluate it strategically.
**Using together:** Use Cagan's empowered teams to DO the work; use Biddle's DHM to define the strategic questions the teams are answering.

### JTBD / Jobs-to-be-Done (Christensen, Moesta, Kalbach)
**Relationship:** JTBD discovers what customers are trying to accomplish ("the job"). DHM evaluates whether a product strategy that addresses the job ALSO builds competitive advantage and margin. JTBD informs the "D" (what would delight?); DHM adds the "H" and "M" filter.
**When to prefer JTBD:** When the user doesn't yet know what problem to solve or needs to understand customer motivations deeply.
**When to prefer DHM:** When the user knows what delights customers but needs to evaluate whether that delight is strategic.
**Using together:** Discover the job (JTBD), then evaluate product strategies that address the job using DHM. Does the solution delight, is it hard to copy, is it margin-enhancing?

### Playing to Win (Lafley/Martin)
**Relationship:** Lafley/Martin's cascade (Winning Aspiration -> Where to Play -> How to Win -> Capabilities -> Management Systems) is a broader corporate strategy framework. DHM operates at the product level, providing the "How to Win" filter for product-specific decisions.
**When to prefer P2W:** When the user's question is about corporate-level choices: which markets, which segments, which capabilities to invest in.
**When to prefer DHM:** When the user has already decided where to play and needs to evaluate product initiatives within that space.

### Good Strategy Bad Strategy (Rumelt)
**Relationship:** Rumelt provides the meta-framework for what makes any strategy good (diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent actions) vs. bad (word salad, refusing to choose, mistaking goals for strategy). DHM is a specific instance of good strategy — it diagnoses (the three-dimension evaluation), provides a guiding policy (invest in initiatives that score well on all three), and drives coherent actions (the lock-up).
**When to prefer Rumelt:** When the user's strategy is word salad or a list of goals. Rumelt helps them understand what strategy IS before filling in Biddle's tables.
**When to prefer DHM:** When the user understands strategy conceptually but needs a specific product-level filter.

### North Star / AARRR (Pirate Metrics)
**Relationship:** Biddle's proxy metrics are related but more nuanced than a single North Star metric. Biddle argues for a hierarchy: high-level engagement metric -> proxy metrics per strategy -> project-level metrics. This is richer than "pick one North Star."
**When to prefer North Star/AARRR:** When the user needs a simpler, team-wide alignment metric. Good for early-stage companies that need focus.
**When to prefer DHM/Proxy Metrics:** When the user has multiple strategies and needs different metrics for each. Biddle's approach scales better with organizational complexity.

### OKRs
**Relationship:** DHM strategies and proxy metrics can be translated into OKRs (Strategy -> Objective; Proxy metric -> Key Result; Tactics -> Key Result initiatives). Biddle doesn't frame it this way explicitly, but the mapping is clean.
**Note:** If the team already uses OKRs, show them how the Strategy/Metric/Tactic lock-up maps directly. It's a compatibility bridge, not a competing framework.
